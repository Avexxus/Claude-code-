Jon put his palm flat on the table's edge and left it there. He read the top of the table end to end, where the grain ran under the varnish in long pale stripes. Then the three cups. The one with the cracked lip sat nearest Derrek's hand with a thumb's depth of tea in it, still as a lid on a pot.

The potman in the doorway had the tray against his chest. Jon had come up at noon saying ya to the barman, and to the dog at the foot of the stairs, and to the potman himself when he took the tray. The noise from the taproom had gone thin. Somebody down there had stopped talking in the middle of a word.

"Ya," Jon said. "Hold still a sec."

Derrek was tipped back on the far chair with one ankle across his knee, picking at a loose thread at his cuff. He saw the hand on the table and laughed, a bark that got out before he could stop it. He flung one arm wide. The laugh kept on going after the arm had stopped.

The cup with the cracked lip came up off the table.

It came up without a jolt. The tea lay smooth in it. It rose level until it hung at the height of Derrek's nose, and then it turned a quarter turn on nothing. Jon's palm stayed on the edge. Two fingers of his other hand lifted off his thigh, half an inch, and went down.

Derrek quit laughing. He looked at the cup. He looked at Jon's hand on the table, which had stayed where it was.

"Put it back."

Jon let the two fingers fall. The cup came down onto its ring, and the tea went over the cracked lip anyway, one thin brown line down the side. Jon took his hand off the table.

Derrek got up. Both arms went out from his sides, palms up, and his hands began to turn over and over at the wrists, fast and loose, like a man shaking out a sheet. "Feel that? Jon. You feel that?" He swung round to the chairs. "Watch the chairs. Watch the chairs, I'm doing the chairs."

Jon watched the chairs.

At the foot of the stairs the street door swung off its catch and slammed back against the wall. The potman heard the taproom door ease shut by itself. The chairs along the wall went back across the boards together, one, two, three, and the third went a finger's width too far and hit the panelling with a crack.

The field came into the room from every side. The potman felt it behind his ears and in the roots of his teeth, a push that eased off and came again. The tray chattered against his chest, and he held it flatter. The lamp swung out on its chain, far out, came back, and swung out again. The window glass took up a low note and held it.

A sheen came up on Derrek's forehead and under his jaw. The pulse in his throat jumped, and jumped, and he was grinning through it. "Good," he said. "You're watching. Good."

Jon looked at the chairs, and the lamp, and kept his eyes off the door.

Against the wall by the window stood the shutter bar, iron, a forearm long, the one the potman had propped there at noon. Jon looked along it once and stopped at a pitted place near the middle. Then he looked at the boards around it, and the nail heads, and the back leg of the chair beside the window, where the wood had gone pale at the joint and a hairline ran up from it. He crossed to the bar. He took his time. His boot came down on the boards and they gave one dead flat knock and no more. Out in the yard a dog barked once and went quiet.

The dice lay on the table where he'd left them. The candlestick stood on the sill. The tea in the cup had gone still. The lamp swung and the chain creaked. Jon shut his right hand on the bar near the middle and turned his wrist, and the iron began to bend.

It came round in a curve, then a tighter one. The middle of it closed into a sharp crook. The two arms of the bar stuck out from the crook on either side of his fist, and his fist sat in the bend. His knuckles split along the first two joints, one and then the next, with small wet clicks. The skin opened white, then red. The potman had seen a smith at the yard bend a bar like that, with a fire going and two men on the bellows, and it had taken all afternoon. Jon flexed the hand. The crook held.

Derrek leaned into the room with both arms. The chairs slid another stretch toward the wall. The lamp went out on its chain and came back late. The bar kept its bend. The sleeve over Jon's forearm pulled tight across the bone.

Derrek laughed, head back, arms going wide. "Jon. Look at your fist. Look at it sitting in there."

His hands came down in front of him, then flew out to the window side, fingers spread, and he pointed with his whole arm. "Watch this one. Watch the chair." He turned his head to be sure Jon was looking. Then two fingers came out of the spread, at the chair nearest the window, at its back leg, a hand above the boards.

The leg split.

There was no crack and no burst. The wood came apart down its length in one clean line, and the two halves stood up touching for a moment before they opened. The seat dropped. The cushion slid off onto the boards.

"Look how clean that is," Derrek said. He said it to the chair. He bent and picked up one half of the leg and held it up beside his face, grinning, the way a boy holds up a stick he has been warned off.

His other arm kept going. It swung through its arc past the window and into the wall, and while it was still going the door jamb beside the potman's shoulder split from top to bottom.

Jon had moved. He had gone left, on his toes, toward the corner. The splinter off the leg came the other way, long and pale, about the height of his face. His left hand came up, palm out, fingers loose. The splinter stopped in the air a hand from his cheek and hung there, its point still aimed at him. His back heel skidded an inch on the boards. He turned his head away from it, slowly, and lowered the hand, and the splinter came down onto the boards with a small dry tick.

The jamb was still going. A sliver came off it at head height, the length of a finger, and went out across the doorway at the potman's face.

The potman stood where he was. He saw the point of the sliver and the grain running out along it.

Jon's left hand came up again. He took his time over it. Two fingers lifted, out and flat, palm to the doorway, and the sliver stopped a foot from the potman's eye and hung there. Jon looked only at Derrek.

A flake of the jamb, the size of a nail paring, got past the edge of his hand. It opened the skin under the potman's eye, a short hot line. The tray dipped, and a heel of bread slid off and went down the stairs.

Jon's hand came down. The sliver came down with it. He lowered it to the boards in front of the potman's boot and let it go.

"You're all right," Jon said. "Stand where you are."

Derrek whooped. His arms went up over his head and came down, and his hands shook, the fingers going in and out. "Didn't mean the door, Jon. Door was just there." He was turning again, arms out. "You saw me aim it. Right at his face. That's aim."

Jon said nothing.

Jon's breath had gone long and even. The potman could see his chest go slow under the shirt.

"Breathe for me," Derrek said. His voice went right down, low and close. "Go on. Let me hear it."

The room held still. Jon's shoulders stayed where they were. The hand on the bar began to shake, a fine tremor at the fingers, then the wrist, then the whole forearm, and the end of the iron ticked against the boards.

"Your heart's going," Derrek said. "I can see it from here. Look at it go. You watching?" The field pushed in on the room, came off, and pushed harder. The chairs jumped toward the wall. The window note climbed and held.

Jon set his feet. His eyes stayed on Derrek's face. The shake went out of the hand a joint at a time, the fingers first, then the wrist, until the bar lay still in his fist and the blood ran slower down the back of it.

Derrek laughed, small at first, then up into something big, arms wide. "Oh, look at that. You're holding it." He turned on the spot, arms out, to see the hand. Jon was looking at him.

Jon opened his right hand. The bar dropped and rang once on the boards. He turned his left palm up, and the two dice came up off the table and hung in the air, one to each side of him, a little above the height of his shoulders. He let the hand fall to his side. The dice stayed where they were. The candlestick stayed on the sill. Jon looked at Derrek, and kept quiet.

Derrek let the field go slack.

The chairs slid back toward the wall and stopped. The sheen on his face stayed. He wiped it on his sleeve and looked at the dice, one and then the other, and then at Jon's hands. Then he turned his shoulder on the room and sat on the sill beside the candlestick, heels against the wall, palms flat on the wood.

"Well," he said at last.

The two men stayed where they were. The potman watched the nearer die turn, a quarter and a quarter, and lost count somewhere past nine.

Derrek's eyes went down to the boards between them, a line of planks a yard from Jon's left boot.

"Last one," he said.

The boards along the line parted. A split ran across the floor from the table's far leg toward Jon's boots, a knife's width wide, and a second split came off the first at a slant. The far leg dropped into the split. The table tipped toward the line and came apart in two halves. The cups rolled. The one with the cracked lip went off the edge and broke on the boards.

Jon put his right hand flat on the boards. Under his palm the split ran on across the floor, reached the toe of his boot, and stopped. The planks under his feet were one piece. The gap beyond them was a hair of dark between two boards, and it stayed a hair wide.

Jon's left hand came down over his right to press it, and the two dice dropped where they had hung. The sill shook, and the candlestick rolled off and rang on the boards.

Blood ran out of the cracks across Jon's knuckles and down the back of his hand, onto the boards, and dripped along the line. The hand on the floor shook. He held it down with the other one.

Derrek was laughing again. He had his shoulders against the wall and his eyes shut, and water ran out from under one lid into his ear, and he laughed through it. "That was a good table."

Jon let it go. He stood with the split at his boots and tried to open his fingers. They opened part way, stiff, the knuckles white at the edges where the blood had dried. He tried again. Two went.

"You see your hands?" Derrek had turned to look at the blood. "Jon. Look at your hands."

The potman was still in the doorway with his tray, which was empty now. The cut under his eye had nearly stopped.

Jon looked at the potman for the first time. He looked for about the length of one breath.

"Go on down," Jon said. "Mind the top step, it's loose. Tell them it's the table."
