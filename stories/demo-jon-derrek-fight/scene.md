Jon put his palm on the table's edge and left it there. He did not look at it. He looked at the three cups on the table, and at the one with the cracked lip nearest Derrek's hand. The potman in the doorway had the tray against his chest. The noise from the taproom had gone thin, as if someone had shut a door on it.

"Ya," Jon said. "Hold still a sec."

Derrek was not holding still. He was tipped back on the far chair with one ankle across his knee, working at a loose thread on his cuff. He saw the hand on the table and laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, and he put the back of his wrist to his mouth and the laugh kept coming behind it.

The cup with the cracked lip came up off the table.

It didn't jump. It rose the way a bucket comes up a well, steady, nothing slopping, and it hung level with Derrek's face and turned a little on nothing. Jon's hand stayed on the table's edge. Two fingers of his other hand moved, the way a man moves to ease a cuff. The tea in the cup did not move at all.

Derrek stopped laughing. He looked at the cup. He looked at Jon's wrist, which had not shifted.

"Put it back," he said.

"Ya." Jon lowered the two fingers. The cup came down and sat on its ring, and the tea went over the lip anyway, a thin brown line down the side, and Jon took his hand off the table.

Derrek got up. He did it slowly, so he'd be seen doing it. He put his hands in his pockets and took them out again. For the length of a breath nothing happened. Then the street door at the foot of the stairs swung off its catch. The taproom door, which the potman could see through the stairwell, eased shut on its own. The chairs along the wall slid back, each one a finger's width, all together, and stopped.

It came into the room from every side. The potman felt it behind his ears and in the roots of his teeth, a pressure with no wind in it. The lamp hung level. The window glass gave a low note that did not stop.

A sheen came up on Derrek's forehead and under his jaw. His pulse showed at the side of his throat, quick and high, and he was still grinning. He moved nothing else. Jon watched the chairs, and the lamp, and did not look at the door.

Against the wall by the window stood a shutter bar, iron, the length of a forearm, that the potman had propped there at noon and nobody had touched since. Jon crossed to it. He didn't hurry. The field took his step and the boards under his boot gave back a dead flat knock, not sinking, only hard and short. He closed his hand on the bar in the middle and began to bend it. The iron went in a curve, then a sharper curve, a long slow turn until it closed into a loop that sat over his fist like a handle. His knuckles split along the first two joints, one after another, small wet clicks, and the skin opened white and then red. He made no sound. He flexed the hand, and the loop held.

Derrek leaned on it. Not with his body. The chairs slid another finger toward the wall, and the lamp chain swung out and back, and the loop of iron did not flatten and did not flex. It took the weight of the room and kept its shape. Where Jon's forearm locked against it, the sleeve pulled tight over the bone.

"Bit of a handle, that," Derrek said.

He lifted two fingers out of his pocket. Not at Jon. At the chair nearest the window, the one he'd been sitting on before he got up, and at a place on its back leg a hand above the boards.

The leg split.

It split clean. There was no crack and no burst. The wood came apart down its length along a line, as if it had been scored and was only now being let go, and the two halves stayed upright and touching for a second before they parted. The seat dropped. The cushion slid off onto the floor.

Jon was already off his mark. He had gone left, on his toes, toward the corner, and the splinter that came off the leg came the other way, long and pale, the height of his face, going for his cheek.

It stopped a hand from him.

It hung in the air with its point still aimed, a sliver of wood the length of a finger. Jon's jaw was locked and the cords stood up in his neck. His back heel skidded an inch on the boards. He didn't look at the splinter. He turned his head away from it, slowly, and the splinter turned with the air until his face had passed it, and then it fell with a small dry tick onto the floor.

Derrek watched Jon breathe. He took his time about it. He tipped his head to one side and looked, and his mouth went soft.

"There," he said, quietly, to nobody.

Jon's chest closed. It didn't heave. The ribs drew in and stayed in, and every breath came short and high under the collarbones. The hand on the bar began to shake. A fine tremor first, then the whole forearm, and the loop of iron ticked against the boards.

Jon did not go down. He set his feet and squared his shoulders and kept his eyes on Derrek's face. The potman had seen men take a beating in the yard behind the Gull and had never once seen one stand like that. The shake went out of the hand a finger at a time. Jon's breath came down, a notch at a time, and the ribs opened.

Derrek laughed. It started as a bark and turned into something warmer, pleased with itself.

"Oh, look at that," he said. "You've got it held."

Jon said nothing. He let go of the loop with his right hand, and the loop stayed where it was. He reached to the table and took the two dice from beside the cups, then the pewter candlestick off the sill, and put them in the air, one on each side of himself, and left them there. They turned. Neither one fell. He kept his hands by his sides, looked at Derrek, and offered nothing else.

Downstairs a man called for more ale. Nobody answered him. The lamp went down a little and came back up, and nobody touched it.

Derrek let the field go slack.

The chairs slid back a finger toward the wall and settled. The sheen on his face did not dry. He wiped it on his sleeve. He looked at the dice, then at the candlestick, then at Jon's hands, and then he turned his shoulder on the room and looked out of the window at the yard. He sat on the sill. He didn't say anything for a while.

"Well," he said at last.

Neither of them moved. The potman counted the turns of the nearest die and lost count.

Derrek didn't look at Jon when he spoke again. He looked at the boards between them, a line of them a yard from Jon's left boot.

"Last one," he said.

The boards along the line parted.

Not a blast. A split a knife's width, running across the floor from the table's far leg toward Jon's boots. The table tipped toward it. Its top went over on the line and the two halves came apart, the cups rolled, and the one with the cracked lip went off the edge and broke on the flags.

Jon's hand came off the bar. Then it didn't. He put his other hand flat on the boards, and the split ran on across the floor until it reached the toe of his boot, and there it stopped. The boards under his feet stayed one piece. The gap on the far side stayed open, a hair of dark between the planks, and it did not come any nearer to him.

The dice came down where they had been hanging. Then the candlestick rang on the floor.

Blood ran out of the split across Jon's knuckles, down the back of his hand and onto the boards, and dripped along the line. The hand on the floor shook. He held it down with the other one.

Derrek was laughing again. He had his head back against the wall and his eyes shut, and water ran out from under one lid and into his ear, and he laughed with it. "Christ," he said. "That was a good table."

"Ya," Jon said.

"Same time Thursday?"

Jon didn't answer. He stood with the split at his boots and tried to open his fingers. They opened only part way, stiff, the knuckles white at the edges where the blood had dried. He tried again. Two of them went.

"Look at your hands," Derrek said.

The potman was still in the doorway with his tray.

"Go on down," Jon said. "Tell them it's the table."

The potman went down the stairs with the tray still against his chest. Below him the taproom noise came up to meet him, and he kept his eyes on the steps.
