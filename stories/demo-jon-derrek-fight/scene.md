Jon put his palm flat on the table's edge and left it there. He didn't look at it. He read the top of the table, end to end, where the grain ran under the varnish like wood under ice. Then the three cups. The one with the cracked lip sat nearest Derrek's hand with a thumb's depth of tea in it, flat as a lid. The potman in the doorway had the tray against his chest. The noise from the taproom had gone thin. Somebody down there had stopped talking in the middle of a word.

"Ya," Jon said. "Hold still a sec."

Derrek was not holding still. He was tipped back on the far chair with one ankle across his knee, picking at a loose thread at his cuff. He saw the hand on the table and laughed. It came out before he could catch it, a bark, and he flung his arm wide to take the sound, and the laugh kept coming through the arm.

The cup with the cracked lip came up off the table.

It came up with no jolt. Nothing slopped. It hung level with Derrek's nose and turned a quarter turn on nothing, the tea lying in it smooth. Jon's palm didn't move on the edge. Two fingers of his other hand lifted from his thigh, half an inch, and went down.

Derrek quit laughing. He looked at the cup. He looked at Jon's wrist, which had not shifted.

"Put it back."

Jon let the two fingers fall. The cup came down onto its ring, and the tea went over the cracked lip anyway, one thin brown line down the side. Jon took his hand off the table.

Derrek got up. Both arms went out from his sides, palms up, and his hands began to turn over and over in the air, fast, loose at the wrists, like a man shaking out a sheet. "Feel that? Jon. You feel that?" He swung round to see. "Watch the chairs. Watch the chairs, I'm doing the chairs."

Jon watched the chairs.

The street door at the foot of the stairs swung off its catch and slammed back against the wall. Through the stairwell the potman saw the taproom door ease shut on its own. The chairs along the wall went back across the boards together, one, two, three, and the third went a finger's width too far and hit the panelling with a crack.

It came into the room from every side. The potman felt it behind his ears and in the roots of his teeth, a pressure with no wind in it, pushing, easing, pushing again. The lamp swung out on its chain past where a lamp should go, came back, and swung out again. The window glass took up a low note and kept it going.

A sheen came up on Derrek's forehead and under his jaw. The pulse at the side of his throat jumped, and jumped, and he was grinning through it. "Good," he said. "You're watching. Good."

Jon looked at the chairs, and the lamp, and not at the door.

Against the wall by the window leaned the shutter bar, iron, a forearm long, the one the potman had propped there at noon. Nobody had touched it since. Jon's eyes went down its length once and stopped at a pitted place near the middle, where the grain of the iron ran against the bend it was about to take. Then the boards around it, the nail heads, and the back leg of the chair beside the window, where the wood had gone pale at the joint and a hairline ran up from it. He crossed to the bar. He didn't hurry. The boards under his boot gave one dead flat knock and no more.

The dice lay on the table where he'd left them. The candlestick stood on the sill. The tea in the cup was flat as glass. The lamp swung and the chain creaked, and none of them moved. Jon closed his hand on the bar in the middle and began to bend it.

The iron went in a curve, then a tighter one, a long slow turn until it closed into a loop that sat over his fist like a handle. His knuckles split along the first two joints, one and then the next, small wet clicks. The skin opened white and then red. He flexed the hand, and the loop held.

Derrek leaned into the room with both arms. The chairs slid another stretch toward the wall. The lamp went out on its chain and came back late, and the loop of iron gave nothing to it. It took the weight and kept its shape. The sleeve over Jon's forearm pulled tight across the bone.

"Bit of a handle, that," Derrek said. He laughed, head back, and then whipped round to check. "Jon. Handle."

His hands came down in front of him, then flew out to the window side, fingers spread, and he pointed with the whole arm and most of his body. "Watch this one. Watch the chair." Two fingers came out of the spread at the chair nearest the window, at its back leg, a hand above the boards.

The leg split.

There was no crack and no burst. The wood came apart down its length in one clean line, and the halves stood up touching for a moment before they opened. The seat dropped. The cushion slid off onto the boards.

Derrek's arm kept going. It swung through its arc past the window and into the wall, and while it was still going the jamb of the door split from top to bottom, a seam nobody had pointed at.

Jon was already off his mark. He had gone left, on his toes, toward the corner, and the splinter off the leg came the other way, long and pale, the height of his face. It stopped a hand from his cheek and hung there, point still aimed. His back heel skidded an inch on the boards. He didn't look at it. He turned his head from it, slowly, and the splinter turned with the air until his face had passed, and then it dropped with a small dry tick.

The jamb was still going. A sliver came off it at head height, the length of a finger, and went out across the doorway at the potman's face.

The potman didn't move. The tray was against his chest, and his feet had decided, without asking him, to stop. He saw the point. He saw the grain running out along it.

Jon's hand came up. He didn't hurry it. The bent two fingers lifted, lazy, out and flat, palm to the doorway, and the sliver stopped a foot from the potman's eye and hung there, turning a little. Jon looked at Derrek, and at nothing else.

A flake of the jamb, no bigger than a nail paring, got past the edge of his hand. It opened the skin under the potman's eye, a short hot line. The tray dipped, and a heel of bread slid off and went down the stairs.

Jon lowered the sliver to the boards in front of the potman's boot and let it go.

"Ya," he said. "You're all right. Stand where you are."

Derrek whooped. His arms went up over his head and came down, and his hands shook as if they were full of water. "Did you see? You saw that. I never meant the door, Jon, the door's just in everything." He was turning again, arms out. "You saw me put it by his eye."

Jon said nothing.

"Breathe for me," Derrek said, and his voice went right down, low and close. "Go on. Let me hear it."

The room waited. Nothing in Jon's shoulders moved that the potman could see. The hand on the bar began to shake, a fine tremor at the fingers, then the wrist, then the whole forearm, and the loop ticked against the boards.

"Your heart's going," Derrek said. "I can see it from here. Look at it go. You watching?" The field pushed in on the room, came off, pushed harder. The chairs jumped toward the wall. The window note climbed and held.

Jon set his feet. His eyes didn't leave Derrek's face. The shake went out of the hand a joint at a time, the fingers first, then the wrist, until the loop lay still over his fist and the blood ran slower down the back of it.

Derrek laughed. It started as a bark and went up into something enormous, arms wide, head back. "Oh, look at that. You've got it held." He turned on the spot to check. Jon was looking at him.

Jon let go of the loop with his right hand. The loop didn't fall. It hung at the height of his fist and turned slowly on nothing. He reached to the table and took up the dice, and then the candlestick off the sill, and put them in the air, one on either side of himself, and left them there. They turned. Neither fell. He kept his hands by his sides and looked at Derrek, and said nothing at all.

Derrek let the field go slack.

The chairs slid back toward the wall and got no further. The sheen on his face didn't dry. He wiped it on his sleeve and looked at the dice, then the candlestick, then at Jon's hands, and then he turned his shoulder on the room and looked out of the window at the yard. He sat on the sill with his heels against the wall and his palms flat on the wood.

"Well," he said at last.

Neither of them moved. The potman counted the turns of the nearer die and lost count somewhere past nine.

Derrek didn't look round. He looked at the boards between them, a line of planks a yard from Jon's left boot.

"Last one," he said.

The boards along the line parted.

Not a blast. A split a knife's width wide, running from the table's far leg across the floor toward Jon's boots, and then, as if Derrek's hand had changed its mind, a second split coming off the first at a slant. The table tipped toward it. Its top went over on the line and came apart in two halves. The cups rolled. The one with the cracked lip went off the edge and broke on the flags.

Jon's hand came off the bar. He put it flat on the boards. The split ran on across the floor, reached the toe of his boot, and stopped there. The boards under his feet were still one piece. The gap beyond them was a hair of dark between two planks, and it came no nearer.

The dice came down where they'd been hanging. Then the candlestick rang on the flags.

Blood ran out of the cracks across Jon's knuckles and down the back of his hand, onto the boards, and dripped along the line. The hand on the floor shook. He held it down with the other one.

Derrek was laughing again. He had his head back against the wall and his eyes shut, and water ran out from under one lid into his ear, and he laughed through it. "Christ," he said. "That was a good table."

"Same time Thursday?"

Jon didn't answer. He stood with the split at his boots and tried to open his fingers. They opened part way, stiff, the knuckles white at the edges where the blood had dried. He tried again. Two went.

"You see your hands?" Derrek had turned to look. "Jon. Look at your hands."

The potman was still in the doorway with his tray, which was empty now. The cut under his eye had nearly stopped.

Jon looked at the potman for the first time. He didn't look long.

"Go on down," Jon said. "Mind the top step, it's loose. Tell them it's the table."
