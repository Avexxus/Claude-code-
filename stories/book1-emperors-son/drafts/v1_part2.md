## FOUR · The Door

"—dead moon, edge of elven space, and the floor's gone black where the world's worn thin," Jon was telling Teo. Before that it had been a fish. "Inquisition station on one rim. On the ninth of every month a dwarven ship sets down on the other."

A bad meal in a worse port, two months on from Thirty-One. The ninth was five days off.

"What for?" said Eli.

"Buying," said Jon, and went on eating.

"Buying what?"

Jon didn't look up.

"Dark elves. The Inquisition catches them as they come out and calls them soul thieves, and the dwarves move them on." He tore his bread. "Organs, mostly. An elf liver's meant to keep you young. It doesn't, but try telling a rich man. The strong ones go to the pits on the inner worlds; they fit them with claws and bet on them. The pretty ones go to houses to stand in halls and be looked at. Some go for crew, to anyone who wants hands that last. Some get grown into things." He pushed the salt across. "This is very good, have you tried it?"

Eli lay awake with it. Two walls away the port's air plant knocked, and caught, and knocked. In the morning he said, "We're going to the crater."

"Why?" said Teo. "They're elves. It's an elf thing. Isn't it? It's their thing, it's not even—"

"The buyers are ours," said Eli. "Rich men, he said. Human. That's Empire money on that ship." He was already checking the flyer's charge. "If I'm taking that chair, I'd like to know what comes with it."

Teo looked at Doc for help. Doc was eating the rest of Teo's bread.

Jon didn't come. "A station keeps ledgers," he said, doing up his coat. "I'm in one already." He had elves to see, and there was a hull out that afternoon, and he was on it with a bag of peaches before Teo had finished the argument. Two days later they paid a hauler to carry them out to the moon's system with the flyer in its hold, and flew the last of it themselves.

---

They watched from the rim for a day. The grit was black and fine and got into the food, and the seals of the flyer, and the creases of their hands. The station was a squat grey block on the far rim with its lights on all night. Nobody came out of it.

On the evening of the ninth the dwarven ship came down on the near side of the floor, low and black, and sat there ticking. Inquisitors in grey walked a line of chained figures out of the station and across the floor toward it. They were starved thin and shackled wrist to wrist, and their eyes caught the station lights and gave them back silver. They walked with their heads down and didn't look at the ship. At the ramp a dwarf in a good coat counted them. He looked in each mouth and pulled down each lower eyelid with his thumb, and marked foreheads with a grease pencil as they went by: a ring, a cross, a line. The smallest came up to his chest. An inquisitor counted the money.

Overhead a human freighter blinked out of the sky, and the air over the black floor shuddered after it like water after a stone.

"Where'd it go?" said Teo.

"Through," said Doc. He was watching the floor. "Their prison's just the other side, you see. Humans drop through it to cross quicker, and the elves hate that, oh, terribly. Their own ships go squeezed down a dimension, and in there they can't feel each other at all. Not a whisper."

"How do you know all that?"

"I listen," said Doc.

The trade at the ramp was nearly done. Eli went down the crater wall.

He didn't sweep. He found the guns on the dwarven ship as he had found the moth under the temple roof, one small hard thing at a time, and closed his grip on each barrel and bent it. Four on the flanks. Two by the ramp. He was still feeling along the hull for more when one he'd missed opened up from underneath, and it walked across the floor and through the end of the line, and he found it and crushed it, and by then it was nine.

Nine. Wrist to wrist. They went down together.

Then everything. He tore weapons out of inquisitors' hands, some of them. Lost the rest. A shot cracked off the rock by his head. Teo shouting on the rim. Teo's rifle, twice.

Doc coming down the wall behind him, slow, puffing, talking to himself. A dwarven crewman off the ramp with a cutting bar. Doc stepping aside, small and clumsy, and the bar going into the floor, and the troll's stick coming down across the man's knees, and down again, until he stopped trying to get up.

Then nothing was moving that wasn't theirs. It might have been three minutes. The inquisitors were dead or running, and the dwarf in the good coat was face down on his own ramp with Eli's grip on the back of his neck, saying something in dwarvish into the metal. Up on the rim Teo sat in the black grit with both hands clamped round his thigh and blood coming through his fingers, swearing at the man he'd shot, who was dead.

They broke the chains. Nobody thanked them. Most of the freed ones ran, out across the floor and into the dark under the far wall, without a word to each other or to anyone. The two at the end of the line lay where the gun had left them, still chained together. Eli broke that chain too, and stood there afterwards with the pieces.

---

One stayed. An old man with no teeth, at the edge of the place where the floor went strange, staring at the air over it. The air there trembled, very faintly, like a pane of glass in wind.

"Someone's there," he said. "I know that shake. I made it once, from the other side. Someone's at the door."

Eli couldn't feel souls. Nobody had ever said he could. But five months on a cold floor had taught him to feel the world a little, and over the black floor it was thin, and it was bulging, slightly, toward him.

"They've been at it a while," Doc said quietly. "Look how the floor's worn. Poor thing."

"Can it be opened?"

The old man laughed with no teeth. "From this side? By who? It doesn't open. It takes."

Eli put out his hands.

Five months of moths and cups of water. He found the edge of the thin place and felt along it a long time, the way he'd felt for the needle's eye, and found a seam narrower than that and got his grip into it, and pulled in a direction that wasn't there. It pulled back. His nose began to bleed. His sight went white at the edges, and went on going white. Somewhere Teo was saying his name, and saying it again, higher.

The seam gave.

A woman fell out of the air onto her hands and knees, and it closed behind her with a sound like a cough.

Starved thin. Rags tied over rags. A short curved knife in each hand. She came up off the floor already moving and went past Eli without looking at him. The last two inquisitors were running for their station. She caught them at the door and went in close, a palm on a chest, fingertips at a throat, and they folded on the step.

She walked back. She looked at the blood on his lip, and then at the air behind him, which had gone still.

"You opened it," she said. Her voice was rough from not being used. "From out here."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Someone was pushing."

She looked at him with plain contempt.

"Thirty of us, when we started," she said. "No one escapes alone. That's what they say down there." She looked at the closed air. "I was a child."

Eli's hands were coming up again. He hadn't decided it. She looked at them, and then at his face, and he put them down.

"The road had most," she said. "The door had the rest."

When he looked round for the old man, he had gone into the dark after the others.

Her eyes were silver, like the eyes in every story the nurses in the house had ever told him.

*Oh*, thought Eli.

"What's your name?"

"When you've earned it."

---

They took the dwarven ship. It was low and black, Returned work, and Doc read the name off its flank in dwarvish, *Ledger Closed*, and said it was a very hopeful thing to call a ship. It could squeeze through the world the way dwarven ships did. Teo would fly it, with his leg tied off in a strip of somebody's coat and his face the colour of the station walls.

The dwarf in the good coat sat on the grit at the foot of his ramp where they'd put him. The grease pencil was still in his breast pocket. He watched their faces one at a time, as he'd watched the prisoners' mouths, and when he got to Eli's he stayed there, the eyes and then the chin.

"Leave him a water ration," said Doc. "There's nothing here, you see. Not till the ninth."

Heels on slag. A rig clicking on a dead man's back.

"No," said Eli.

Doc looked at him. "That's slower," he said mildly.

"No water."

The dark elf was sitting on the ramp with her knives across her knees. She looked at Eli then, properly, for the first time.

"They'll want this ship back," Teo said, in the pilot's chair, with his teeth together. "You know that. Whoever it belongs to, they'll want it."

"It's fast," said Eli.

They lifted with the flyer in the hold. The dwarf was a small dark shape on the grit and then he wasn't.

Two days out, Eli counted the stores. They were a water ration short. He counted again and got the same number. Down the corridor Doc was in the galley dressing Teo's leg and talking about goats, and Teo was telling him to shut up about the goats, and Doc was telling him this was a different goat. Eli stood outside the galley and listened to it. Then he went back to the hold.

He thought about the ones who had run into the dark. There was nowhere on that moon to run to. The station had the only air and water, and the dwarven ship had been the only ship, and he was standing in it. The Inquisition would be back on the ninth.

---

It took her four months to stop sleeping with a knife in her hand.

She ate fast and without a sound, with her arm curled round her plate, and she couldn't stand a closed door, or being thanked, so they left the galley hatch open and stopped thanking her for things. Jon still wasn't back. Eli got into the habit of sitting up late in the galley with the hatch open, in case she wanted to talk. Mostly she didn't, and he sat there anyway, with the tea going cold and the drive humming up through the deck into his feet.

Some nights she told him things. She had trained on the road, she said, fifty years of it, with whoever would teach her, and paid for most of it by fighting. The road ran between the towns down there, one farm to the next. One teacher had sold her to a pit for three years and kept the money.

"A pit down there," she said. "For the human crews who stop over on the crossing. They came down to bet." She turned her wrist on the table. The rings round it were from the pit chain. "Fair price, for what he taught me."

"You could have gone up with one of them," said Eli. "In a hold. Nobody looks in a hold."

"The crews are the farmers' customers. They count what they carry." She went on eating. "They sell what they find at the next port."

Since the door, she said another night, she could feel the elves. Never down there. Out here they were always at the edge of her, a light, the homeworld, trillions of them from across the galaxy.

"It never goes out," she said, not looking at him. "Worst thing they did. They let us keep that."

Teo came in for water one night on his bad leg and called her a dark elf without thinking. She didn't look up from her plate. "There's a sun down there," she said. "It's a world. Like these. They named it hell. Then they named us after it." Teo filled his cup and went out, and he didn't use the word again.

One good night she talked for most of an hour, about a river on the road where the water came up warm out of the ground, and how you could sleep in it if you tied yourself to a root. When she got up to go he said, "Thank you."

He heard it on its way out of his mouth. She stopped in the hatch. Then she went, and she didn't come to the galley the next night, or the one after.

He sat up anyway. Nine nights the chair across from him stayed empty, and he drank his tea and listened to the drive. On the tenth he looked up from the cup and she was in the chair. He hadn't heard her come in. Neither of them said anything about it.

"The ones they sent down for stealing souls kept on doing it," she said, some nights after that. "Nobody stops them. They farm the towns. Every street pays a share. My street paid in children. Children are small, and nobody misses them long." She went back to eating. Then, still eating: "The ones that get grown into things. What do your people do with them."

Eli opened his mouth.

"I don't know," he said.

She nodded, and finished her plate.

---

She practised alone in the hold at night and stopped whenever anyone came. One night in the second month she didn't hear Eli. He hadn't meant to be quiet. He was in his socks.

She stood in the middle of the empty deck with one hand raised, the fingers a little curled, and closed them slowly. The air in front of her folded in on itself with a small flat sound, like a book being shut. A cargo strap a yard away went into it and was gone. That was all. It was very quiet. Eli had broken walls from across a room, and he had never once made anything go so quietly.

She saw him and dropped her hand.

"Lirael's Collapse," she said. "Down there you learn her name before your own. Everyone wants that one." She looked at where the strap had been. "Forty years. It's the one thing of theirs we took."

Jon was in the doorway behind Eli. They had picked him up off a port that morning, two months gone. Teo had asked him where, and Jon had said elves, and that five weeks of it were nobody's business, and smiled, and gone straight to his bunk. He had been grey in the face then. He was grey now.

"That's a hard one to learn," he said. "Good for you."

Her face shut.

He went off down the corridor whistling. Halfway along he lost the tune, and stopped, and started it again from the top.

## FIVE · The Queen and the Giant

Eli put the ship down in the dry riverbed between the two camps and walked out alone under both sets of guns.

The dead were stacked in the dunes on either side, under sheets on the elven side and under nothing on the other. A colony at the far end of nowhere, cut off so long it had forgotten anyone else was out there, and an elven seedship come down in the middle of its oldest story with its back broken. The colonists called their world the Dry. Invaders, they said. Vermin, said the elves.

Jon had brought them there for water, he said. He stood at the top of the ramp in his coat in the heat, looking at the seedship, not at Eli.

This much the house had taught him. He found the colony's grandmothers and the elven captain's second and sat them across a folding table in the sun, and let each side describe the other until it ran out of words. On the second day the elves walked out. On the fourth a grandmother spat on the table and the talks stopped for two days, and Eli sat at the table alone in the heat through both of them, with a jug of water in front of him that he didn't touch. Both camps could see the jug.

On the sixth day they were back at the table, and a colonist boy shot an elf at the water line.

Eli walked the boy to the elven camp himself. The boy was perhaps fourteen and didn't struggle. He kept asking where they were going, though he could see. Eli handed him over and stood there while they did it. They knelt him at the water line and the captain's second cut his throat, and two elves held him by the ankles so the blood went into the sand and not into the water. Eli stayed until it was done.

There was sand in his collar. It had worked down under his shirt to the small of his back, and for most of it that was what he felt.

One of the grandmothers was the boy's. She came back to the table the next morning and sat in her place and never spoke to Eli again. By the ninth day there was a line in the riverbed that neither side crossed, and nobody had died for three days.

---

On the twelfth day an elven ship came down out of the sky, and a woman walked down its ramp.

She was taller than any elf he'd met, armoured, young-looking in the way that meant nothing with elves. Her sword hung at her hip in a pale, faintly ribbed sheath, and the sheath shifted against her leg when she walked, not quite in time with her. The seedship elves straightened as she passed and stopped talking.

"They call her the Queen," Jon said quietly beside him. "Like on the board. The strongest piece in the box. Nothing to do with who her mother was."

She stopped in front of Eli and took in his face and his hands, and her mouth curled a little.

"You saved four hundred of my king's people," she said, "with a folding table. My king has heard of you. He hears you are going to the Tower."

Eli looked at Jon. Jon was looking at the seedship.

"My king has sworn to kill the man in that Tower," said the Queen. "So has every elf alive. In all the ages he has sat in it, not one of us has passed its door. Now a human walks toward it, a human who can talk his way across a riverbed, and my king would like me to walk behind him." Her eyes were flat. "No one said I must like it."

She went past all of them to their ramp. At the top the dark elf was standing in the hatch, and she stepped back out of it without a word and let her by. Jon was at the foot of the ramp. The Queen didn't slow.

"They tell me you answered well," she said.

"I always do," said Jon.

She took the best bunk. It had been Doc's. Doc moved his bag out without a word, and then with several.

---

That night in the galley Eli asked Jon about the sword. He'd been watching the sheath all afternoon.

"Grown," said Jon. He was peeling something with a small knife. "When they catch one of the hell-born outside, they grow a blade out of her. It takes about a year. She's alive for most of it; it holds better that way." He thought about it. "It's a very good sword."

The dark elf was in the galley doorway. Eli didn't know how long she'd been there. He turned in his chair, and it was empty.

Her cabin door was shut when he went past it. He stood outside it a while. Then he went on.

She didn't come out for two days. On the second he sat down on the deck in the corridor with his back to the bulkhead beside her door and said nothing, and after an hour her voice came through it, low down, as though she were sitting on the floor on the other side.

"I can feel her," she said. "Close. Loud."

A long time.

"From the sword, nothing. I knew there'd be nothing." The deck hummed. "There's nobody there."

He waited. There wasn't any more.

He found the Queen at dusk at the top of the ramp, watching the seedship elves fill barrels at the colony's well under the line. He chose the words on the way up the corridor, and he said them carefully. The crew decks were narrow. If she would keep the blade aft when she could, off them—

"No," said the Queen. She didn't stop walking.

On the third day the dark elf came out. Her hands weren't quite steady. In the corridor she passed the Queen with her back to the bulkhead, and she looked at the sheath the whole way past, and never once at the Queen. The Queen never looked at her at all. After that everyone else learned to wait until one of them had gone by.

---

The ship sat in the riverbed another week while the seedship took on water, a barrel at a time, with the grandmothers counting every one.

The Queen sparred with him in the hold every day and put him on the deck every day, without malice and without effort, and walked off while he was still getting up. She called him *the human*. "The human is bleeding on the floor," she told Doc once, on her way out, as she might have mentioned a leak.

"Why her?" Eli asked Jon one night, when his ribs hurt too much to sleep.

"Mika sent her," said Jon. "Mika's very much in charge." He patted Eli's arm. "I'm glad he did, though. She's better than you, and she hates you. You'll come on twice as fast."

Late, in the galley, with the dregs of the tea gone cold, the dark elf went past the open hatch with her pack on her back.

She didn't look in. He heard her bare feet go down the corridor, and then on the ramp. Past the ramp there was the riverbed and the line in it, and past that the dunes, and the dead under their sheets, and then more dunes to the edge of the world and nothing in any of them. He took hold of the edge of the table. He didn't get up.

He counted. He lost it in the usual place and started again.

An hour, near enough. Feet on the ramp, coming up. She passed the hatch going the other way, and she didn't have the pack.

In the morning she ate across from him with her arm round her plate, as before. He never found out where the hour had gone.

---

The giant was Eli's idea. A war needed ships, and the giants built the cathedral ships for the dragons and sold them and kept to themselves, fewer every century, everyone said so, and Jon knew one who would talk to a human.

From orbit the forge world was black rock under a red sky, and its yards held a dozen cathedral ships in their cradles, each the length of a city, ribbed and pale and half-made. Feed lines ran up into the cradles from a ring of dwarven bulk haulers in low orbit. From the shuttle window Eli saw what was in them. Orcs, single file, walking up the long ribbed tubes into the half-made hulls. None came back down. He watched until the shuttle turned and the window filled with rock.

The giant met them in a hall so big it had its own weather. Cloud hung under the roof, and at the far end, where nobody stood, a thin rain was falling on the stone and running off down channels cut for it. Sitting, he was the height of a four-storey house. When he spoke, Eli felt it in the soles of his feet.

"Jon," he said.

"Hello, old friend."

"I am not your friend," said the giant. "But I am friendly." He looked down at the rest of them. "Sit, little ones."

Doc sat. Teo sat, with his bad leg out in front of him. Eli stayed on his feet. He asked for passage, and a ship, and the giants' old quarrel with the dragons turned on the one man who sat above them all. The giant heard him out in silence.

"Do you know what those are made of?" he said. "Once, our own dead. Families. A grandmother in the keel, her sons in the ribs. There are not enough of us left to die, so now we buy. The dwarves bring them by the hauler-load, and they walk in, and the hull closes on them, and they stay. That is what makes it a ship and not a shell." The rain went on at the far end of the hall. "Every hull that leaves Hurrunaal goes to a dragon. We build them for the dragons, so the dragons will kill each other. You humans are their favourite pupils." He looked at Eli. "And why would I lend a ship to a human?"

"Because I'm the Emperor's son," Eli said.

Behind him the Queen went very still.

The giant didn't move. Then he told them about the golden age: the giants' laws and the giants' peace, and the dragons who had been their seconds and decided they would rule better. On betrayal day the emperor's closest advisor, a dragon, walked into the heart of the capital and breathed it to ash. In the last moment of his life the emperor took his whole city, every body and every soul in it, and shaped it into a shield.

"What was left is the Tower," said the giant. "Your people took it from the dragons, and your father built his seat in it. Your father's house. Do not call it a building. The souls of that day are still in its walls, all of them, run together."

Eli hadn't called it anything.

The giant looked at him a long while, without hurry, as he might have looked at a bill.

"A hostage is worth what the other side would pay," he said. "I have read your father's posters." The rain at the end of the hall. "Keep you, and we feed you. Let you go, and we lose nothing."

He leaned down until his face filled the air in front of Eli.

"And you would like me to send giants to knock on its door. No."

A servant showed them out by a side door. It was the one they used for deliveries. Jon stayed behind in the hall, and the great doors shut on him and the giant together.

---

The Queen came for him that night in the dark corridor outside the hall.

He didn't see the first cut. Cold. A line of it, across his ribs. The second he felt coming, the air moving ahead of the blade, the moth, and he put a wall of hard air in its way. The blade flexed against the wall and slid round it. He got his grip on her wrist. She broke it with a twist he didn't follow. Her knee in his chest. The floor. Her foot on his throat, and the pale edge an inch from his eye.

"His son," she said. Her voice was level. "My king sent me to guard the son of the man who—"

She stopped. The blade trembled. He had never seen it do that.

"He knew. My king knew, and sent me anyway, and did not tell me."

Eli looked down the corridor for Jon. Empty. At the end of it the hall doors were shut.

"Then ask him," Eli said. It came out a croak round her foot. "The relay's on the ship. If he says kill me, you've lost a day."

She looked down at him. Then the blade moved, very fast, and opened his cheek from ear to jaw, and she stepped back and sheathed it, and the sheath closed over it like a mouth.

"So you remember who you are," she said.

---

Doc stitched him on the ship, the ribs and then the cheek, and told him it would scar. He didn't make a joke. He didn't mention a goat. Eli lay there waiting for the goat the whole time, and it didn't come.

Jon came back near dawn, saw the stitches and asked what had happened, and when Eli told him he said, "Ah." He lifted a hand toward Eli's face, then put it in his coat pocket.

"The yard wants the berth back by morning," he said to Teo, brisk. "Can we?"

Teo said they could.

"What did you talk about," said Eli. "All night."

"Old times." Jon turned. "Teo, is there any breakfast?"

Eli put his fingers on the stitches and went down them, ear to jaw. Twenty-six. Doc had done them very small. A side door and twenty-six stitches: that was what the name had bought. He wasn't going to spend it again.

She sent the message from the relay before they were out of the system. The reply came in eleven hours. Eli never saw it. He saw her face when she read it. Then she went down to the training deck and put her fist through the bulkhead twice, and came back with her knuckles split to the bone and her face exactly as it had been.

"Continue," she said, to no one. Her mouth hardly opened on it.

Eli thought of the man on the stones in Seluvane, and didn't know why.

After that she stopped calling him *the human*, or anything at all. She sparred with him every day and put him on the deck as before. But she no longer walked off while he was getting up. She stood and watched until he was on his feet, and then she put him down again.
