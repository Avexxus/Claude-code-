## ONE · Property

The guard didn't get tired. That was the whole problem.

Eli did. It started in his thighs and climbed into his chest, until every breath came in hot and short and not enough. Behind him the guard's boots hit the slag in a rhythm that never broke, and the rig on the man's back, a ribbed hump of grown flesh in a steel cage, gave a wet click every few strides.

"Stop! By imperial warrant!" The voice came out of a speaker at his collar, flat and much too loud. "Return what you took!"

One reach and the guard would be a stain on the wall. In the street. In daylight, with every window looking to see whose reach it was. He ran.

Towers on both sides, white, grown up out of the bedrock without a seam, the cheap kind every colony puts up, twelve floors and a flat roof. The one he'd grown up under had filled every window. He stopped looking up.

Wire ahead. Past it the yards: slag, cranes, open sheds, pens. Hot fat and ozone in his mouth.

He went over the fence. It bit him on the way, and he came down hard on one hip, and got up, and kept going.

The pens were full. Orcs in rows under the shed roofs, hundreds deep, men and women both, heavy in the shoulder and bare to the waist, waiting their turn on the ramps that ran up into the hull presses. Each of them had a neat pale scar at the base of the skull.

At the top of each ramp a press shut with a long sigh and opened again, and out of its far side, on rollers, came plate: ribbed, the size of a door, steaming in the cold. LEGION CONTRACT, CARRIER PLATE, down the flank of every press in yellow stencil. Men in gloves stacked it. When one of them leaned on a stack to rest, he took his hand away quickly, because it was warm.

The orcs on the ramps could see the stacks.

The young ones, not yet full in the shoulder, were penned apart beside a windowless shed with CORES stencilled over the door. At the rail one of them had wrapped a rag round his hand where the wire had cut it. He looked up at Eli going past, and then down at his feet. A blue comb lay in the slag by the rail with most of its teeth gone.

The guard didn't bother with the fence. He stopped at the wire, took a slate off his belt and spoke into it.

Every orc in the yard turned their head at once.

They came out of the pens in a slow wave, over the rails, hands open and reaching. The young one with the rag was crying. An old man near the front with a split lip was saying something over and over while his legs walked him forward, and when he got close enough Eli heard that it was a name.

Shed wall at his back. Nowhere.

Eli reached.

The air in front of him went hard and he swung it, and the front rank went over into the rank behind. He felt bones go, and heard them. More came on over the ones he'd dropped. The ones on the ground were trying to get up. Some of them couldn't.

He could have cleared the yard.

A man on the counterweight of the nearest crane, twenty feet up. Eating a peach. Two stones on the iron beside him already. A long coat buttoned to the chin, and the kind of face that looks pleased to see you before it knows who you are.

"You look," he said, "like you could use an exit."

He dropped off the crane, landed hard, and came across the slag still chewing. A woman with burn scars up both arms got to Eli first. Her eyes were on his face and she was saying sorry, sorry, in a small ordinary voice, while her hands came up for his throat. The man stepped in and laid two fingers on the back of her neck, on the scar, the way you'd test a child's forehead for fever. Her legs went. He let her down.

He took the next one by the wrist, turned him, touched, and let him down too. Then two at once, a hand for each, and he grunted at that and shook his fingers out afterwards. He kept himself between them and Eli and took them as they came. By the fifth or sixth he was breathing through his mouth. The old man with the split lip reached them still saying the name, and the man put a hand to the back of his head, and the name stopped halfway.

Then nobody else came. Over by the fence the guard had lowered the slate and was backing off, talking fast into his collar. In the pens and on the ramps the rest stood where they'd been stopped, hands half up. The young one with the rag was still crying.

The man wiped his fingers on his coat and put a warm hand on Eli's shoulder.

"Breathe," he said. "You're doing beautifully." He nodded at the fence. "He's seen your face. He'll write it up, and it goes up, and up again, till it lands on the desk of whoever you're running from." The hand stayed where it was. "Your call. I won't make it for you."

Eli had ordered men flogged. At sixteen he'd watched a steward's hands broken for theft; his tutors said a prince should see what his word cost. He had never done it himself. Not once.

He reached. Forty yards of slag, and then the guard's throat, and he closed his grip on it and held. It took longer than he'd thought it would. The guard's heels drummed on the slag. Somebody's lunch tin stood open on the step of the nearest shed, with a fork in it. When it was over the body sat down against the fence, and the rig on its back kept clicking.

Eli's hand was shaking. He looked at it and thought that he would have to wash it before he ate anything, and then about where there might be water out here, and whether it would be clean, while it went on shaking.

The man took the hand in both of his and held it until it was still. "That's how you know you're not him," he said. "Whoever he is." He let go. "What did you steal, by the way?"

Eli wiped his mouth.

"Me," he said.

The man laughed so hard he had to bend over with his hands on his knees. Then he straightened up, finished the peach, and dropped the stone on the old orc with the split lip. It landed on his cheek and stayed there. Nobody took it off. Eli kept thinking someone should.

---

Teo was meant to be at the fence with the flyer. He wasn't.

Eli stood among the bodies and watched the sky over the sheds and counted to six hundred, and started again. Somewhere in the city a siren went up and down and quit. The man in the coat sat on an upturned bucket, cleaning his nails with a sliver of fence wire, and didn't offer to help.

Somewhere in the four hundreds the count went out from under him. He was thinking about the bath off his old rooms, down on the side of the house where the Tower's shadow lay all day and the light came in grey: the tiles warm underfoot, the taps shaped like fishes, and which of the fishes ran hot. He couldn't remember which. He started again from one.

"He's not coming," the man said pleasantly, somewhere in the second six hundred. "We could walk."

Eli didn't move.

The flyer came in low over the sheds forty minutes late, an old plain-engine thing with a cracked canopy and a fresh dent down one side, and hit the slag hard. Teo leaned out, white in the face, talking before the engine had stopped.

"They put a checkpoint on the ring road, I had to go round by the drainage cuts, the whole way round, I thought you were dead, I thought they'd—" He saw the bodies. He looked at the guard against the fence, and then at the stranger.

Nine days ago Teo had forged the code that put the two of them in the hold of an empty food convoy, outbound. His parents were still in the house.

"Did you do all that?" he said to Eli.

"No," said Eli.

"Jon," said the stranger, and climbed into the back of the flyer as though he'd been invited. "Friend of the family."

"Which family?" said Eli.

"Long story. Bad ending."

Teo lifted off. Below them the orcs were still standing in the pens. Another man with a slate had come out of the sheds, and a yard crew behind him with hooks. They worked along the dead in a line, and the bodies went up the ramps to the presses with everyone else, because the yard paid by weight. The young one with the rag was on a ramp. He was still crying, and his feet were still climbing.

"Where to?" said Teo.

Jon had found another peach somewhere. "Up," he said. "There's a hull going out tonight that takes flyers in the hold."

## TWO · The Master

They came down out of terraced hills on the fourth morning, green in rows on every slope. Teo said it was food. Growing, in the ground, where anyone could take it. Eli looked at it a long time.

That afternoon a drone came down the road Jon had sworn the drones didn't watch, and hung in the air ahead of them, looking.

Teo dropped them into the river gorge so hard that Eli bit his tongue. Under the overhang he killed the engine. Water underneath, brown and loud. Somewhere above them the drone's hum, holding one note.

Jon was asleep in the back with his mouth open.

An hour. Teo's hands never came off the controls.

"Can't you do something?" he said at last, to the back.

Jon didn't open his eyes. "Then whoever's watching would know what's in here."

He went back to sleep. After a while he snored a little. Eli sat with his back against the wet rock and watched him, a man who had walked into a yard full of chipped orcs not a week before, and listened to the drone not leave. It left after dark. When Jon woke he said they must have changed the routes, and ate something, and that was all he said about it.

That night Teo sat alone on the wing for an hour. When he came back in he said to Eli, quietly, "He just touched them and they died. He was eating a peach."

Jon, in his blanket, said without opening his eyes that he hadn't killed all of them, only the ones that reached Eli. The rest would have woken up in the pens and gone up the ramps on schedule. "They're awake in there the whole time, you know," he said. "The chip doesn't put you out. It only does the walking."

Nobody said anything about the one at the fence.

Teo lay down across the front seats. After a while, half under, he said, "They were closing the house. Did you know? Before we went."

"Closing it how?"

"The stewards had a list. My mother was on it." A long breath. "The codes were easy, that's the thing. Nobody watches a servant's boy at a terminal. Nobody ever—"

He stopped there. Eli waited for the rest of it and it didn't come, and after a while Teo's breathing changed.

In the morning Jon made Teo breakfast and asked about his parents and listened to the whole answer, the kitchens, his father's bad knees, the list, and said they must be very proud of him. By noon Teo was laughing at his stories.

On the seventh morning Jon climbed out at a fuel stop in the middle of nothing and stretched until his back cracked.

"I have to go and see some elves about a thing," he said.

"What do you want from me?" said Eli.

Jon looked at him pleasantly.

"Company," he said, and walked off down the road.

---

After that he came and went. A week on a salvage hull where Eli and Teo were cutting cable out of dead ships for cash. Nothing all winter. A transit platform, and a bag of peaches, and a month. When Eli asked where he went, he said, "Elves."

Eli learned what food cost. He went hungry three days on a station where nobody would hire a man with soft hands, and on the fourth he broke a dock boss's knee for a job, and took the job. Teo didn't speak to him for a week. Eli found he minded that more than the knee.

Half the shopfronts on that station kept a little shrine to the Emperor's god, lit with a cheap bulb, and not one of them had the Emperor's face on it. Where a face would have gone there was the seal, the plain empty oval that was on the coins too. Nobody knew the face. Eli walked past the shrines twice a day, to the docks and back, and it was most of a month before it came to him that he didn't know it either.

On the docks they talked about elves. Raiders had hit an imperial convoy three days out from the capital, forty hulls, the whole string.

"Food," said the man next to Eli on the line. "Earth'll be eating its furniture."

"Earth's never missed a meal in its life," said somebody else.

Eli had come out on one of those convoys, nine days in an empty hold with no window. He kept his head down and his hands moving.

Crates came off the transit hulls with well-company marks on them, POOL-GRADE CORE, THIS WAY UP. A dockhand kicked a cargo loader that had stopped to think too long. "Battery," he said, and spat.

Jon was in that month. He watched the loader trundle off. "Orc," he said. "Young one, by the size of the core. Flesh takes the shape better at that age. They put pool on the label so nobody has to feel funny about it." He offered Eli a peach.

Eli took it.

The station's legal exchange was two decks below the hiring hall, and Eli passed it every morning. It had a clean white front and a board of prices that changed through the day: eyes, kidneys, skin by the hand's width, ELF LIVER ENQUIRE WITHIN. Behind the glass the whole-body stock stood asleep in upright cases with tags at their throats. CLONE. NO SOUL. GRADE B. One morning a buyer had a case opened to look at a man's teeth, and the man woke. His face did what a baby's face does just before it cries. The clerk put him under again. "Reflex," he told the buyer. "Nobody home." The buyer took the kidneys and one eye and left the rest on account. The eye cost about what Eli made in a week on the docks. Two decks further down, with no front at all, the same stock sold for four times as much with papers saying born.

Eli stopped reading the board.

---

In the fourth month he found his own face on a post outside a customs hall.

RECOVER IMPERIAL PROPERTY, it said, under the seal. Return what you took. It had him down to the scar on his chin. By the date, it had gone up seven days after he ran. Two days before the yard.

He stood in front of it while the shift changed in the hall behind him.

He'll write it up, Jon had said. And it goes up, and up again.

It had gone up already. The man against the fence had died for nothing, and Jon had known.

He looked at the face a long time. The print was cheap. The mouth was his, or near enough. But the jaw under the scar had come out harder and squarer than he thought of his own, and the longer he looked the less it belonged to him. A stranger's jaw.

Teo came up beside him, read it, and said nothing.

"Property," said Eli.

His father had never once asked to see him. Tutors, reports, orders. Never a face. The Tower had stood over the house every day of his life, and he had never been through one of its doors. And now a post on every world, till somebody dragged him home.

"I'm going to the Tower," he said. "I'm going to take the chair away from him."

Teo laughed, then saw his face, and stopped.

---

When Jon next turned up Eli told him before he had his coat off. The Tower. He'd need people. He'd need to be better than he was, a lot better.

"You knew it was already up," he said. "The warrant. You let me kill him for nothing."

"I knew it would be." Jon went on with his buttons. "Up that week, up the next. Does it matter much? He'd have written it either way. They always write it up." He put his hand on Eli's shoulder, where it had been in the yard. "A great deal better," he said. "Yes. I'd think so."

"Then teach me."

"Oh, it wouldn't take. My kind of teaching, you'd come out the other end being me, and one of those is plenty." He was looking round the bunk room, at the hot plate, the one window, the wet coat on the hook. "There's a rift on Tessid. People go there to learn to listen. Teo, is there anything to eat?"

Teo said there was soup. Jon said soup was wonderful.

Eli stood there with the question still in his mouth and watched himself not ask it again. He had watched stewards do it to his tutors for years: agree with everything, and walk off with the point. He had never had it done to him.

---

He had been taught to push, hard and clean. He could break a wall from across a room. He could not tell, with his eyes shut, where the wall was.

Tessid was grey and cold and too far from anything to matter, apart from the wound in the ground: three miles across, the rock gone to glass, the air tasting like a struck bell. The well companies pumped pool out of the glass like oil, five of them along the rim, and none of them had ever quite won. They had been at it longer than anyone in Rimtown had been alive. Somebody had built a temple on the near edge, and somebody else had shelled it, and somebody had built it again.

Soldiers came off the transports in amplifier rigs that let an ordinary man reach three times as far as he should. On his second day Eli watched one burn out. He was perhaps nineteen, holding a line on the north wells, and he pushed through his rig twenty seconds longer than his body could carry. His face went grey, then slack. He sat down in the mud with blood coming out of his ears and eyes. Two company men came with a stretcher and a cold box, and the eyes and the kidneys were in the box before they had him off the field. The rig was off him before he was cold and buckled onto the next boy in line. Eli watched the new boy's hands doing up the straps. They were steady.

"Burnout," said the woman who sold Eli noodles that night. "Rig's fine. It's the meat that goes."

At the end of her counter two rig boys with their straps still on were arguing whether the Hand could take a dragon.

"He doesn't sleep," one said. "Carrier-born. Stands by the chair all night, every night, waiting to be told."

"Told what?"

"Anything."

The master lived in the shelled temple. He was old and spare, and at one time or another he had trained rig soldiers for every company on the rim. He watched Eli come up the broken steps and then looked at his hands.

"Who taught you?"

The three best tutors in the Empire, Eli told him, and a duelling master from the dragon schools.

"They taught you to shout," said the master. "Sit."

---

He kept Eli five months and never once let him push.

Every morning the master sat cross-legged on the cold floor of the temple with his eyes shut, and Eli sat across from him and did the same. Teo had found work on the company docks patching flyers and came up the steps at night with whatever there was to eat. Then the master blindfolded Eli in the middle of the room and told him to find the one moth in it, and when Eli couldn't, he left him sitting until he could.

On the eighth day, with the cloth over his eyes, he heard an engine scream on the practice field below the steps, and boys shouting, and he pulled the cloth off. One of the rig students had pushed and lost hold of it. A loader twice the height of a man was going over off its tracks toward the line of them, slowly, the way heavy things go.

The master didn't get up. He looked at it from where he sat in the temple door. The loader stopped leaning. It came upright and settled back onto its tracks as gently as a cup set down, and the boys stood under it with their mouths open.

The master rubbed his eyes for a long time. When he took his hand away there was blood on his lip, and he wiped it off with his thumb before any of the boys looked up the steps. Eli was looking.

"Anyone can lift," the master said to him. Nobody had asked. "Everyone out here who could lift is dead. Learn to listen." He nodded at the cloth in Eli's hand. "Put that back on."

It took eleven days. When it came it was nothing like pushing, only a small dry flutter up near the broken roof, and Eli was so surprised that he lost it again and had to sit most of another afternoon to get it back. After that the master had him lift a cup of water without spilling it, and then lift the water without the cup.

Nobody on the rim knew whose son he was, and the master never asked. Eli slept on the temple floor in his coat and woke before light without anyone calling him. The glass outside went from black to grey to a colour like weak tea, and the company guns started up along the rim at the same hour every day, far off, almost friendly. Some mornings, sitting in the cold with his eyes shut, he forgot for an hour at a time what he had come there for. He didn't tell Teo. He didn't tell anyone.

"You've the strength of an army," the master said, "and the hands of a man in mittens. One day it'll kill you."

In the fifth month Eli threaded a needle across the room without touching it, and sat looking at it, thread and all, until the master came over and took it away.

"You'll never be fine," said the master. "You started too late. But you'll be less likely to kill your friends by accident."

---

Before he left, Eli told him who he was. The Emperor's son. Where he was going, and what he meant to do there. He hadn't said it out loud to anyone since the house. He asked him to come.

The master went to the temple door and stood looking out over the glass and the far fires of the company lines.

"My master went up to that Tower," he said.

Eli waited.

"Fifty years ago. Best I ever saw. Said he'd go and see who sat in the chair." He didn't turn round. "Nobody ever told me how far he got."

He was quiet a while.

"No. I won't walk into that place behind a boy with a grudge. Go and die somewhere better than this."

Eli didn't argue. He let the no stand there in the doorway with the two of them until the master went back inside.

On the hull out Teo said, "We'll find someone else. There's other masters. There's got to be."

Eli didn't answer. He sat with his eyes shut and tried to find the walls of the cabin, and couldn't, and kept trying until Teo fell asleep.

## THREE · The Healer and the Statue

"If you're going to get hurt," the stretcher man had said on the rim, "get hurt near the gnome at Thirty-One. He sits up with the dying." The companies shipped him the ones they thought worth saving. Gnome healers came cheap.

The miner screamed the whole time the gnome was setting his arm, and the gnome talked the whole time, about a goat.

"Ate my whole herb garden, that goat. My brother's goat, properly; he was the clever one. The winter half the village came to me with fever, and there's me with not a leaf. I could have strangled him. Didn't, mind. I was very fond of him. Hold still."

He was small even for a gnome, round in the face, bald on top, with enormous ears and a stamped tin tag on a cord round his neck. Every gnome in the quarter wore one. The dwarves called the place the Tins for it. Holding Thirty-One was a mining district on a world with nothing but the number for a name, and in the dwarven districts gnomes worked on contracts that ran for life, and got very good at not being noticed. The clinic line went out the door and down the street. Eli stood at the back and watched him work through the afternoon, a boy with a crushed foot, then an old dwarf woman with a cough she said she'd had since the last war, and liked him more with each of them. Teo stood by the door and didn't like any of it.

The gnome took a feverish girl from her mother and sat with her behind a curtain for an hour, and brought her out again cool and asleep. She hung very loose in her mother's arms.

The mother tried to thank him. He held up a small hand.

"Thank me when she's grown," he said. "Next."

When he saw Eli and Teo he looked them over, quick and professional. "Not dying?"

"No," said Eli.

"Pity. I could use one I can fix." His eyes went to Eli's chin and away again, and he dried his hands. "So what is it you want?"

A long journey, Eli told him. A small crew. Someone who could close a wound and set a bone and not ask where either came from.

"Then you'll have to buy me," said the gnome. "The council holds my paper." He named a price. It was most of what they had.

Outside, Teo said, "There's something wrong with him."

"He's good."

"He's too good. Did you see that line? Nobody that good stays somewhere like this. Nobody. Why would you?"

"Then he'll be glad to leave," said Eli, and went back in and paid.

The gnome signed his own transfer with a flourish. Eli read it over and put it in the clinic stove.

The gnome watched it burn. Then he smiled up at Eli, very warmly.

"Well," he said. "Where would I go?"

"What do we call you?" Teo asked.

"Doc. The children call me that. Saves everyone the bother of a name." He picked up his bag. The tag stayed round his neck.

On the river bridge out of the district a troll sat in a toll box, a squat grumbling fellow a head taller than Doc and still a head short of Eli's shoulder. Doc paid him in coin and a joke about his mother. The troll didn't laugh. A mile on, Teo noticed the troll's stick was gone from the box. It was in Doc's bag, with a nail through the end.

"Always wanted one," said Doc.

---

Jon found them on the Thirty-One docks a week later with a sack of peaches, as if they'd arranged it, and nothing to say about the five months.

Eli wanted to see the elves. They were the Empire's oldest enemy, and they would be his war, if he got the chair. Jon said they were the only people left who could build a city that made you want to cry, and came along to get them in.

Seluvane stood on a world of high cold rivers, grown whole like the old giant cities but finer, and its towers leaned in toward each other at the top. Eli stood on the landing field longer than he needed to, with his bag at his feet, looking up at them.

Getting in took three hours of papers and searches. At the end of it an inquisitor in grey sat across a table from Eli with a ledger open at his elbow.

"Whose are you?" he said.

"My own," said Eli, and smiled.

The inquisitor didn't write anything down. He asked where Eli had been born, and Eli gave him a town that existed. He asked what Eli carried, and why, and how long, and who would answer for him, and which of his companions he would leave behind if he had to. Eleven questions. Eli tried charm on the first few, and then he tried being exact, dates and tonnages and the names of docks, and then he tried saying as little as a man could and still be answering. The inquisitor watched his eyes through all of it.

Then he went back to the beginning.

"Whose are you?"

The same eleven, in the same order, in the same voice. Then a third time. By the third Eli could hear himself giving the same answers, and hear how they sounded the third time.

At the end Jon spoke to the inquisitor in elvish and showed him a token on a chain. The inquisitor turned the token over, then held out his hand and kept it out until Jon took the chain off his neck and gave it to him. He put it in his coat. Then he stamped the papers, three days and not one more, with a red bar across the corner, and wrote a line in the ledger beside each of their names.

"I helped the king once, when he was a boy," Jon said, inside. He touched his collar where the chain had been. "That was my only one. Try not to steal anything."

"Who was he?" said Teo.

"Inquisition. The king's. They like to know where everyone is."

A grey coat was behind them in the market that afternoon, and another at the end of their street that night, and it was never the same coat twice.

---

On the second day they crossed a square where an elf knelt on the stones with his hands bound behind him and his mouth sewn shut with silver wire. People walked past without looking. A woman stood at the edge of the square in good clothes with her hands folded, not looking at him either. She was his wife, Jon said later. She was required to come every morning and stand there until dark.

"What did he do?" Teo whispered.

"Disagreed," said Jon. "Out loud."

Teo had his knife out. He'd gone two steps toward the man before Eli caught his arm above the wrist and held it.

"Three days," Eli said. "Not one more."

Teo looked down at the hand on his arm. He looked at it until Eli let go, and then he put the knife away, and after that he walked a little apart.

Nobody jostled in the streets. Nobody said excuse me in a doorway. Crowds parted and closed round each other without a word or a glance. The king's name was on the arches and on the coins and said quietly in the markets: Mika.

A young archivist had been sent to be helpful. Teo asked her where you could get something to eat that wasn't leaves. She told them about the dark elves instead, in two sentences, as though that had been the question. Born in the prison realm with the soul thieves and the traitors, and came out of it full of hate. Not really elves any more.

On the last afternoon a grey coat stood across the end of their street, and the way round went through a long square with a statue in the middle of it.

An elven woman, cut in white stone at twice life size. She stood with one hand raised and the fingers a little curled, about to close them on something only she could see. Fresh flowers were heaped at her feet, and elves stood round her in silence. The square was very still. The water in the gutters ran quieter there, and the light lay on the stone without moving, and for the first time in three days Eli couldn't see a grey coat anywhere.

"Lirael," said Jon. "Hero of her people. The king's sister. A long time ago."

At the foot of the statue an old elven woman was crying without a sound. Teo crouched beside her and asked in his bad elvish if she'd lost someone.

"Yes," she said.

"Who?"

She looked at him.

"I don't know," she said. "I never know. None of us do." She turned back to the statue. "We come here because it seems the place to bring it."

The others walked on. Eli turned to say something to Jon, and Jon wasn't beside him.

He was standing in front of the statue with his hands in his coat pockets, looking up into the stone face. He stood there long enough that the old woman looked up at him, and Eli started to say his name.

Then Jon turned round.

"Dinner," he said. It came out hoarse. He cleared his throat and said it again. "Come on."
