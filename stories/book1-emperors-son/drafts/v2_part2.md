## FOUR · The Door

Eleven cans. He knocked each one with a knuckle, and seven of them rang.

Day eleven. Jon had said six. "Dead moon. Nobody goes there. Wait for me." He had said it over that dinner in Seluvane with his mouth full, and paid a hauler to drop them in the crater with the flyer in its hold, and the hauler had gone on, and the flyer couldn't cross a system.

The grit was black and fine as soot. It was in the bread and between his back teeth, and when he blew his nose it came out black. Doc had found nothing to eat here and said so daily.

On the far rim sat a squat grey block with its lights on all night.

"Nobody, he said," said Teo.

They had put the glass on it the first morning and seen the coats. Their names were in a grey coat's ledger with a line beside each, and for ten days they had kept the flyer under the lip of the crater wall and gone no further than the wing to piss.

That evening a ship came down. Low, black, dwarven. It sat on the crater floor ticking. The station opened, and grey coats walked a line out across the floor toward it: forty or so, elves, chained wrist to wrist, heads down, the wrists under the iron gone to wet meat. At the ramp a dwarf in a good coat counted them. He thumbed down each lower eyelid and looked in each mouth, and dragged a grease pencil across each forehead as it came by. A ring, a cross, a line. One woman jerked her head away from the pencil. He took her by the jaw and did it again, straighter.

"What's the ring for?" said Teo. "Eli. What's he— those are elves. That's elves selling—"

Coin was counted out on a crate. A coat picked one piece off the stack and held it up to the station light and turned it, and Eli had the glass on his hand. The empty oval.

"It's theirs," Teo said. "It's an elf thing. There's water on that hull, fine, I know there is, there's also fourteen coats and it isn't ours, it's not even—"

Eli was already going down the wall.

He didn't sweep. He found the guns on the dwarven hull one small hard thing at a time, and closed his grip on each barrel and bent it. Four along the flanks. Two by the ramp. He was feeling along the belly for more when one he had missed opened up from underneath and walked across the floor and through the end of the line.

He had it. He crushed it. Nine.

They went down together because the chain went down. The gun had not left them the shape of people. It had left one heap with a chain running in at one side and out at the other, steaming in the cold, and at the near end the chain was still jerking. A tenth. A boy with a ring on his forehead, fastened by one wrist and buried to the waist in the others, pulling.

Then everything. A shot off the rock by his head. Teo's rifle on the rim, twice. Guns torn out of grey hands, some of them. Doc coming down the wall behind him, puffing, and a crewman off the ramp with a cutting bar, and Doc stepping aside, and the bar going into the grit, and the troll's stick across the man's knees, nail first, and again. The dwarf in the good coat face down on his own ramp under Eli's grip, saying something into the metal. Two coats got back inside the station. The door sealed behind them and a red light came on over it.

They broke the chains. Nobody thanked them. Most ran, out across the floor and into the dark under the far wall. Eli dug the boy out by the armpits. The shackle took a cuff of skin off with it, and the boy looked at his wrist, and then wiped both hands down his thighs, twice, and they were no cleaner.

Three didn't run. The boy, a woman, an old man with no teeth. They walked out to a place in the middle of the floor where the air trembled and stood facing it.

---

The old man walked at it. He went in at the tremble and came out of it six paces on, and stopped, and looked behind him. He did that three times.

"I can hear my goats," he said. It was elvish, older than the court's, and broad.

"Where's he trying to go?" said Teo.

"Home," the woman said. Eli asked her two other ways and got home both times.

"Can it be opened?"

"It doesn't open," said the old man. "It takes."

Eli put out his hands. The air there had a taste, a struck bell, the glass at Tessid. He felt along it and found a seam finer than a needle's eye, and got a grip in it, and pulled in a direction that wasn't there. It pulled back. His nose started.

A slit of daylight stood on the black grit. Tall as a man, thin as a wire, yellow. Warm air came through it onto his face: dung smoke, something green, wet earth. A fly came through and went round his head.

He let go to wipe his lip. It shut on the fly. Half of it lay on the grit with the legs going.

"It's a well-mouth," said Teo. "You hold the lip."

Eli opened it again and walked at it, and it thinned as he came. By the time he could have touched it, it was a hair. He backed off and it widened. He tried twice more and bled down his shirt.

"Oh, I couldn't," said Doc, before anyone had asked. "I can barely knock a hat off." He had the empty cans, two in each fist. He had picked them up already.

Teo went up the wall. He came back down with the rig over one shoulder, grey at the hump, the sealant shining on it.

"No," said Eli.

"It holds."

"I watched one of those kill a boy in twenty seconds. No. Doc can stand here and—"

Teo's eyes were on Doc. He said it low. "I'm not leaving him on the door."

Doc was showing the boy how the lid of a can screwed on.

Teo buckled the cage across his chest. He reached behind and set the stalk to the back of his neck and pushed, and it went in. His knees went. He knelt in the grit with a line of spit swinging from his chin, and then his arms came up, and the slit stood open on its own, a hand wide, a shoulder wide, dragging a little to the left. It held.

"An hour," Eli said. "Count. Out loud, so I can hear you're there."

"To what?"

"Six hundred. Then again."

"One," said Teo. "Two."

"He'll cook," said the dwarf in the good coat. They had chained him to his own ramp strut. He said it in trade talk and went back to working at his wrists.

The old man went through first.

---

Sun.

It lay on his face and he stood in it with his eyes shut and eleven days of grit in his teeth, and for a few breaths there was nothing else. He was warm. He was nobody's. Then goats, somewhere. Thorn, terraces cut into a hill, droppings on the track and some of them fresh. Behind him a slit of night hung in the path at noon with stars in it, and out of it, thin, came Teo. Forty-one. Forty-two.

The road went down past three cairns with a goat skull on each, horns and all. The three walked faster than he could. First houses. Elves, every one, and the best-fed people he had seen since the house: round arms, good teeth, a fat baby asleep on a step with its fist in its mouth. They looked at a human and a gnome coming down their road, and looked at each other, and went on shelling beans.

A door opened for the woman. A hand took her in by the sleeve, and a bar went across inside.

The big house stood at the top of the street. Along its porch a row of people sat on a bench in the sun, washed, combed, in clean shirts, hands in their laps. Their eyes were open. A fly walked across the eye of the nearest, a man of forty or so, and he let it. A girl on a stool beside him fanned it off with her hand, and it came back, and she fanned it off. Under the soap there was another smell. Where his shirt had ridden up, his hip was open in a hole the size of a palm, packed with clean lint.

Doc had stopped at the rail. "Kept very clean," he said. "Somebody here knows their work."

A man came down the steps with a knotted cord. He ran the knots through his fingers at the sight of the old man, and his lips moved.

"You were counted gone," he said. "Your street paid in your place. Now it's one over." He looked at the boy. "That one's got no street. He ran from his turn."

Eli began. A mistake, and nobody's fault; the man had been taken, he had not gone; surely the house could carry one head over to the next count. The tally-man heard all of it politely. Eli put seals on the rail, a stack of five.

"The crews bring us those," the tally-man said. "We've a crock of them."

So Eli let the air go hard under the porch step, and the step cracked across. Nothing moved on the bench. The girl with the fan stood up off her stool with both hands over her mouth.

"Stop." The old man had him by the wrist. His hand was dry, and it shook. "You'll put my daughter's street over."

He scraped the black off his feet on the broken step, one and then the other, and walked himself up, and in.

A young man came to the house door. Down in the street a man white to the elbows with flour took off his cap and held it against his chest. The young man had his face, the same face with twenty years off it. He glanced at Eli. Then he looked at Doc, and went on looking, and Doc dipped his head. The young man went in.

They filled the cans at the trough. A woman came out of a doorway and pushed a loaf under Eli's arm. She brushed at the black on his sleeve and looked at her fingers.

"Go before the count," she said.

The boy with the ring followed them out of the village.

---

Up past the third cairn, with Doc talking about the water, how sweet it was, how you could taste the hill in it. Eli had the bread under his arm and a can in each hand, and he was working out what to tell Teo about the hour.

They were walking downhill toward roofs.

Nobody had turned. The thorn was on the same hand. It was the same village, the bottom end of it; the big house stood small at the far top of the street. The sun was lower. He stopped and listened for the counting, and got goats.

Below the last houses lay a field of slag, and on it a human transit hull ticking as it cooled, with the seal on its flank taller than a door. Cold boxes were going up the ramp with the big house's mark burned into the lids. They dripped. Men shouted to each other across the ramp in his own accent, the capital's, the stable yard's, and something in his chest got up and went toward it before he could stop it.

Beside the slag was a pit, a ring of sand cut a man's height into the ground. Crews stood round the rim with seals in their fists. The sand had gone to clots, and there were teeth in it. A crewman was pissing down the wall. In the pit stood a big farmhand, soft in the belly, shaking his head up at somebody, and a thin woman in rags tied over rags. A pole prodded him forward. She went in close and laid a palm on his chest, and he folded up and sat with his legs out and was sick down himself. The rim groaned. Somebody threw down seals and a loaf. She picked each seal out of the sand and bit one. Then she squatted against the wall and ate with her arm round the bread.

She looked up at the rim once. A gnome, a human with no crew patch, a boy with a ring on his forehead. She went on eating.

The purser stood at the foot of the ramp with a slate. Eli asked passage for three and said he could pay.

"We count what we carry." He licked his thumb and tried the boy's forehead with it. "Whose mark's that?" Then he was looking at Eli's chin. He turned round. In the ramp-way behind him a sheet was pinned up among the watch lists. RECOVER IMPERIAL PROPERTY. Return what you took.

"Well now," said the purser. "Gnome walks. That one goes in the hold, he's pencilled, somebody's paid for him or somebody will. And you home, sir. In a cabin. Door on it and everything." He put two fingers in his mouth and whistled.

One reach, and every man on that rim would be telling it at the next port. Eli kept his hands down. They backed out through the betting crowd, a fist in his collar, onion breath, a seal dropped and trodden on, and Doc's stick found an ankle with the nail end and somebody went down swearing, and they ran.

The crewmen followed as far as the last houses and stopped there, leaning on a wall, laughing. Nobody chased anyone up the hill road.

"See you at supper!" one of them shouted. "Sir!"

An old woman on the last doorstep had a lap full of beans. "Hill woman's gone up again," she said, into the house behind her. "She'll be down by dark."

The road ahead of them was empty.

---

She was sitting on the third cairn beside the goat skull, eating. The thin woman from the pit.

"You won't get there," the hill woman said. It was the crews' talk, learned by ear.

She had a short hooked knife, and she cut the bread toward her thumb.

"Past here you come down in the village. Top end, or the bottom. I go up each tenth day, with bread. Thirty years." She found grit in her mouth and took it out and looked at it. "Some don't come down. Not many. I used to count them."

"We came in up there," said Eli.

She stopped chewing.

"From where."

"Outside."

"It's open."

"Someone's holding it."

"How long."

"I said an hour."

She looked at the place in the sky where the sun had been. She tied the bread into her rags and got down off the cairn and stood in the road behind them.

"Walk."

They walked. Thorn, the track, goat. Roofs, below them. The top end this time, and the bench on the porch empty, taken in for the night.

"Like that," she said.

Eli put the cans down. He tore the hem off his shirt and tied it over his eyes.

Dark. The inside of the cloth, his own breath coming back off it. Doc was saying something about supper. He stood in the road until Doc ran out, and then longer. A struck bell, faint, well to the left of where the road said it ought to be. Under it a thread of cold with no smell in it at all. Under that, a voice. Three thousand and six. Three thousand and four. Three thousand. Nine.

"Hold my coat," he said. "Don't look where you're going."

A fist went into the back of his coat. "The lad's got mine," said Doc. "She won't take his hand, mind. She's got him by the shirt. Eyes shut, the pair of them."

"I'm not led," said the hill woman.

He walked at the cold. The road kept going downhill under his feet, and he let his feet say so and followed his tongue. Thorn dragged across his shins where there had been no thorn.

"I can see the houses," the boy said. He sounded pleased. "There's the lamps. It's just there, look, it's—"

"Oh," said Doc.

Bare feet came past on the left, a pace and a half, quick. Doc grunted as his collar was taken. The feet dropped back into step.

Eli stopped.

"It's just here!" Further off. Downhill. "I can see our—"

Ahead the count skipped. It stopped. He stood in the road with the cloth on his face. Nothing in front of him. Behind him a cheerful voice, getting smaller. Then, cracked:

"One."

He walked on.

---

Cold on his face. Grit under his boots.

"Whose are you?"

It wasn't Teo's voice. He pulled the cloth off.

Grey light along the rim. Teo was on his knees at the slit with both arms out, like a man holding a door against wind. The hump of the rig had gone black, and a little smoke stood off it, and it smelled of a kitchen. He had bled from both eyes and it had dried, and he had bled again over the dry. His lip was bitten through. His trousers were frozen stiff to the knee. He was still counting, without numbers now, only the beat of it, a grunt and a grunt.

A grey coat stood over him with one hand on the back of his neck, on the stalk, and a gun in the other. "Hold it, boy," he said. A second coat had chains laid out along the grit in a row, every shackle open. Past them lay the nine. Flies had been coming through from the warm side all night, and the heap crawled.

Doc came out blinking, a can in each hand. "Ah," he said.

Then her, with her eyes shut. She opened them and was already moving: a palm on a chest, fingertips at a throat. She stepped over Teo's legs to get to the second one. The coats folded, one of them across his own chains.

Eli got his hands under Teo's arms. "Let go. I've got you. Teo. Let go."

He couldn't. His grip had cramped shut round the thing. Eli had to go in after it and find it by feel and open it for him, a finger at a time, and Teo made a noise for each finger.

The slit shut with a sound like a cough.

Then the tremble went out of the air. Eli felt for the seam. No seam. No taste. It had healed over thick, like the ridge across his own palm.

The hill woman laid her hand flat on the air where it had been. She stood like that. Then her head came round toward one part of the sky, up and off to the left, as if somebody there had spoken, and stayed.

Doc cut the rig off. The stalk had cooked in, and he had to work it out of the neck with the small knife, and the hump came away from Teo's spine in strips, and the strips had Teo on them. Teo screamed into the grit. His left ear was full to the brim, and black. Eli said his name into it three times.

"Other side," said Doc.

Eli went round.

"Did I— how long was— I lost count. I lost it. I started again, was that all right?"

"Yes."

She was still standing at the place.

"There were people in there," Eli said to her.

She looked at him. "There are."

"I'll want one of those cans," said Doc. "For his back. The clean one, if you would."

---

They took the dwarven ship. Doc read the name off its flank in dwarvish, *Ledger Closed*, and said it was a very hopeful thing to call a ship. The hold had drains in the deck and shackles along the benches and a tin of pencil stubs, and when Eli sat down there was a milk tooth in the seam of the bench.

The dwarf sat on the grit in his good coat with his wrists rubbed raw. He studied each of their faces in turn and took his time over it. At hers he frowned.

"That one's not on my sheet," he said.

"Leave him a water ration," said Doc. "Their supply boat's eight days, he tells me." Over the station door the light was still red.

"He's seen our faces," said Teo. He was on his front along a bench with his back bare and wet.

Eli looked at his own hand.

"No water."

"That's slower," said Doc mildly.

"No water."

The hill woman sat on the ramp with two knives across her knees. She looked at Eli then, properly, for the first time.

"What's your name?" he said.

"When you've earned it."

She hadn't asked where the ship was going.

Teo flew it by feel, leaning forward off the seat back, with his teeth together and his right ear turned to the engines and both hands shaking on the yoke. The ones who had run were still out under the far wall. The station had the only water on that moon, and the station was shut.

Two days out the stores were a ration short. Eli counted again. Down the corridor Doc was dressing Teo's back and talking about goats, and Teo was telling him to shut up about the goats, and Doc was telling him this was a different goat. Eli stood outside the galley until the dressing was done. Then he went back to the hold.

She slept in the galley, sitting up, with a knife in her fist and a bench shackle wedged in the hatch track so it couldn't close.

At the first port she walked down the ramp and into the crowd in her rags, barefoot, without a word to anyone.

"Good," said Teo.

That night the shackle was still in the track. Eli stepped over it and left it there.

---

On the third day in port he went past the legal exchange. The same white front as on every station, the same drain. She was standing at the price board. Eyes, kidneys, skin by the hand's width. ELF LIVER ENQUIRE WITHIN. She read with her lips moving and one finger up. She had her letters off crew crates: THIS WAY UP, she knew. LIVER was taking her a while. Behind the glass the cases stood with breath running down the insides of the lids, and at the far end of the concourse a grey coat was buying a pie.

He walked on.

That night she was in the galley chair. He hadn't heard her come aboard.

"Whose is the oval."

"The man in the Tower's."

"Where does this go."

"There. In the end."

She ate what was in front of her, all of it. "I'll ride," she said. Then, chewing: "I'm not yours."

It took her four months to stop sleeping with a knife in her hand. She ate fast, with her arm round her plate. She couldn't stand a shut door, or to be thanked, so the shackle stayed in the track and they stopped thanking her. Eli sat up late in the galley most nights with the tea going cold and the drive coming up through the deck into his feet. Mostly she said nothing, and he sat there anyway.

One good night she talked for most of an hour, about a place on the hill where the water came out of the ground warm, and you could sleep in it if you tied yourself to a root. When she got up he said, "Thank you."

He heard it on its way out. She stopped in the hatch. Nine nights the chair was empty, and he drank his tea across from it. On the tenth she was in it.

"Down there you feel your street," she said, some night after. "The near ones. Who's up. Who's gone in the house." She tipped her chin at a bulkhead. "Out here there's that. All the— always. At the edge." She hunted for the word and didn't get it. "It doesn't go out."

Teo came in for water with his good ear toward her, and asked did the dark elf want the last of the tea or could he have it.

"There's a sun down there," she said to her plate. "It's a place. They named it hell. Then they named us after it."

Teo had seen a slit of it, for one night. He filled his cup and went out, and he didn't use the word again.

"My street paid in children," she said, another night. "Children are small. Nobody misses them long." She went on eating. "The crews say some get grown into things. What do your people do with them."

"I don't know," Eli said.

She finished the plate.

---

They got Jon back in the second month, off a port quay, grey in the face, with one small bag and no peaches. Eli stood in the bunk-room door and stayed in it.

"Six days," he said.

"I was kept."

"By who."

"Elves." Jon put the bag down. "Five weeks of it nobody's business."

"Nobody goes there, you said."

"Nobody does."

"You knew what it was."

"The Inquisition catches the ones they call dark elves as they come out, and the dwarves move them on. Organs, mostly. An elf liver's meant to keep you young. It doesn't, but try telling a rich man. The strong ones go to the pits on the inner worlds; they fit them with claws. The pretty ones go to houses, to stand in halls. Some go for crew, to anyone who wants hands that last. Some get grown into things." He rubbed his eyes. "Is there any of that salt left? The grey sort. I've thought about that salt."

Eli didn't fetch it.

"Did you know she was in there?"

Jon looked past him, down the corridor, at the galley hatch with the shackle in its track.

"There's always someone."

"You don't pick where we wait again."

"All right."

"When we get there. What do we do?"

"Get there," said Jon. He picked up his bag and stood holding it until Eli moved.

A night later Eli went down to the hold in his socks. She was in the middle of the empty deck with one hand raised, the fingers a little curled, and she closed them slowly. The air in front of her folded in on itself with a small flat sound, a book being shut. A cargo strap a yard away went into it and was gone.

She saw him and dropped the hand.

"Lirael's Collapse," she said. "Down there you learn her name before your own. Everyone wants that one." She looked at where the strap had been. "It's the one thing of theirs we took."

Jon was in the doorway behind Eli.

"That's a hard one to learn," he said. "Good for you."

Her face shut.

He went off down the corridor whistling. Halfway along he lost the tune, and stopped, and started it again from the top.

## FIVE · The Queen and the Giant

Eli put the ship down in the dry riverbed between the two camps and walked out alone under both sets of guns.

Jon had said there was a well. There was a well. There was also an elven seedship lying across the dunes with its back broken, and a colony so long cut off that it had forgotten anyone else was out there, and the dead of both. The elven dead lay under sheets, and the sheets moved with flies. The colonists' lay under nothing and had dried to leather, lips shrunk back off the teeth. The colonists called their world the Dry. Invaders, they said. Vermin, said the elves.

Jon stood at the top of the ramp in his coat in the heat, looking at the seedship.

This much the house had taught him. He found the colony's grandmothers and the elven captain's second and sat them across a folding table in the sun, and let each side describe the other until it ran out of words. On the second day the elves walked out. On the fourth a grandmother spat on the table, and for two days Eli sat at it alone, with the spit dried to a ring in front of him and a jug of water at his elbow that he didn't touch. Both camps could see the jug.

On the sixth day they were back, and a colonist boy shot an elf at the water line.

Eli walked the boy to the elven camp himself. He was perhaps fourteen, and he didn't struggle. He kept asking where they were going, though he could see. They knelt him at the water line, and the captain's second cut his throat, and two elves held him up by the ankles so that it went into the sand and not the water. One of his sandals came off in an elf's hand. The elf set it down and took hold of the bare ankle. It made more noise than Eli had expected, and for longer, and the heel in the elf's hand kept trying to walk.

There was sand in his collar. It had worked down under his shirt to the small of his back, and for most of it that was what he felt.

One of the grandmothers was the boy's. She came back to the table the next morning and sat in her place, and she didn't speak to Eli again. By the ninth day there was a line in the riverbed that neither side crossed, and three days with nobody dead.

---

On the twelfth day an elven warship came down across the line.

It dropped its ramp on the colony's bank. Armoured elves came off it with rods and a cord and began pacing out a field, through the colonists' dead. One of them trod on a chest, and it gave under him like a basket. He moved his rod a foot to the left.

A woman walked down the ramp behind them. She was taller than any elf Eli had met. Her sword hung at her hip in a pale ribbed sheath, and the sheath shifted against her leg as she came, a little out of time with her. It sweated. It had left a dark patch on the plate of her thigh.

She walked through the folding table. It went over, and the jug with it, and the sand had the water before the jug stopped rolling. The grandmothers got out of her road. Going by, the sheath smelled like the inside of a mouth.

"They call her the Queen," Jon said quietly. "Like on the board."

Eli put himself on the line in front of her. He had the first words out. She put him on the ground with one hand, somewhere near his collarbone, without drawing, and stepped over him.

"Four hundred of your king's people are drinking from that well," he said, from the ground, with sand in his mouth. "It's the colony's. Burn the colony and those old women will have a dead goat down it before your first barrel's up."

She stopped. She stood with her back to him and weighed it.

"A week," she said. "To water." Then, with no more interest: "I have a second order. I am to travel on the human's hull, behind him, to the Tower. My king has sworn to kill the man in it. So has every elf alive. In all the ages he has sat there, not one of us has passed its door."

"No," said Eli. He was on one knee.

"It was not asked of you."

That night Teo warmed the drive to lift without her. They heard her ship's guns come round, a long grinding through the hull, and then the forward screen was eight black mouths. Teo sat with his hands on the yoke.

"Well, that's that," he said, and shut it down.

At first light Eli walked across and made the only trade there was. She came aboard now. Her ship lifted with the seedship, in a week, and the line stood. He asked for her word on it.

"I do not give my word to humans," she said. "My orders for the field are delayed."

She passed Jon at the foot of the ramp and didn't slow.

"They tell me you answered well."

"I always do," said Jon.

She took the best bunk. It had been Doc's. Doc moved his bag out without a word, and then with several.

---

In the galley Eli asked Jon about the sword.

"Grown," said Jon. He was peeling something with a small knife. "When they catch one of hers outside, the ones they call hell-born, they grow a blade out of her. It takes about a year. She's alive for most of it; it holds better that way." He thought about it. "It's a very good sword."

The hill woman was in the hatch. Eli turned in his chair, and the hatch was empty.

Her cabin door was shut. He had not seen it shut before.

She didn't come out for two days. On the second he sat down on the deck in the corridor with his back to the bulkhead beside her door and said nothing. His leg went dead under him. He had counted the rivets in the opposite wall twice and got different numbers when her voice came through, low down, from the floor on the other side.

"I can feel her," she said. "Close. Loud."

The deck hummed.

"From the sword, nothing. There's nobody there."

He waited.

"I used to count the ones who didn't come down. I thought they'd done it."

There wasn't any more.

He found the Queen at dusk at the top of the ramp, watching the seedship elves fill barrels under the line. He had chosen the words on the way up the corridor, and he said them carefully. The crew decks were narrow. If she would keep the blade aft when she could, off them—

"No," said the Queen. She was already walking, through the place where he stood, and he got out of it.

On the third day the hill woman came out. Her hands weren't steady. In the corridor she passed the Queen with her back flat to the bulkhead, and she watched the sheath the whole way by. The Queen walked as if the corridor were empty. After that everyone else learned to wait until one of them had gone through.

---

The ship sat in the riverbed another week while the seedship took on water, a barrel at a time, with the grandmothers counting every barrel.

The Queen sparred with him in the hold every day and put him on the deck every day, without malice or effort, and walked off while he was still getting up. He came to know the deck by taste: oil, and other people's boots. She called him *the human*. "The human is bleeding on the floor," she told Doc once, on her way out.

"Why her?" Eli asked Jon one night, when his ribs hurt too much to sleep.

"Mika sent her. Mika's very much in charge." Jon patted his arm. "I'm glad he did, though. She's better than you, and she hates you. You'll come on twice as fast."

---

"I owe a call at Hurrunaal," Jon said. "One night. A dwarven hull can get a hauler's berth there, nobody looks twice. Wait on the ship. He doesn't lend."

"Lend what?"

"Anything."

The smell reached them in orbit. The haulers vented before they unloaded, and it came in through the scrubbers, ammonia, a pen. Yard control took them for a hauler and asked their count.

"Count of what?" said Teo into the band.

"Head."

Jon leaned over him. "Empty. Calling on the old man."

Eli went down in the shuttle anyway, and took the rest of them. Jon held the hatch. "Mind the step," he said.

A dozen cathedral ships lay in their cradles, each the length of a city, ribbed and pale and half-made. Feed tubes ran up into them from a ring of dwarven bulk haulers, and in the tubes orcs walked, single file, up into the hulls. None came down. In the nearest hull a rib was closing. There were hands in it still, a row of them, open. Eli watched until the shuttle turned and the window filled with rock.

The hall was so big it had weather. Cloud hung under the roof, and at the far end a thin rain was falling on the stone and running off down channels cut for it. Sitting, the giant was the height of a four-storey house. When he spoke, Eli felt it in the soles of his feet.

"Jon."

"Hello, old friend."

"I am not your friend. But I am friendly. Sit, little ones." He had not taken his eyes off Jon. "You said the spring."

"I was kept."

"You are always kept."

Doc sat. Teo sat, and turned his good ear up. Eli stayed on his feet and said his piece to a kneecap. He had made a truce between four hundred elves and a colony, with a table. An elven king's piece stood at his back. He had one ship and wanted another, against the man in the Tower, and he was prepared to talk terms.

The giant asked Jon whether it still rained on the capital at this time of year.

"Sideways," said Jon. "You'd hate it."

"And why do you bring me a human who wants a ship?"

"Because I'm the Emperor's son," Eli said.

Behind him the Queen's armour had creaked with her breathing all the way down the hall. It stopped.

The giant's head came down. It took some time, and it turned the cloud.

"Do you know what those are made of?" he said. "Once, our own dead. Families. A grandmother in the keel, her sons in the ribs. There are not enough of us left to die, so now we buy. The dwarves bring them by the hauler-load, and they walk in, and the hull closes on them, and they stay. That is what makes it a ship and not a shell. Every hull that leaves here goes to a dragon. We build them for the dragons, so the dragons will kill each other. You humans are their favourite pupils."

The rain went on at the far end.

"We had laws once, and a peace, and dragons to carry them for us. On betrayal day one of them walked into our capital and breathed it to ash. Our emperor took his city, every body and every soul in it, and made a shield of it. What was left is the Tower. Your people had it from the dragons, and your father built his seat in it. Your father's house. Do not call it a building. The souls of that day are in its walls, all of them, run together."

Eli hadn't called it anything. Teo was staring at Jon.

"A hostage is worth what the other side would pay," the giant said. "I have read your father's posters. Keep you, and we feed you. Let you go, and we lose nothing."

He leaned down until his face filled the air in front of Eli. His breath was cold and smelled of wet stone.

"And you would like me to send giants to knock on its door. No."

A servant showed them out by a side door, past a stack of crated cabbages. Jon stayed. The great doors shut on him and the giant together.

---

The Queen came for him that night in the dark corridor outside the hall.

He didn't see the first cut. Cold. A line of it, across his ribs. The second he felt coming, the air moving ahead of the blade, and he put hard air in its way. The blade flexed against the wall and slid round it. He got his grip on her wrist. She broke it with a twist he didn't follow. Her knee in his chest. The floor. Her foot on his throat, and the pale edge an inch from his eye, wet, with a smell coming off it.

"His son," she said. Her voice was level. "My king sent me to guard the son of the man who—"

She stopped. The blade trembled. He had not seen it do that.

"He knew. My king knew, and sent me anyway, and did not tell me."

Eli rolled his eyes down the corridor for Jon. Empty. At the end of it the hall doors were shut.

"Then ask him." It came out a croak round her foot. "The relay's on the ship. If he says kill me, you've lost a day."

She stood on his throat and considered it. Then the blade moved, fast, and opened his cheek from ear to jaw, and she stepped back and sheathed it, and the sheath closed over it like a mouth.

"So you remember who you are," she said.

He lay where he was. His cheek hung. When he breathed in he could feel the air on his back teeth, from the side.

---

Doc stitched him on the ship, the ribs and then the cheek, with nothing for it, and told him it would scar. He didn't make a joke. He didn't mention a goat. Eli lay with a strap between his teeth and waited for the goat the whole time, and it didn't come.

Jon came back near dawn and saw the stitches and asked what had happened. When Eli told him he said, "Ah." He lifted a hand toward Eli's face. Then he put it in his coat pocket.

"The yard wants the berth back by morning," he said to Teo. "Can we?"

Teo said they could.

"What did you talk about," said Eli. It hurt to say. "All night."

"Old times."

"Whose?"

"His. Mostly." Jon went forward to see about the berth.

Eli put his fingers on the stitches and went down them, ear to jaw. Twenty-six. Doc had done them very small. A side door, and twenty-six.

She sent the message from the relay before they were out of the system. The reply came in eleven hours. Eli never saw it. He saw her read it. Then she went down to the hold and put her fist through the bulkhead, twice, and came back up with her knuckles open to the bone and her face exactly as it had been.

"Continue," she said. Her mouth hardly opened on it.

After that she stopped calling him *the human*, or anything at all. She sparred with him every day and put him on the deck as before. But she no longer walked off while he was getting up. She stood and waited until he was on his feet, and then she put him down again.
