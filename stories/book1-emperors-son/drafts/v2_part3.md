## SIX · The Strike

They were a day out from the forge world, with the stitches in Eli's cheek still pulling whenever he swallowed, when every band on the bridge went to the same picture.

A long chamber, ribbed, laid along a ship's spine. Crew strapped in at their stations, scaled and wingless and quick in the hands, and they kept glancing at one another. A demonstration, the caption said, by House Vaskhir.

In the middle of it stood a cradle. His duelling master from the dragon schools had drawn him one on a slate: a body poured its whole strength in, and the ship threw it across the dark in one line. Crews took turns and paid for it.

The ship-master was climbing into it himself.

"Master." One of the crew, half out of his straps. "The chamber is not rated for—"

"Then it will learn." He pulled the last strap tight with his teeth. "For my grandfather's grandfather, who burned in the old capital. Aim for the forge-heart."

He opened his mouth. Along the stations the scales were standing up off their arms, and steam came out from under them, and the one who had spoken was beating at his own chest with both hands. The long bones of the ship-master's neck went one after another. He held the line. Then the band was grey.

On the other screen, from orbit, the line. It went in at the forge-heart and the forge-heart was gone, and then the yards, the half-made hulls folding in their berths, and the feed tubes with everyone still walking up them. Then the crust came up in a ring and kept coming, red rock taller than mountains.

The yard wants the berth back by morning, Jon had said.

The hill woman was at Eli's shoulder. Teo was crying and hadn't wiped his face.

"The giant," said Teo. "He was in the hall."

Jon stood at the back with a cup of tea.

"His crew will have cooked," he said. "In their scales, at their stations. Ask any of them and they'd have told you they were dragons. His people were in that city when it went into the wall. Betrayal day, the old man called it. That house has kept the bill ever since."

Three days later the giants came out. Cathedral ships, hundreds of them, from yards on worlds nobody had known they still held.

A dwarven relay had the fleets on every dragon flag in reach, and every port that flew one, Earth among them. It said so again every week after. Then it went on to prices.

"So where do we," said Teo, "there's no ship now, is there, there's no giant, where do we even—"

"Back for the master," said Eli.

---

Jon got off at the rim port before the ramp was all the way down, his bag on his shoulder.

"Rig town," he said. "Ledgers. I'll find you."

It had taken them a month to get there through a sector coming apart. Three of the five companies had pulled their boys and gone to the war. Their wells stood capped along the rim, and the caps wept, a grey crust down the side of each. The glass looked bigger without the guns.

The master watched Eli come up the broken steps and stayed sitting. "You're better," he said.

Eli sat down across from him on the cold floor and folded his legs. His knees remembered it.

"You said nobody told you how far he got. Your master. I'm going up those stairs. Come and find out."

The old man scratched his neck. "That's a cheap trick," he said. "Get out of my temple."

On the steps going down he met the last two rig students coming up with their breakfast, a loaf and a can of tea between them. They had both been under the loader. They stood in against the wall until he was past.

---

"Hear that?" Teo was on his back under the drive panel with his good ear to the housing. "That whine. It's the coil, or the thing next to the coil, it wants a part, and the part's dwarven, two systems off. Four days."

"We wait," said Eli.

He found the two students at the noodle counter and asked them to come. He told them where. One of them heard him out to the end.

"What's the pay?"

He had none. That afternoon they crossed the field in company straps to a recruiter's hull, bound for a troop muster two systems on. The master came down the temple steps and crossed after them. Eli watched from his own ramp and couldn't hear any of it. The recruiter laughed once and held a pen out to the old man. The boys went up past the pair of them with their eyes on their boots. The hull lifted, and the master stood in the wash of it with his coat blown open.

The part came on the fourth night. By morning it was raining, and the ramp was half up when he came across the field with a bag on his shoulder: old leather, split at the corners, off the peg by the temple door.

"I'm not coming to your Tower," he said. "That hull's gone to the muster. You're going that way. Put me off there."

He went up past Eli. From inside: "Your drive's whining."

---

The cutter caught them over a gas giant none of them had a name for.

The grapple hit and the hold went sideways. Eli went with it, across the deck and into a stanchion ribs first, and his side gave with a small wet click that he heard from the inside.

He lay there. Breath came in pieces.

"How did they find us." Teo, from the pilot's chair.

"You ordered a part," said the master.

Eli got to the bridge along the walls. The hail light was blinking. The Queen took two paces out of the screen's line and stood with her back to the bulkhead beside it.

The screen opened on a grey coat. A narrow face he had sat across a table from in Seluvane, three times through.

"Whose are you?" said the inquisitor.

He had their faces up on a panel, the four from his gate, Jon's smiling. Under them was a sheet in a dwarvish hand with four more drawn small and exact, and beside Eli's a ring in grease pencil. He knew the pencil. One of the four was a thin face with no name under it.

"The hull," said the inquisitor, "and the hell-born you carry. She will be pardoned. You may go on."

The hill woman stood by the galley hatch with one bare foot on the shackle in its track.

"No," said Eli. It cost him the breath he had.

The inquisitor wrote in his ledger. "Then you may stay aboard." The screen went dark.

"Stay aboard and he tows us to his station," said the master. He was at the forward port, where the cable ran out to the cutter. "Cut that and he fires on a hull he's no more use for." He looked down at Eli, who was on the deck by then with both arms round his own side. Then he looked at the lifeboat hatch.

They took what they could carry, water first. The hill woman pulled the shackle out of the track and took that. The Queen put her hand on the release and pulled it.

"He will not fire," she said. "He stamped you through his own gate. He will not write that in a firing log."

Through the port Eli watched the cutter take up the slack and tow the low black hull down into the bands of the gas giant, until it was too small to be anything.

---

Six of them and one bucket, in a boat built for four dwarves. By the second day the air was wet with them. By the tenth Eli had stopped smelling the bucket and could only taste it. The master kept the water and gave it out by the mouthful. Eli couldn't lie on either side. He sat up against the hull with the Queen's armoured shoulder hard against his, for the cold, and neither of them moved it.

By the fourteenth his tongue had outgrown his mouth. It stuck to his teeth and came off them with a click. Doc's stories had stopped having ends. Teo had split his lip on nothing and kept sucking it.

On the seventeenth they shared the last cup, a mouthful each, the master holding it. It tasted of all six of them. Doc made a joke about the vintage, and then another.

On the nineteenth there was a hull on the scope.

A salvage tug, old and slow, with a crew of three. Its skipper hauled them aboard by their collars one at a time and stood them round a bucket.

"Paid to sweep here," he said, when Teo could ask. "Didn't say what for. Didn't say people, I'd have brought more— not so fast, lad, you'll only bring it up. There. Told him."

The tug sold its scrap on the muster world. The master went down the ramp there with his bag on his shoulder and into the crowd on the Legion quay.

Jon was on the dock with a bag of peaches. He said he was sorry. He held Eli by both arms and saw how he was standing and said, "Ribs," and said he was sorry about that too.

"Nineteen days," said Eli. "Where were you?"

"Elves."

"You're never there when the grey coats are."

"No," said Jon. "I'm not." He held out the bag. "Teo. How's that ear? Really, now."

Teo took one. Eli left the bag hanging there. That afternoon he put the last of his coin on passage for five in a dwarven arms hull, and when Jon asked where it was bound he said he hadn't decided.

"Then I'll go on ahead," said Jon. "Somebody has to find you a way down." He ate the peach himself.

---

An hour before the arms hull lifted, Eli went along the Legion quay for bread and found the master. Cold boxes were coming off a troop hull on a belt, dripping, and the old man was working along the stacks with his bag on his shoulder, turning each company tag to the light. He had stopped at one. Past him boys in straps stood in line at a pay table. The rig on the nearest was dark down the cage with another boy's sweat. Four along stood the student who had asked about pay. He lifted a hand, not far.

"They pay, master," he said.

The old man was sitting on a crate in the hold when the ramp came up, with the bag pushed in behind it.

They slept on the crates, and the lids were warm. FLESH RIFLE. GROWN FROM CERTIFIED CLONE STOCK. NO SOUL. FEED DAILY. The rifles twitched whenever the drive spun up, and sweated, and took grey mash through a slot in the lid every morning, and by the third night the hold smelled of a sickroom. One crate had split. The rifle in it had a fingernail in the grip, small and clean, where a thumb would go.

The Dry came up on the hull's feed in the second week. An elven fleet had cleared both banks for a landing field. No colony in the picture. The line was still there in the riverbed. He found the Queen and told her it had stood a month.

"Delayed," she said.

At the next port the low black hull sat two berths along under fresh paint, *Fair Weight*. At the gate a guard held a poster up beside Eli's face. A strip was pasted along the foot: SEEN, and a hull's name, and a crossing. The guard went from one to the other, chin and jaw.

"Pays at an imperial post," he said to the man beside him. "You know where the nearest one of them is now?"

He waved Eli on. Teo had come through behind them with a crate on his shoulder and his eyes down, and no hand had touched him.

On every world after that his poster had the face dug out to the backing and the seal left standing.

On a human world they came out of a market into a square where a street was hanging a gnome from a lamp standard. A well had gone bad and a child had died of it, and he kept the pump. He was still in his apron. He kept getting two fingers in under the rope and losing them. The crowd sang to the Emperor's god with their mouths open, under a broadcast wall with the seal where a face should be. His heels knocked on the standard. A dark line ran down the inside of one leg and dripped off his shoe.

Eli's hand was half up. Teo was watching it. He put it down.

"Poor thing," said Doc. "They always get the wrong one."

The master had turned away from the lamp. He was watching Doc, and he had gone still, with his head a little to one side.

Doc glanced up at him. "Hurt yourself?"

"No," said the master, and rubbed his eyes.

---

They crossed once by a human transit hull, because there was no other way.

At the port before it the Queen bought a collar off a stall, a plain grey band with a mark stamped in the front, and held it out. The hill woman turned it over twice. Then she buckled it on herself. It took her three goes.

"We count what we carry," said the purser.

"It is mine," said the Queen.

He wrote it down. Eli went up the ramp behind them under the Queen's baggage with his chin in his collar, and was written down as hers too.

They were put in a hold. When the hull dropped through, the hill woman went rigid. She sat with her back to the bulkhead and her eyes shut, and her hand came across the deck and shut on his.

"It's gone out," she said.

An hour in, the hull came down and sat ticking. Boots overhead, going out by the ramp. Dung smoke came in through the vents, and wet earth. Later, a long way off through the plating, a ring of men groaned all together and then laughed. Goats.

"All of them," she said. "Every street. The whole— pressing."

Across the hold the Queen sat upright with sweat on her lip.

The first finger went then, with a sound he felt in his elbow. He got his sleeve between his teeth. The second took longer. It bent, and went on bending most of an hour while the crew bet overhead, and he watched it go and breathed through his nose. By the eighth hour he couldn't have said which finger was which. His hand was a hot glove with her hand round it, and there were three hours left.

On the far side she let go. She looked at his hand, which had come up fat and dark as a blood sausage, and then at him. She got the collar off and dropped it in the slop bucket.

Doc set the fingers. At the mess table next morning Eli flexed the splint at her. It hurt. He did it again.

She told him her name. She said it low, into her plate, with nobody else at the table. Then she took her arm from round the plate to reach the salt, and left it off.

---

The dragon found them on a moon with no air, where the second arms hull had put down for ice. Eli had booked that one too.

He was out on the dust in a borrowed suit when the stars went out overhead. A quarter-mile of wing. No sound. The drive section came off the hull at the root, white, and he felt it through his boots. The dwarven crew ran for the hills. The head turned, and a line went along the hills, and there were no dwarves.

"It's young," said the master on the band. "Make it breathe. It can't do that forever. Make it waste them."

He walked out from under the hull into the open and stood there.

Eli's own breath was the loudest thing on the moon. The Queen was crossing the flat in plain sight, and then she was somewhere else on it, and the head went after her, and a canyon opened where she had been and its lip threw her. He lost her. The master had one hand up. A line came down on him and bent off into the ground behind, and a mile of moon went up in a slow fan and hung there. Blood inside the old man's plate, under the nose. Eli's had started too. It ran over his lip into his mouth and he couldn't get a hand to it.

Rocks, going up out of the hull's shadow in slow arcs. Doc on the band, high and cracked. Under the jaw a thin figure with one hand raised and the fingers curled: a fistful of scales went out of the jaw into nothing, and she was on her knees. Up. Her palm flat on the throat, and the whole head jerked aside. Not far enough. The master was down on one knee. Eli hadn't seen that happen.

"Do something!" Teo. Eli turned and found him a long way out on the open flat with a rifle from the crates jammed into his shoulder, running back. "Doc! Do something!"

"Saving myself for something special," said Doc, puffing.

White, on Eli's left. Then a noise on the band, thin, going on and on with no breath in it. He didn't know it for Teo.

The lines came slower after that, and shorter, and ragged at the edges. The dragon stood on the dust with its head low and its sides heaving.

"Now," said the master. "I can't do both."

Eli shut his eyes and reached. Scale, like feeling through a hill. Meat. The lungs, huge and hot. Then the heart, the size of the flyer and labouring, and in it one narrow place where everything went through. He closed his hand.

The head came up. It looked at him across the dust, and he saw it find out he was there. Then it fell, and the moon shook under his knees, and the dust went up over it and stayed up.

The master was on both knees with black in both ears. "That," he said, "was fine."

They found Teo against the landing strut with his left arm held out from his body. The suit had fused to it from the fingers to the elbow. Inside the hull Doc cut the sleeve away and the skin went with the cloth, whole, a glove. Afterwards Teo set off the dead hull's beacon with his right hand. Dwarven beacons ran on the open band, so that anyone could sell a rescue.

---

They lived in the hold on dwarven rations. Teo's arm went sweet on the second day. By the fourth the sweet had something sour under it, and they all breathed through their mouths.

Teo wouldn't have Doc dress it alone. He asked Eli the first night, low: would he stay, just while, just so—

"Of course," said Doc, unrolling gauze.

Eli stayed, every time. When he shut his eyes the dragon lifted its head off the dust.

On the fourth day the port went dark. A hull lay alongside the moon. Pale ribbing, curving away out of sight both ways. He knew it. One had hung over the capital every day of his life and filled the window of his old rooms. His father's.

"Not this one," said the master, from the deck. He couldn't stand. The Queen's ribs were strapped.

Eli opened the hatch himself.

They came in suited: humans, dwarves, elves, and an orc carrying the tip of the wing off the dragon outside. Their hunter. The elves wore grey collars stamped at the front. She laid her two knives down on a crate.

"Pardoned," she said.

The lord's ship was warm inside, and the walls had pores. He sat on a throne at the head of the bridge with his crew at their stations in front of him. Behind the throne, on a raised gallery, ran a ring of cradles, two dozen, most of them empty, the straps gone pale and stiff with other people's sweat. From his, Eli could see over the throne's back: folded wings, the long neck, the open deck, the stations. Her hands were shut on her straps. All four days Eli had the back of the lord's head.

The ship stayed. This lord took his dead home. For four days the hunter came aboard through the hull, a quarter-mile of wing sawn up and winched in a piece at a time, and the smell came in with it. The orc scraped scale off the deck with a blade and hummed.

---

By the fourth day he had his first sentence ready, and what they were worth.

Every head at the stations turned.

Somebody was standing on the open deck between the stations and the throne. He was taller than anything on the bridge but the lord. Behind him, far down past the stations, a seam in the hull was closing from its edges in.

"Don't reach," said the master, low, along the ring. "Any of you. That's a god."

The god looked up at the dragon and opened his mouth.

The dragon breathed first.

The great head went back and the jaw opened over the throne, and nothing came out of it that Eli could see. The air between the jaw and the god shivered once and lay still.

The god looked down at himself. His chest. His hands.

Nothing. The cradle hummed against Eli's spine. At the stations somebody coughed.

Then the orc.

He was at the back, against the hull, with the scraping blade in his fist, and he had stopped humming. He put his other hand up to his face. The face went grey under it, and slack, and old, and he burst. He was standing at his station, and then his clothes were coming down onto the deck with dust going up out of them and wet running out from underneath.

"That's not heat," said the master.

The humans next. A woman at the helm turned in her seat to see the heap, and her hair was white before she had finished turning. The man beside her stood up and got old standing. His back went. He opened his mouth to shout and his teeth came out of it. They went down at their stations a dozen together, the skin drawing in and then no skin, until there was dust in a uniform in every seat. The vents had it. It came up to the gallery warm, and Eli shut his mouth, and it came in at his nose.

The dwarves lasted. One got out of his harness and took three steps toward the throne, and his beard went white and came away on the third, and he kept walking. He didn't make the fourth.

Then the elves.

They went slowest. Every one of them turned at their stations and stared up at the gallery, at the same two cradles. Beside Eli the hill woman's hands were locked white on her straps.

"Still there," she said. "All of them. Loud."

They got old slowly enough to watch. A collar came loose round a throat as the throat shrank inside it. The Queen bent forward as far as her straps would let her and was sick between her knees, and sat up again and kept her eyes on the stations. The strap under the hill woman's right hand folded in on itself with a small flat sound and was gone. There was nothing else in reach.

The elves went down.

"Gone," said the hill woman.

The god hadn't moved. He stood on the open deck in the dust of the whole crew, with his hands at his sides, and he smiled up at the dragon.

"Your crew is dead," he said. Eli felt it through the deck. "How will you get home?"

The dragon had no answer. From the gallery Eli could see the side of the great jaw. The scales along it had gone wet, and a claw was working at the arm of the throne, in and out.

Then the god trembled.

It went through him once, top to bottom, and the smile went. He lifted a hand and turned it over, and back. The fingers shook. He pressed them into his own forearm and studied the dents they left, and pressed again, harder.

He turned and looked behind him. The heap against the hull. The humans in their seats. The dwarf on the deck, three steps short. The elves. Then his hand again, and the skin on the back of it had gone fine as paper.

It took him longer than all of them together. He got old on his feet and watched every part of it. Near the end he was on his knees, holding one wrist in the other hand. Then there was dust where he had knelt, a great drift of it, settling.

Doc had leaned out as far as his straps would go. His lips were moving.

Dust at every station.

The dragon breathed out. Then his head came round, and he went along the ring, cradle by cradle, counting what he had left.

---

At Eli he stopped. He looked at him the way you look at weather.

Eli had a first sentence ready. He used the god's.

"You'll want a crew to get home," he said, out of the straps. His mouth was full of grit. "There are six of us. I'll hold a station. He can fly anything with a seat, one hand or two. Those two can each hold one, and the old man. The gnome keeps everyone alive. Your hunter was young, and it lost to cargo. That's its own shame, and the voyage settles it. Take us as far as your house port. Your house will have hands there, bought and yours, and you won't want to feed strangers past that."

The lord's head went back round to the front.

The hill woman already had one arm out through the place where her strap had been. She did the other buckles with her teeth and her free hand, and came along the ring barefoot, and unbuckled them one at a time. The Queen. The old man. Eli last. His legs went and he got the rail. Below him the dust in the nearest seat still had shoulders, until the vent moved it.

She took the station farthest from the throne and said nothing to him for nine days.

Nine days, at dead people's stations, inside a hull grown out of dead giants. The dust was in the water and the ration tins. Eli coughed it up grey each morning and by the afternoon had breathed in more. Teo flew with one hand and talked to the helm under his breath. The Queen sat down on what was in an elven seat and left it under her. Doc said nothing for one whole day, and next morning started on a goat over the tins.

---

At the house port the lord's own hands came up the ramp with a manifest and a crate of collars. They swept each seat into a sack, tagged the sack, and ticked it off. The six were on no list. One of them looked at her throat as she went down past him with her knives, and then at his crate, and let her by.

She stopped by Eli on the quay. "Next time ask me first," she said, and went and sat on a bollard.

Jon wasn't there.

Three days on the stones, with what water they could beg. Teo's arm went bad. The fingers swelled till they stood apart, and the grey crept up past the elbow, and Doc sat him against a bollard with a strap in his teeth and cut. It came away in wet grey sheets and he laid them on the stones beside him. Gulls took them. Eli held the arm still by the wrist and breathed through his mouth.

"It won't close right," Doc said. "He'll keep it, though. Clean water, and I'd have had the lot."

On the third afternoon a house clerk came along the quay with a slate and began to take them down, by height. He had reached the Queen when Jon came through the fish stalls at a walk, grey, with no bag.

"I had the other port," Jon said. "I was sure of it."

"Why?"

Jon opened his mouth and shut it. He looked up at the hull, all the way along the ribs to where they went out of sight.

"I don't know," he said.

He gave the clerk a coin, and then a second, and the clerk rubbed six lines off the slate with his thumb. There was a hop hired, Jon said, on the far side of the fish.

---

A month on, on another freighter, the hull took a hit and the cold got into the hold where forty chipped orcs were going to the Legion yards.

Teo found them. He came back to the bunks and sat on the end of Eli's with his good hand between his knees, and it came out in pieces. They'd been told to stand in rows. They had frozen standing, in rows. There was frost in their eyelashes. By the warm bulkhead the rows leaned in as far as the chips let them, and some had got an arm round the next one.

Eli stayed in his bunk. Jon went and looked.

That evening Teo went down the passage toward the purser's hatch with the twist of cloth out of his boot. Jon was in the passage first. He sat him down on a bench under the lights and put an arm round him.

Eli heard it through the bulkhead. "The first one I saw was a carter," Jon was saying. "Frozen, as it happens. Sat up on the box with the reins in his hands, and the horse had brought him all the way home. I was sick in the ditch." Teo's breath, wet. "It doesn't stop. That's how you know you're still a person."

An hour of it. Something about a list, and his mother.

"They'll ask me where he is," Teo said. "If I go back. And I'll tell them. I know I will."

"They must be very proud of you," said Jon.

Eli came out into the passage. "You'll stay?" Jon was saying.

"Yeah."

The purser came by on his rounds. Jon kept his arm where it was and booked all seven of them through to the next system, in that hold.

"At a discount," he said. "They won't be needing it."

The purser wrote it down.

---

Eli stepped out in front of him.

"You held him for an hour. Then you sold their deaths for a fare. You talked him into staying. For what?"

Jon smiled at him.

"You didn't go and look," he said.

"The man at the fence. Nine at the crater, chained together. The old man you walked home. That boy at the water line, and his grandmother back at the table next morning." He waited. "I held Teo for an hour, and I bought a fare. Both things are true. Would you like me to pick one?"

Eli's hand shut on the splint and opened.

Jon patted his arm. "You're doing beautifully," he said, and went back to Teo.

They rode eight days in that hold with the forty. The cold was mended on the second. By the fourth the rows by the hull were dripping at the fingers, and the lean had gone further, and a smell came down the hold toward the bunks a row at a time. Eli slept better than he had since the lifeboat.

The orcs were delivered at the Legion yards at the same weight, near enough, and signed for.

---

The last night before Earth they sat in the cabin of a borrowed lander with the heat off. Eli's mended fingers ached, and he kept working them. Earth was under the port, the night side, with fires on it. He had lived his whole life down there and had not seen it from above.

"I asked you once what we do when we get there," he said.

Jon looked up. "We tire him out."

"I came to take the chair."

"I know. That's why." Jon held up a hand to count on and then didn't. "He's sat in it longer than there are records of anybody sitting anywhere. People have tried to kill him. Every kind of people, with every kind of—"

"So we don't kill him," said Teo.

"So we don't. We go in, and every one of you does the best thing you've ever done, and he has to answer every single one. He'll keep a hand for me. You get the other one. At the end of it he's tired. Tired men can be held. Tired men can be talked to. Tired men give up chairs."

"My order says dead," said the Queen.

"Then that's your best thing," Jon said, "and he'll have to answer it."

The sheath shifted against her leg. That was all.

"In his own house," said the master. "In the strongest place on earth."

"It's strong for everyone. It makes everyone bigger. It doesn't make anyone less tired."

"And when he's tired I talk to him," said Eli. "That part's mine."

"Then we'll see."

Eli asked them then, round the cabin, one at a time, plainly: were they going up.

"Wouldn't miss it," said Doc.

"I'm not going with you." She had a knife across her knee. "I'm going where you're going."

"I do not go at your asking," said the Queen.

Teo turned his good ear. "What do you call him? The first time. To his face."

Eli told him. It had been drilled into him at eight, for a man he was never let near: the title, every word; the bow from the waist and not the neck; eyes on the second step, never the first; then what you'd come to ask, short.

The master hadn't answered. He stood up.

---

He stood looking down at his bag.

"He'd have—" Jon began.

"Don't," said the master. "You never met him."

Jon shut his mouth.

The master picked the bag up and went to the hatch and opened it, and stood in it with the dock's cold coming in round him. Eli thought of two things to say. The cold reached his fingers.

The old man shut the hatch. He came back and shoved the bag under his seat with his foot.

Later, in the dark, Teo was saying the title over under his breath. He got it wrong and went back to the first word. Wrong again, further on. Back to the first word.

Across the cabin Doc laughed softly.

"Sorry," he said. "I was thinking about my brother's goat."
