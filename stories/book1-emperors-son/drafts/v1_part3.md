## SIX · The Strike

They were a day out from the forge world, with the stitches in Eli's cheek still pulling every time he swallowed, when every band on the bridge went to the same picture.

A long chamber, ribbed, built along the curve of a ship's spine. Crew strapped in at their stations, scaled and wingless and quick in the hands, and they kept looking at one another. A demonstration, the caption said, by House Vaskhir.

At the centre of the chamber stood the cradle. Eli knew it before he knew why. His duelling master from the dragon schools had drawn one for him on a slate, the winter he was fourteen: a body poured its whole strength in, and the ship gathered it up and threw it across the dark in one line. A focusing chamber, the dwarves who built them said. Crews took turns in it, a few shots each and then a day on their backs.

The ship's master was climbing into it himself.

"Master." One of the crew, half out of his straps. "The chamber is not rated for—"

"Then it will learn." He pulled the last strap tight with his teeth. "For my grandfather's grandfather, who died on betrayal day. Aim for the forge-heart."

He opened his mouth. The picture shook. The last of it was the long bones of his neck going one after another while he held the line anyway, and then the band went grey.

On the other screen, from orbit, the line. Straight as a rule. It went in at the forge-heart and the forge-heart was gone, and then the yards, the half-made hulls buckling in their berths one after another like ribs under a boot, and the feed lines from the haulers with everyone still walking up them. Then the crust came up in a ring and kept coming, red rock taller than mountains rolling out across the face of the world.

The yard wants the berth back by morning, Jon had said.

Nobody spoke. The dark elf was at Eli's shoulder, close enough that he could hear her breathing, slow and even, and then not. Teo was crying. He didn't wipe his face.

"The giant," said Teo. "Was he— did he get— he'd have been in the hall, wouldn't he, he'd—"

Nobody answered. Jon stood at the back of the bridge with a cup of tea he hadn't touched.

"His crew will have cooked," he said. "In their scales, at their stations. Ask any of them and they'd have told you they were dragons." He looked into the cup. "Nobody warned the giants' city either, on betrayal day. They'll be saying that to each other all week."

Three days later the giants came out. Hundreds of cathedral ships, out of yards on worlds nobody had known the giants still held. Eli counted them on the display until he lost count, somewhere in the four hundreds, and started again, and lost it in the same place.

*There are not enough of us left to die.* He had stood in that hall and watched the giant's face the whole time he said it.

A dwarven relay had the fleets hitting every dragon flag in reach, and every port that flew one, Earth among them. Then it went on to prices.

"So where do we," said Teo, "there's no ship now, is there, there's no giant, where do we even—"

"Back for the master," said Eli.

---

Jon got off at the rim port before the ramp was all the way down, his bag on his shoulder.

"Rig town," he said, looking out at the dock lights. "Companies, ledgers, cameras on the field. I'll find you."

It had taken them a month to get there through a sector coming apart. Rimtown was emptier. Three of the five companies had pulled their rigs and their boys and gone to the war, and their wells stood capped along the rim with nobody shelling them. The glass looked bigger without the guns.

The master was in his temple. He watched Eli come up the broken steps and didn't get up.

"You're better," he said.

Eli sat down across from him on the cold floor where he had sat for five months, and folded his legs the same way.

"You said nobody told you how far he got," he said. "Your master. I'm going up those stairs. Come and find out."

The master looked at him a long time.

"That's a cheap trick," he said. "Get out of my temple."

---

"Hear that?" Teo was on his back under the drive panel. "That whine. It's the coil, or the thing next to the coil, I can patch it but it won't hold, it wants a part, and the part's dwarven, two systems off. Four days." He slid out. "Or we go now and it holds. Probably holds."

"We wait," said Eli.

He didn't go back up the steps. He sat on the ramp in the cold four nights, and slept a little in the afternoons, and didn't look at the steps at all.

On the third day the last two rig students came down from the temple with their bags, in company straps, and walked across the field to a recruiter's hull with its ramp down. One of them looked back once. Nobody came down after them. The temple door stood open at the top of the steps on nothing.

The part came on the fourth night. Teo had the drive warm by morning. They had the ramp half up and the rain coming in under it when the master came across the field with a bag on his shoulder: old leather, split at the corners, the one that had hung on a peg by the temple door for five months and that Eli had never once seen him touch.

Eli opened his mouth. The old man went past him up the ramp.

"Your drive's whining," he said from inside. "Get somebody to look at it."

---

The cutter caught them over a gas giant whose name none of them ever learned.

The grapple hit like a dropped building. The hold went sideways and Eli went with it, across the deck and into a stanchion ribs first, and something in his side gave with a small wet sound he heard from the inside.

He lay there. Breathing came in pieces.

"How did they find us?" Teo, from the pilot's chair, shouting. "We squeezed, we were squeezed the whole way, how did they—"

"You ordered a part," said the master.

Across the passage Jon's bunk stood empty, the blanket folded. It had been folded since Tessid.

Eli got to the bridge along the walls. The hail light was blinking. Before it opened the Queen stepped out of the screen's line, two paces, to the bulkhead beside it, and stood there with her back to the metal. Eli saw her do it. Nobody else was looking at her.

The screen opened. A grey coat. A narrow face he had sat across a table from in Seluvane, three times through.

"Whose are you?" said the inquisitor.

On a panel beside him he had their faces up, the four who had come through his gate, Jon's among them, smiling. Under them a scanned sheet in a dwarvish hand, and in its margin, by Eli's face, a ring in grease pencil.

Eli had last seen that pencil in a breast pocket, on a moon with no water on it.

He didn't answer the question.

"The hull," said the inquisitor, "and the hell-born you carry. She will be pardoned. You may go on."

Everyone looked at Eli. The dark elf didn't move. She was standing where she always stood, by the open hatch.

"No," said Eli.

The inquisitor wrote something in his ledger. "Then you may stay aboard," he said, and the screen went dark.

"Stay aboard and he tows us to his station," said the master. He was looking out of the forward port at the grapple, the cable of it running a ship's length out to the cutter, and he looked at it a long time. Then he looked at the lifeboat hatch.

The Queen was still against the bulkhead. One step would put her in the screen's eye, the king's own piece. Eli watched her not take it. She didn't look at the dark elf.

They took what they could carry, water first. The Queen put her hand on the release and looked at nobody and pulled it.

"They don't board what they can starve," said the dark elf.

Through the lifeboat's port Eli watched the cutter take up the slack and tow the low black hull away into the bands of the gas giant, slowly, until it was too small to be anything.

---

On the seventeenth day they shared the last cup by count, a mouthful each, the master holding it. Doc made a joke about it. Nobody laughed, and he made another one.

On the nineteenth there was a hull on the scope.

A salvage tug, old and slow, with a crew of three. Its master hauled them aboard by their collars one at a time and gave them water out of a bucket.

"Paid to sweep here," he said, when Teo asked. "Didn't say for what."

Eli put that away with the rest.

Jon was on the dock at the next port with a bag of peaches. He was sorry. He said so, and looked it, and held Eli by both arms and saw how he was standing and said, "Ribs," and was sorry about that too.

"Nineteen days," said Eli. "Where were you?"

"Elves."

A drone over a river gorge, and Jon asleep. *A station keeps ledgers.* The rim port. The grapple. *They tell me you answered well.*

"You're never there when the grey coats are," said Eli.

"No," said Jon. "I'm not." He held out the bag. "Teo. How's that leg? Really, now."

It hurt Eli to reach. He took a peach anyway.

---

His ribs took four months to knit, and the war went on round them the whole time.

They crossed two systems in the hold of a dwarven arms freighter, sleeping on crates of flesh rifles that twitched whenever the drive spun up. GROWN FROM CERTIFIED CLONE STOCK, the stencil said. NO SOUL. One crate had split. The rifle in it had a fingernail in the grip, small and clean, where a thumb would go.

Eli looked for the Dry on the freighter's feed every night for a month. One night it was there. An elven fleet had come down for the seedship and cleared the dunes on both sides of the riverbed for a landing field. There was no colony in the picture. The line in the riverbed was still there. He turned it off.

At a dwarven port two systems on, the low black hull sat on the next berth along with fresh paint down its flank. Doc read the new name off it. *Fair Weight.* He didn't say what he thought of that one. The dark elf looked at it, and then somewhere else, and kept looking there until they were past.

At the port gate the guards stopped everyone in the line and went through bags and coats, Doc's and the master's too. One held a poster up beside Eli's face and looked from one to the other, chin and jaw. The poster had the chin scar. It didn't have the cheek. He waved Eli on. On the far side Teo was already waiting, with a crate on his shoulder he'd carried through behind them, eyes down. Nobody had stopped him. Nobody had looked.

The posters were on every world after that, and somebody had always got to them first with a key or a knife: the face scratched out to the backing, the seal left standing over the hole.

On a human world whose name he didn't catch they came out of a market into a square where a crowd was hanging a gnome from a lamp standard. He'd stolen souls, somebody said. It was the week's thing to say. The crowd sang to the Emperor's god while he kicked, under a broadcast wall with the seal where a face should be. The wall ran on a cored mind; it said so on the plate. Across the square was Eli's own face on a post, the eyes dug out.

His hand was half up. Teo was watching it. He put it down.

"Poor thing," said Doc, at the back with them. "They always get the wrong one."

The master stood beside Doc through all of it and didn't move at all.

On the next freighter a boarding party came over the side, and Doc fought, badly, slow and short of wind, with a reach that would knock a rifle aside and not much more. Then he finished the man with the troll's stick and complained about his knees. Afterwards he sat bandaging a dwarf's leg and talking to him about goats, and the master watched him, and the master's face went still and strange, as if he were listening for something in another room.

Doc looked up. "Hurt yourself?"

"No," said the master, and walked off rubbing his eyes.

---

They went through hell once, on a human transit hull, because there was no other way across.

When the hull dropped through, the dark elf went rigid. She sat with her back to the bulkhead and her eyes shut, and found his hand without looking, and held it.

The light had gone out, she said afterwards. The homeworld. And in its place the world outside the hull, hundreds of millions of her own kind, everyone she had ever got away from. Pressing.

Over the eleven hours she broke two of his fingers, one at a time. The first went early, with a sound he felt in his elbow. The second took longer. He didn't make a sound, and he didn't take the hand away. By the eighth hour he couldn't have said which finger was which, and there were three hours left.

When they came out the other side she let go and looked at his hand, and then at him.

Doc set the fingers. The next morning in the galley Eli flexed the splinted hand at her across the table, and it hurt, and he did it again.

She told him her name. Nobody else heard her say it. He carried it round with him all that day, and his fingers throbbed under the splint the whole time, and he didn't notice them until night.

---

Jon had left them two ports back, for Earth's edge. Somebody had to find them a way down, he said.

The dragon came for them on an airless moon where the freighter had put down for water.

It came down out of the black with a quarter-mile of wing spread over them, hunting gunrunners, and it had found one. Its first breath took the drive section off at the root. No sound. Nothing to carry it. Eli felt it come up through his boots. Its second breath took the dwarven crew, who had run for the hills.

"It's young," said the master on the suit band, very calm. "Make it breathe. It can't do that forever. Make it waste them."

He walked out from under the hull into the open dust and stood there.

The Queen went the other way, across the flat in plain sight, so fast she was in two places at once. The head swung after her. Breath, and a miss, and a canyon cut across the moon where she'd been, and the lip of it threw her twenty yards. She got up with a hand on her side.

The next breath came for the master and didn't get there. It bent off him into the ground like water off a blade, and a mile of moon went up behind him in a slow fan that never came down. His nose was bleeding inside his faceplate. The next one, his ears. The one after that put him on one knee, and he stayed there with his hand up.

Doc was in the freighter's shadow the whole time, out of the line, throwing rocks, his voice on the band gone high and cracked.

The dark elf came out of the dust under the head with one hand raised and the fingers a little curled, and closed them. A fistful of scales went out of the jaw into nothing, and she went down on her knees. Then she was up again, under the jaw, a step late, and laid her palm flat on the scales of its throat, and the whole head jerked aside. Not far enough.

Teo had left the master's cover. He was out on the open flat with a flesh rifle against his shoulder, firing into the flank, and then he was running back past the freighter's shadow.

"Do something!" Eli heard him on the band. "Doc! Do something!"

"Saving myself for something special," said Doc, puffing.

The line went past Teo a second later, out where the master wasn't looking, close enough to take the skin off his left hand and forearm through the suit.

After that the breaths came slower, and ragged, and short. The dragon's sides heaved. It stood on the dust with its head down, swaying.

"Now," said the master.

Eli had stood at the edge of it with his hands at his sides. He reached.

He went slowly and blind, as he had for the moth. Through the scales, which was like feeling through a mountain. Through muscle and bone and the lungs, huge and hot. He found the heart, the size of the flyer and labouring, and in it the narrow place where everything went through.

He closed his hand.

The dragon lifted its head and looked straight at him across the dust, as if it had only now noticed he was there. Then it fell, and the moon shook under his knees, and the dust went up over it in the airless dark and stayed up.

Eli was on his knees with his nose running inside his faceplate. The master was on his, ten yards off, blood dried black in both ears.

"That," said the master, "was fine."

Teo sat against the landing strut with his burned arm held out from his body, the suit seal hissing where the skin had come off with the cloth, and Doc was already cutting it open. Afterwards Teo went into the dead freighter and set off its beacon with his good hand. Dwarven beacons ran on the open band, so that anyone could sell a rescue. Nobody asked who would hear it.

---

They lived in the freighter's hold on dwarven rations. Over the hatch the beacon went red, and dark, and red.

Teo wouldn't let Doc dress his arm alone. He said it to Eli the first night, low, while Doc was laying out the dressings: would Eli stay, or the master, just while, just so—

"Of course," said Doc, easily, unrolling gauze.

Eli stayed, every time. When he shut his eyes the dragon lifted its head off the dust and looked at him.

On the fourth day the hold's one port went dark.

Something was lying alongside the moon, bigger than the moon's horizon. Eli put his face to the port and saw ribbing, pale and curved, running away out of sight both ways, and he knew it. His father's ship had hung at anchor over the capital every day of his life, filling the window of his old rooms.

They counted it out loud. The master couldn't stand. The Queen's ribs were taped. Teo had one hand. Six rifles, and the troll's stick. Out there, a ship that could take the face off a moon.

"Not this one," said the master, from the floor of the hold, to nobody.

Eli opened the hatch himself.

The lord's crew came in suited: humans, dwarves, an orc with the dead hunter's ruined wing-tip folded over his shoulders, and elves. The elves wore collars, plain grey bands with a mark stamped in the front. The dark elf looked at the collars and said one word.

"Pardoned."

On the bridge the lord sat on his throne at the head of everything, and his crew went down to their stations in front of him. Behind the throne, up on a raised gallery, ran the ring of the focusing chamber: two dozen cradles, most of them empty, the straps on the empty ones worn pale. Eli knew what it was before they strapped him in. He had seen one on a slate, and once since, breaking a man's neck.

From the gallery he could see over the throne's back: the folded wings, the long neck, and past them the open deck and the stations and the crew at them.

The dark elf let them buckle her in. She didn't look at Eli. The lord never once turned his head to see what had been wired in behind him.

The ship didn't leave. For days the sound of the hunter coming aboard came up through the hull, a quarter-mile of wing cut and winched in a piece at a time. A dragon's dead went home.

---

By the fourth day he had it ready: what they were worth, in what order, and the first sentence. He had learned the bridge from the gallery. He had held his cradle steady when it hummed, and been sick only once.

The bridge turned.

That was how he had it afterwards: every head at the stations turning at once toward the open deck between them and the throne. Someone was standing there.

He was taller than anything on the bridge but the lord. He didn't stoop. The ceiling had been grown for someone his size. At the far end, past the stations, a seam in the hull was closing, slowly, from the edges in. Nobody was near it.

He looked up at the dragon like a man come to collect, and opened his mouth.

The dragon breathed first.

Eli saw the great head go back and the jaw open over the back of the throne, and nothing come out of it that he could see. The air between the jaw and the god wavered once and lay still. Behind Eli the cradles stayed dark.

The god looked down at himself. His chest. His hands.

Nothing. Eli's cradle hummed against his spine the way the whole ship hummed. Somebody at the stations coughed.

Then the orc.

He was at the back, at a station against the hull, the one who'd carried the wing-tip in. He had spent four days scraping scale off the deck with a blade and humming something with no tune. He put his hand up to his face, and his face went grey and slack and old under it, and then he burst. All at once. He was standing at his station, and then his clothes were coming down on to the deck with dust going up out of them, and something wet under the dust.

"That's not heat," said the master, very low.

Then the humans.

A woman at the helm turned in her seat to look at the heap where the orc had been, and her hair was white before she'd finished turning. The man beside her stood up and got old standing. His back went. His hands went to knots on the board. When he opened his mouth to shout his teeth came out of it. They went down at their stations one after another, a dozen of them, the skin drawing in and then no skin, until what was left in the seats was dust in a uniform. Eli watched every one. He'd have been in one of those seats if the lord had wanted him there.

Teo was crying again, and again not wiping it.

Then the dwarves.

They lasted. One had time to get out of his harness and take three steps toward the throne, and his beard went white and came away on the third step, and he kept walking. He didn't make the fourth.

Then the elves.

They went slowest. Every one of them turned at their stations and looked up at the gallery, at the same place, at the two elves in the cradle ring. The dark elf's hands were locked on her straps.

"Still there," she said. "All of them."

They got old. Slow enough to watch, slow enough that Eli saw a collar go loose round a throat as the throat thinned inside it. The strap under the dark elf's right hand folded in on itself with a small flat sound and was gone. That was all it did. There was nothing in reach to use it on. The Queen's face didn't move. Her eyes were on the stations and they stayed there.

The elves went down.

"Gone," said the dark elf.

The god hadn't moved. He stood on the open deck in the dust of the whole crew with his hands at his sides, and he smiled up at the dragon.

"Your crew is dead," he said. The deck carried it. "How will you get home?"

The dragon didn't answer. From the gallery Eli could see the side of the great jaw. The scales along it were wet.

Then the god trembled.

It went through him once, all the way down, and he stopped smiling. He looked at his hand. He turned it over and looked at the palm, and back, and the fingers shook, and he watched them shake.

He turned his head and looked behind him at the stations. He looked at them in order: the heap at the back by the hull, the humans in their seats, the dwarf on the deck three steps short of the throne, the elves. As far as the elves. Then down at his hand again, and the skin on the back of it had gone fine as paper.

It took him longer than any of them. He got old standing up, as the man at the helm had, but slowly, every part of it there to be seen, and he never once looked away from his hands. Toward the end he was on his knees. Then there was dust on the deck where he had knelt, a great deal of it, settling.

Doc had watched the god the whole way down. He was leaning as far forward as his straps let him, with the bright, attentive face he'd worn over the miner's arm.

Dust at every station.

The dragon breathed out. Then, for the first time since they had come aboard, he turned his head and looked behind him, at the ring.

---

He looked along it at what was left. Then at Eli. He looked at Eli the way you look at weather.

They unstrapped him. His legs went, and he caught the gallery rail. Below him the dust in the nearest seat had kept the shape of whoever had sat there, shoulders and all, until air from a vent moved it.

His first sentence was no use now. He used the god's.

"You'll want a crew to get home," he said. "There are six of us. I'll take a station. He can fly anything with a seat, one hand or two. Those two can hold a station each, and the old man. The gnome keeps everyone alive. Take us as far as your house port. Your house will have hands waiting there, your own, and you won't want to feed strangers past that. We won't ask you to. The voyage. That's all."

The lord's head turned back to the front.

That was the answer. Somebody came along the ring and unbuckled the rest.

The dark elf didn't look at him. She took the station farthest from the throne, and sat in it, and didn't speak to him again.

Nine days to the house port, at the stations, in the dust of the crew, inside a hull grown out of dead giants. Teo flew one-handed and talked to the helm under his breath. The Queen held an elven station and didn't say whose it had been. She sat down on the dust in the seat and didn't brush it off.

Doc didn't talk for a whole day. Nobody had ever seen him go a day. "He hasn't said anything, has he," Teo whispered that night, "all day, not a goat, not anything, is he—" and in the morning Doc started a story about a goat over the ration tins as if nothing had happened.

The lord never asked their names.

---

Jon was on the quay when they came down the ramp, with a hired hop behind him and a bag of peaches.

The dark elf was first off. She went past Jon without looking at him and stopped by Eli.

"Next time ask me first," she said, and walked on.

Jon looked up at the hull a long while, all the way along the ribs to where they went out of sight. He didn't ask what had happened aboard. Eli waited for him to ask.

He put his hand on Eli's shoulder. "I knew you would," he said.

"How?"

Jon opened his mouth and shut it. He looked up at the hull again.

"I don't know," he said. He took his hand away. "Teo. Let me see that arm."

Eli stood on the quay a while with a peach in his hand that he didn't remember taking, and didn't eat it, and didn't ask again.

---

A month on, on another freighter, the hull took a hit and the cold got into the hold where forty chipped orcs were being shipped to the Legion yards.

Teo found them. He came back to the bunks shaking and couldn't speak, and sat on the end of Eli's bunk with his good hand between his knees and got it out in pieces. They'd been told to stand in rows. Most of them had frozen standing, in rows. Near the warmest bulkhead the rows had leaned in toward it as far as the chips would let them, and some of them had got an arm round the next one.

Eli didn't go and look. He stayed in his bunk.

Jon went and looked.

That evening Teo went down the passage toward the purser's hatch with his savings in a twist of cloth, the one that lived in his boot. Jon was in the passage before he got there, and put a hand on his shoulder, and sat him down on a bench under the lights.

He sat with him an hour, an arm round him. Eli heard it from the bunk room: the first dead Jon had ever seen, a long time ago; how it never stopped mattering; how that was how you knew you were still a person. Teo cried into his coat. Something about a list, and his mother.

"They'll ask me where he is," Teo said. "If I go back. And I'll tell them. I know I'll tell them."

Jon said his parents must be very proud of him.

When Eli came out into the passage it was nearly done.

"You'll stay?" said Jon.

A long time.

"Yeah."

The purser came by on his rounds. Jon, without taking his arm off Teo, booked the whole crew's passage to the next system, in that same hold.

"At a discount," he said. "They won't be needing it."

The purser wrote it down.

---

Eli stepped out in front of him in the passage.

"You held him for an hour," he said. "Then you sold their deaths for a fare. You talked him into staying. For what?"

Jon smiled at him, kindly, as if he were glad to be asked.

"You didn't go and look," he said.

Eli said nothing.

"The man at the fence," said Jon, gently. "Nine at the crater, chained together. That boy at the water line, and his grandmother back at the table next morning." He waited. "I held Teo for an hour, and I bought a fare. Both things are true. Would you like me to pick one?"

Eli's hand opened and closed on the splint. It hurt. He did it again.

Jon patted his arm. "You're doing beautifully," he said, and went back to Teo.

Eli stayed where he was. After a while he went to his bunk and lay down, and the freighter went on toward Earth with him in it.

The orcs were delivered at the Legion yards eight days later, at the same weight, and signed for.

---

On the last night before Earth they sat in the dark of a borrowed lander's cabin. It was cold. Eli's mended fingers ached with it, and he kept opening and closing them, and stopped when he noticed, and started again.

Earth was under the port, the night side. He had lived his whole life on it and never once seen it from above.

"When we get there," he said. "What do we do?"

Jon looked up. "We tire him out."

"I didn't come all this way to tire him out. I came to take the chair."

"I know," said Jon. "That's why." He held up a hand as if to count on it, and didn't. "Your father has sat in that chair longer than there are records of anyone sitting anywhere. People have tried to kill him. Every kind of people, with every kind of—"

"So we're not even," said Teo, "we're not even trying to— so we don't kill him?"

"So we don't. We go in, and every one of you does the best thing you've ever done, and he has to answer every single one." Jon looked round the cabin at them. "He'll keep a hand for me. You get the other one. At the end of it he's tired. Tired men can be held. Tired men can be talked to. Tired men give up chairs."

"In his own house," said the master, from the dark. "In the strongest place on earth."

"It's strong for everyone," said Jon. "It makes everyone bigger. It doesn't make anyone less tired."

"And when he's tired," said Eli, "I talk to him. That part's mine."

Jon looked at him. "Then we'll see," he said.

Later, when they were lying down, Teo said very low, "How do you talk to him? The first time. What do you call him?"

Eli told him, as the stewards had drilled it into him at eight for a man he would never be let near: the title, every word of it; the bow from the waist and not the neck; eyes on the second step, never the first; then whatever you'd come to ask, short. He didn't ask why Teo wanted it.

Teo said it over under his breath, and got it wrong, and started again from the title, until his breathing changed.

---

The master got up after that, slowly, and stood looking down at his bag.

"Your master—" Jon began, gently.

"Don't," said the master. "You didn't know him."

Jon shut his mouth.

The master picked up the bag and went to the hatch and opened it, and stood in it with the cold coming in round him. Nobody spoke. Eli thought of three things to say and said none of them.

In the end the master shut the hatch and came back and shoved the bag under his seat, and didn't speak for the rest of the night.

Somewhere in the dark Doc laughed softly.

"Sorry," he said. "I was thinking about my brother's goat."
