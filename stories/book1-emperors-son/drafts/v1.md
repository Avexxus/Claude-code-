## ONE · Property

The guard didn't get tired. That was the whole problem.

Eli did. It started in his thighs and climbed into his chest, until every breath came in hot and short and not enough. Behind him the guard's boots hit the slag in a rhythm that never broke, and the rig on the man's back, a ribbed hump of grown flesh in a steel cage, gave a wet click every few strides.

"Stop! By imperial warrant!" The voice came out of a speaker at his collar, flat and much too loud. "Return what you took!"

One reach and the guard would be a stain on the wall. In the street. In daylight, with every window looking to see whose reach it was. He ran.

Towers on both sides, white, grown up out of the bedrock without a seam, the cheap kind every colony puts up, twelve floors and a flat roof. The one he'd grown up under had filled every window. He stopped looking up.

Wire ahead. Past it the yards: slag, cranes, open sheds, pens. Hot fat and ozone in his mouth.

He went over the fence. It bit him on the way, and he came down hard on one hip, and got up, and kept going.

The pens were full. Orcs in rows under the shed roofs, hundreds deep, men and women both, heavy in the shoulder and bare to the waist, waiting their turn on the ramps that ran up into the hull presses. Each of them had a neat pale scar at the base of the skull.

At the top of each ramp a press shut with a long sigh and opened again, and out of its far side, on rollers, came plate: ribbed, the size of a door, steaming in the cold. LEGION CONTRACT, CARRIER PLATE, down the flank of every press in yellow stencil. Men in gloves stacked it. When one of them leaned on a stack to rest, he took his hand away quickly, because it was warm.

The orcs on the ramps could see the stacks.

The young ones, not yet full in the shoulder, were penned apart beside a windowless shed with CORES stencilled over the door. At the rail one of them had wrapped a rag round his hand where the wire had cut it. He looked up at Eli going past, and then down at his feet. A blue comb lay in the slag by the rail with most of its teeth gone.

The guard didn't bother with the fence. He stopped at the wire, took a slate off his belt and spoke into it.

Every orc in the yard turned their head at once.

They came out of the pens in a slow wave, over the rails, hands open and reaching. The young one with the rag was crying. An old man near the front with a split lip was saying something over and over while his legs walked him forward, and when he got close enough Eli heard that it was a name.

Shed wall at his back. Nowhere.

Eli reached.

The air in front of him went hard and he swung it, and the front rank went over into the rank behind. He felt bones go, and heard them. More came on over the ones he'd dropped. The ones on the ground were trying to get up. Some of them couldn't.

He could have cleared the yard.

A man on the counterweight of the nearest crane, twenty feet up. Eating a peach. Two stones on the iron beside him already. A long coat buttoned to the chin, and the kind of face that looks pleased to see you before it knows who you are.

"You look," he said, "like you could use an exit."

He dropped off the crane, landed hard, and came across the slag still chewing. A woman with burn scars up both arms got to Eli first. Her eyes were on his face and she was saying sorry, sorry, in a small ordinary voice, while her hands came up for his throat. The man stepped in and laid two fingers on the back of her neck, on the scar, the way you'd test a child's forehead for fever. Her legs went. He let her down.

He took the next one by the wrist, turned him, touched, and let him down too. Then two at once, a hand for each, and he grunted at that and shook his fingers out afterwards. He kept himself between them and Eli and took them as they came. By the fifth or sixth he was breathing through his mouth. The old man with the split lip reached them still saying the name, and the man put a hand to the back of his head, and the name stopped halfway.

Then nobody else came. Over by the fence the guard had lowered the slate and was backing off, talking fast into his collar. In the pens and on the ramps the rest stood where they'd been stopped, hands half up. The young one with the rag was still crying.

The man wiped his fingers on his coat and put a warm hand on Eli's shoulder.

"Breathe," he said. "You're doing beautifully." He nodded at the fence. "He's seen your face. He'll write it up, and it goes up, and up again, till it lands on the desk of whoever you're running from." The hand stayed where it was. "Your call. I won't make it for you."

Eli had ordered men flogged. At sixteen he'd watched a steward's hands broken for theft; his tutors said a prince should see what his word cost. He had never done it himself. Not once.

He reached. Forty yards of slag, and then the guard's throat, and he closed his grip on it and held. It took longer than he'd thought it would. The guard's heels drummed on the slag. Somebody's lunch tin stood open on the step of the nearest shed, with a fork in it. When it was over the body sat down against the fence, and the rig on its back kept clicking.

Eli's hand was shaking. He looked at it and thought that he would have to wash it before he ate anything, and then about where there might be water out here, and whether it would be clean, while it went on shaking.

The man took the hand in both of his and held it until it was still. "That's how you know you're not him," he said. "Whoever he is." He let go. "What did you steal, by the way?"

Eli wiped his mouth.

"Me," he said.

The man laughed so hard he had to bend over with his hands on his knees. Then he straightened up, finished the peach, and dropped the stone on the old orc with the split lip. It landed on his cheek and stayed there. Nobody took it off. Eli kept thinking someone should.

---

Teo was meant to be at the fence with the flyer. He wasn't.

Eli stood among the bodies and watched the sky over the sheds and counted to six hundred, and started again. Somewhere in the city a siren went up and down and quit. The man in the coat sat on an upturned bucket, cleaning his nails with a sliver of fence wire, and didn't offer to help.

Somewhere in the four hundreds the count went out from under him. He was thinking about the bath off his old rooms, down on the side of the house where the Tower's shadow lay all day and the light came in grey: the tiles warm underfoot, the taps shaped like fishes, and which of the fishes ran hot. He couldn't remember which. He started again from one.

"He's not coming," the man said pleasantly, somewhere in the second six hundred. "We could walk."

Eli didn't move.

The flyer came in low over the sheds forty minutes late, an old plain-engine thing with a cracked canopy and a fresh dent down one side, and hit the slag hard. Teo leaned out, white in the face, talking before the engine had stopped.

"They put a checkpoint on the ring road, I had to go round by the drainage cuts, the whole way round, I thought you were dead, I thought they'd—" He saw the bodies. He looked at the guard against the fence, and then at the stranger.

Nine days ago Teo had forged the code that put the two of them in the hold of an empty food convoy, outbound. His parents were still in the house.

"Did you do all that?" he said to Eli.

"No," said Eli.

"Jon," said the stranger, and climbed into the back of the flyer as though he'd been invited. "Friend of the family."

"Which family?" said Eli.

"Long story. Bad ending."

Teo lifted off. Below them the orcs were still standing in the pens. Another man with a slate had come out of the sheds, and a yard crew behind him with hooks. They worked along the dead in a line, and the bodies went up the ramps to the presses with everyone else, because the yard paid by weight. The young one with the rag was on a ramp. He was still crying, and his feet were still climbing.

"Where to?" said Teo.

Jon had found another peach somewhere. "Up," he said. "There's a hull going out tonight that takes flyers in the hold."

## TWO · The Master

They came down out of terraced hills on the fourth morning, green in rows on every slope. Teo said it was food. Growing, in the ground, where anyone could take it. Eli looked at it a long time.

That afternoon a drone came down the road Jon had sworn the drones didn't watch, and hung in the air ahead of them, looking.

Teo dropped them into the river gorge so hard that Eli bit his tongue. Under the overhang he killed the engine. Water underneath, brown and loud. Somewhere above them the drone's hum, holding one note.

Jon was asleep in the back with his mouth open.

An hour. Teo's hands never came off the controls.

"Can't you do something?" he said at last, to the back.

Jon didn't open his eyes. "Then whoever's watching would know what's in here."

He went back to sleep. After a while he snored a little. Eli sat with his back against the wet rock and watched him, a man who had walked into a yard full of chipped orcs not a week before, and listened to the drone not leave. It left after dark. When Jon woke he said they must have changed the routes, and ate something, and that was all he said about it.

That night Teo sat alone on the wing for an hour. When he came back in he said to Eli, quietly, "He just touched them and they died. He was eating a peach."

Jon, in his blanket, said without opening his eyes that he hadn't killed all of them, only the ones that reached Eli. The rest would have woken up in the pens and gone up the ramps on schedule. "They're awake in there the whole time, you know," he said. "The chip doesn't put you out. It only does the walking."

Nobody said anything about the one at the fence.

Teo lay down across the front seats. After a while, half under, he said, "They were closing the house. Did you know? Before we went."

"Closing it how?"

"The stewards had a list. My mother was on it." A long breath. "The codes were easy, that's the thing. Nobody watches a servant's boy at a terminal. Nobody ever—"

He stopped there. Eli waited for the rest of it and it didn't come, and after a while Teo's breathing changed.

In the morning Jon made Teo breakfast and asked about his parents and listened to the whole answer, the kitchens, his father's bad knees, the list, and said they must be very proud of him. By noon Teo was laughing at his stories.

On the seventh morning Jon climbed out at a fuel stop in the middle of nothing and stretched until his back cracked.

"I have to go and see some elves about a thing," he said.

"What do you want from me?" said Eli.

Jon looked at him pleasantly.

"Company," he said, and walked off down the road.

---

After that he came and went. A week on a salvage hull where Eli and Teo were cutting cable out of dead ships for cash. Nothing all winter. A transit platform, and a bag of peaches, and a month. When Eli asked where he went, he said, "Elves."

Eli learned what food cost. He went hungry three days on a station where nobody would hire a man with soft hands, and on the fourth he broke a dock boss's knee for a job, and took the job. Teo didn't speak to him for a week. Eli found he minded that more than the knee.

Half the shopfronts on that station kept a little shrine to the Emperor's god, lit with a cheap bulb, and not one of them had the Emperor's face on it. Where a face would have gone there was the seal, the plain empty oval that was on the coins too. Nobody knew the face. Eli walked past the shrines twice a day, to the docks and back, and it was most of a month before it came to him that he didn't know it either.

On the docks they talked about elves. Raiders had hit an imperial convoy three days out from the capital, forty hulls, the whole string.

"Food," said the man next to Eli on the line. "Earth'll be eating its furniture."

"Earth's never missed a meal in its life," said somebody else.

Eli had come out on one of those convoys, nine days in an empty hold with no window. He kept his head down and his hands moving.

Crates came off the transit hulls with well-company marks on them, POOL-GRADE CORE, THIS WAY UP. A dockhand kicked a cargo loader that had stopped to think too long. "Battery," he said, and spat.

Jon was in that month. He watched the loader trundle off. "Orc," he said. "Young one, by the size of the core. Flesh takes the shape better at that age. They put pool on the label so nobody has to feel funny about it." He offered Eli a peach.

Eli took it.

The station's legal exchange was two decks below the hiring hall, and Eli passed it every morning. It had a clean white front and a board of prices that changed through the day: eyes, kidneys, skin by the hand's width, ELF LIVER ENQUIRE WITHIN. Behind the glass the whole-body stock stood asleep in upright cases with tags at their throats. CLONE. NO SOUL. GRADE B. One morning a buyer had a case opened to look at a man's teeth, and the man woke. His face did what a baby's face does just before it cries. The clerk put him under again. "Reflex," he told the buyer. "Nobody home." The buyer took the kidneys and one eye and left the rest on account. The eye cost about what Eli made in a week on the docks. Two decks further down, with no front at all, the same stock sold for four times as much with papers saying born.

Eli stopped reading the board.

---

In the fourth month he found his own face on a post outside a customs hall.

RECOVER IMPERIAL PROPERTY, it said, under the seal. Return what you took. It had him down to the scar on his chin. By the date, it had gone up seven days after he ran. Two days before the yard.

He stood in front of it while the shift changed in the hall behind him.

He'll write it up, Jon had said. And it goes up, and up again.

It had gone up already. The man against the fence had died for nothing, and Jon had known.

He looked at the face a long time. The print was cheap. The mouth was his, or near enough. But the jaw under the scar had come out harder and squarer than he thought of his own, and the longer he looked the less it belonged to him. A stranger's jaw.

Teo came up beside him, read it, and said nothing.

"Property," said Eli.

His father had never once asked to see him. Tutors, reports, orders. Never a face. The Tower had stood over the house every day of his life, and he had never been through one of its doors. And now a post on every world, till somebody dragged him home.

"I'm going to the Tower," he said. "I'm going to take the chair away from him."

Teo laughed, then saw his face, and stopped.

---

When Jon next turned up Eli told him before he had his coat off. The Tower. He'd need people. He'd need to be better than he was, a lot better.

"You knew it was already up," he said. "The warrant. You let me kill him for nothing."

"I knew it would be." Jon went on with his buttons. "Up that week, up the next. Does it matter much? He'd have written it either way. They always write it up." He put his hand on Eli's shoulder, where it had been in the yard. "A great deal better," he said. "Yes. I'd think so."

"Then teach me."

"Oh, it wouldn't take. My kind of teaching, you'd come out the other end being me, and one of those is plenty." He was looking round the bunk room, at the hot plate, the one window, the wet coat on the hook. "There's a rift on Tessid. People go there to learn to listen. Teo, is there anything to eat?"

Teo said there was soup. Jon said soup was wonderful.

Eli stood there with the question still in his mouth and watched himself not ask it again. He had watched stewards do it to his tutors for years: agree with everything, and walk off with the point. He had never had it done to him.

---

He had been taught to push, hard and clean. He could break a wall from across a room. He could not tell, with his eyes shut, where the wall was.

Tessid was grey and cold and too far from anything to matter, apart from the wound in the ground: three miles across, the rock gone to glass, the air tasting like a struck bell. The well companies pumped pool out of the glass like oil, five of them along the rim, and none of them had ever quite won. They had been at it longer than anyone in Rimtown had been alive. Somebody had built a temple on the near edge, and somebody else had shelled it, and somebody had built it again.

Soldiers came off the transports in amplifier rigs that let an ordinary man reach three times as far as he should. On his second day Eli watched one burn out. He was perhaps nineteen, holding a line on the north wells, and he pushed through his rig twenty seconds longer than his body could carry. His face went grey, then slack. He sat down in the mud with blood coming out of his ears and eyes. Two company men came with a stretcher and a cold box, and the eyes and the kidneys were in the box before they had him off the field. The rig was off him before he was cold and buckled onto the next boy in line. Eli watched the new boy's hands doing up the straps. They were steady.

"Burnout," said the woman who sold Eli noodles that night. "Rig's fine. It's the meat that goes."

At the end of her counter two rig boys with their straps still on were arguing whether the Hand could take a dragon.

"He doesn't sleep," one said. "Carrier-born. Stands by the chair all night, every night, waiting to be told."

"Told what?"

"Anything."

The master lived in the shelled temple. He was old and spare, and at one time or another he had trained rig soldiers for every company on the rim. He watched Eli come up the broken steps and then looked at his hands.

"Who taught you?"

The three best tutors in the Empire, Eli told him, and a duelling master from the dragon schools.

"They taught you to shout," said the master. "Sit."

---

He kept Eli five months and never once let him push.

Every morning the master sat cross-legged on the cold floor of the temple with his eyes shut, and Eli sat across from him and did the same. Teo had found work on the company docks patching flyers and came up the steps at night with whatever there was to eat. Then the master blindfolded Eli in the middle of the room and told him to find the one moth in it, and when Eli couldn't, he left him sitting until he could.

On the eighth day, with the cloth over his eyes, he heard an engine scream on the practice field below the steps, and boys shouting, and he pulled the cloth off. One of the rig students had pushed and lost hold of it. A loader twice the height of a man was going over off its tracks toward the line of them, slowly, the way heavy things go.

The master didn't get up. He looked at it from where he sat in the temple door. The loader stopped leaning. It came upright and settled back onto its tracks as gently as a cup set down, and the boys stood under it with their mouths open.

The master rubbed his eyes for a long time. When he took his hand away there was blood on his lip, and he wiped it off with his thumb before any of the boys looked up the steps. Eli was looking.

"Anyone can lift," the master said to him. Nobody had asked. "Everyone out here who could lift is dead. Learn to listen." He nodded at the cloth in Eli's hand. "Put that back on."

It took eleven days. When it came it was nothing like pushing, only a small dry flutter up near the broken roof, and Eli was so surprised that he lost it again and had to sit most of another afternoon to get it back. After that the master had him lift a cup of water without spilling it, and then lift the water without the cup.

Nobody on the rim knew whose son he was, and the master never asked. Eli slept on the temple floor in his coat and woke before light without anyone calling him. The glass outside went from black to grey to a colour like weak tea, and the company guns started up along the rim at the same hour every day, far off, almost friendly. Some mornings, sitting in the cold with his eyes shut, he forgot for an hour at a time what he had come there for. He didn't tell Teo. He didn't tell anyone.

"You've the strength of an army," the master said, "and the hands of a man in mittens. One day it'll kill you."

In the fifth month Eli threaded a needle across the room without touching it, and sat looking at it, thread and all, until the master came over and took it away.

"You'll never be fine," said the master. "You started too late. But you'll be less likely to kill your friends by accident."

---

Before he left, Eli told him who he was. The Emperor's son. Where he was going, and what he meant to do there. He hadn't said it out loud to anyone since the house. He asked him to come.

The master went to the temple door and stood looking out over the glass and the far fires of the company lines.

"My master went up to that Tower," he said.

Eli waited.

"Fifty years ago. Best I ever saw. Said he'd go and see who sat in the chair." He didn't turn round. "Nobody ever told me how far he got."

He was quiet a while.

"No. I won't walk into that place behind a boy with a grudge. Go and die somewhere better than this."

Eli didn't argue. He let the no stand there in the doorway with the two of them until the master went back inside.

On the hull out Teo said, "We'll find someone else. There's other masters. There's got to be."

Eli didn't answer. He sat with his eyes shut and tried to find the walls of the cabin, and couldn't, and kept trying until Teo fell asleep.

## THREE · The Healer and the Statue

"If you're going to get hurt," the stretcher man had said on the rim, "get hurt near the gnome at Thirty-One. He sits up with the dying." The companies shipped him the ones they thought worth saving. Gnome healers came cheap.

The miner screamed the whole time the gnome was setting his arm, and the gnome talked the whole time, about a goat.

"Ate my whole herb garden, that goat. My brother's goat, properly; he was the clever one. The winter half the village came to me with fever, and there's me with not a leaf. I could have strangled him. Didn't, mind. I was very fond of him. Hold still."

He was small even for a gnome, round in the face, bald on top, with enormous ears and a stamped tin tag on a cord round his neck. Every gnome in the quarter wore one. The dwarves called the place the Tins for it. Holding Thirty-One was a mining district on a world with nothing but the number for a name, and in the dwarven districts gnomes worked on contracts that ran for life, and got very good at not being noticed. The clinic line went out the door and down the street. Eli stood at the back and watched him work through the afternoon, a boy with a crushed foot, then an old dwarf woman with a cough she said she'd had since the last war, and liked him more with each of them. Teo stood by the door and didn't like any of it.

The gnome took a feverish girl from her mother and sat with her behind a curtain for an hour, and brought her out again cool and asleep. She hung very loose in her mother's arms.

The mother tried to thank him. He held up a small hand.

"Thank me when she's grown," he said. "Next."

When he saw Eli and Teo he looked them over, quick and professional. "Not dying?"

"No," said Eli.

"Pity. I could use one I can fix." His eyes went to Eli's chin and away again, and he dried his hands. "So what is it you want?"

A long journey, Eli told him. A small crew. Someone who could close a wound and set a bone and not ask where either came from.

"Then you'll have to buy me," said the gnome. "The council holds my paper." He named a price. It was most of what they had.

Outside, Teo said, "There's something wrong with him."

"He's good."

"He's too good. Did you see that line? Nobody that good stays somewhere like this. Nobody. Why would you?"

"Then he'll be glad to leave," said Eli, and went back in and paid.

The gnome signed his own transfer with a flourish. Eli read it over and put it in the clinic stove.

The gnome watched it burn. Then he smiled up at Eli, very warmly.

"Well," he said. "Where would I go?"

"What do we call you?" Teo asked.

"Doc. The children call me that. Saves everyone the bother of a name." He picked up his bag. The tag stayed round his neck.

On the river bridge out of the district a troll sat in a toll box, a squat grumbling fellow a head taller than Doc and still a head short of Eli's shoulder. Doc paid him in coin and a joke about his mother. The troll didn't laugh. A mile on, Teo noticed the troll's stick was gone from the box. It was in Doc's bag, with a nail through the end.

"Always wanted one," said Doc.

---

Jon found them on the Thirty-One docks a week later with a sack of peaches, as if they'd arranged it, and nothing to say about the five months.

Eli wanted to see the elves. They were the Empire's oldest enemy, and they would be his war, if he got the chair. Jon said they were the only people left who could build a city that made you want to cry, and came along to get them in.

Seluvane stood on a world of high cold rivers, grown whole like the old giant cities but finer, and its towers leaned in toward each other at the top. Eli stood on the landing field longer than he needed to, with his bag at his feet, looking up at them.

Getting in took three hours of papers and searches. At the end of it an inquisitor in grey sat across a table from Eli with a ledger open at his elbow.

"Whose are you?" he said.

"My own," said Eli, and smiled.

The inquisitor didn't write anything down. He asked where Eli had been born, and Eli gave him a town that existed. He asked what Eli carried, and why, and how long, and who would answer for him, and which of his companions he would leave behind if he had to. Eleven questions. Eli tried charm on the first few, and then he tried being exact, dates and tonnages and the names of docks, and then he tried saying as little as a man could and still be answering. The inquisitor watched his eyes through all of it.

Then he went back to the beginning.

"Whose are you?"

The same eleven, in the same order, in the same voice. Then a third time. By the third Eli could hear himself giving the same answers, and hear how they sounded the third time.

At the end Jon spoke to the inquisitor in elvish and showed him a token on a chain. The inquisitor turned the token over, then held out his hand and kept it out until Jon took the chain off his neck and gave it to him. He put it in his coat. Then he stamped the papers, three days and not one more, with a red bar across the corner, and wrote a line in the ledger beside each of their names.

"I helped the king once, when he was a boy," Jon said, inside. He touched his collar where the chain had been. "That was my only one. Try not to steal anything."

"Who was he?" said Teo.

"Inquisition. The king's. They like to know where everyone is."

A grey coat was behind them in the market that afternoon, and another at the end of their street that night, and it was never the same coat twice.

---

On the second day they crossed a square where an elf knelt on the stones with his hands bound behind him and his mouth sewn shut with silver wire. People walked past without looking. A woman stood at the edge of the square in good clothes with her hands folded, not looking at him either. She was his wife, Jon said later. She was required to come every morning and stand there until dark.

"What did he do?" Teo whispered.

"Disagreed," said Jon. "Out loud."

Teo had his knife out. He'd gone two steps toward the man before Eli caught his arm above the wrist and held it.

"Three days," Eli said. "Not one more."

Teo looked down at the hand on his arm. He looked at it until Eli let go, and then he put the knife away, and after that he walked a little apart.

Nobody jostled in the streets. Nobody said excuse me in a doorway. Crowds parted and closed round each other without a word or a glance. The king's name was on the arches and on the coins and said quietly in the markets: Mika.

A young archivist had been sent to be helpful. Teo asked her where you could get something to eat that wasn't leaves. She told them about the dark elves instead, in two sentences, as though that had been the question. Born in the prison realm with the soul thieves and the traitors, and came out of it full of hate. Not really elves any more.

On the last afternoon a grey coat stood across the end of their street, and the way round went through a long square with a statue in the middle of it.

An elven woman, cut in white stone at twice life size. She stood with one hand raised and the fingers a little curled, about to close them on something only she could see. Fresh flowers were heaped at her feet, and elves stood round her in silence. The square was very still. The water in the gutters ran quieter there, and the light lay on the stone without moving, and for the first time in three days Eli couldn't see a grey coat anywhere.

"Lirael," said Jon. "Hero of her people. The king's sister. A long time ago."

At the foot of the statue an old elven woman was crying without a sound. Teo crouched beside her and asked in his bad elvish if she'd lost someone.

"Yes," she said.

"Who?"

She looked at him.

"I don't know," she said. "I never know. None of us do." She turned back to the statue. "We come here because it seems the place to bring it."

The others walked on. Eli turned to say something to Jon, and Jon wasn't beside him.

He was standing in front of the statue with his hands in his coat pockets, looking up into the stone face. He stood there long enough that the old woman looked up at him, and Eli started to say his name.

Then Jon turned round.

"Dinner," he said. It came out hoarse. He cleared his throat and said it again. "Come on."

## FOUR · The Door

"—dead moon, edge of elven space, and the floor's gone black where the world's worn thin," Jon was telling Teo. Before that it had been a fish. "Inquisition station on one rim. On the ninth of every month a dwarven ship sets down on the other."

A bad meal in a worse port, two months on from Thirty-One. The ninth was five days off.

"What for?" said Eli.

"Buying," said Jon, and went on eating.

"Buying what?"

Jon didn't look up.

"Dark elves. The Inquisition catches them as they come out and calls them soul thieves, and the dwarves move them on." He tore his bread. "Organs, mostly. An elf liver's meant to keep you young. It doesn't, but try telling a rich man. The strong ones go to the pits on the inner worlds; they fit them with claws and bet on them. The pretty ones go to houses to stand in halls and be looked at. Some go for crew, to anyone who wants hands that last. Some get grown into things." He pushed the salt across. "This is very good, have you tried it?"

Eli lay awake with it. Two walls away the port's air plant knocked, and caught, and knocked. In the morning he said, "We're going to the crater."

"Why?" said Teo. "They're elves. It's an elf thing. Isn't it? It's their thing, it's not even—"

"The buyers are ours," said Eli. "Rich men, he said. Human. That's Empire money on that ship." He was already checking the flyer's charge. "If I'm taking that chair, I'd like to know what comes with it."

Teo looked at Doc for help. Doc was eating the rest of Teo's bread.

Jon didn't come. "A station keeps ledgers," he said, doing up his coat. "I'm in one already." He had elves to see, and there was a hull out that afternoon, and he was on it with a bag of peaches before Teo had finished the argument. Two days later they paid a hauler to carry them out to the moon's system with the flyer in its hold, and flew the last of it themselves.

---

They watched from the rim for a day. The grit was black and fine and got into the food, and the seals of the flyer, and the creases of their hands. The station was a squat grey block on the far rim with its lights on all night. Nobody came out of it.

On the evening of the ninth the dwarven ship came down on the near side of the floor, low and black, and sat there ticking. Inquisitors in grey walked a line of chained figures out of the station and across the floor toward it. They were starved thin and shackled wrist to wrist, and their eyes caught the station lights and gave them back silver. They walked with their heads down and didn't look at the ship. At the ramp a dwarf in a good coat counted them. He looked in each mouth and pulled down each lower eyelid with his thumb, and marked foreheads with a grease pencil as they went by: a ring, a cross, a line. The smallest came up to his chest. An inquisitor counted the money.

Overhead a human freighter blinked out of the sky, and the air over the black floor shuddered after it like water after a stone.

"Where'd it go?" said Teo.

"Through," said Doc. He was watching the floor. "Their prison's just the other side, you see. Humans drop through it to cross quicker, and the elves hate that, oh, terribly. Their own ships go squeezed down a dimension, and in there they can't feel each other at all. Not a whisper."

"How do you know all that?"

"I listen," said Doc.

The trade at the ramp was nearly done. Eli went down the crater wall.

He didn't sweep. He found the guns on the dwarven ship as he had found the moth under the temple roof, one small hard thing at a time, and closed his grip on each barrel and bent it. Four on the flanks. Two by the ramp. He was still feeling along the hull for more when one he'd missed opened up from underneath, and it walked across the floor and through the end of the line, and he found it and crushed it, and by then it was nine.

Nine. Wrist to wrist. They went down together.

Then everything. He tore weapons out of inquisitors' hands, some of them. Lost the rest. A shot cracked off the rock by his head. Teo shouting on the rim. Teo's rifle, twice.

Doc coming down the wall behind him, slow, puffing, talking to himself. A dwarven crewman off the ramp with a cutting bar. Doc stepping aside, small and clumsy, and the bar going into the floor, and the troll's stick coming down across the man's knees, and down again, until he stopped trying to get up.

Then nothing was moving that wasn't theirs. It might have been three minutes. The inquisitors were dead or running, and the dwarf in the good coat was face down on his own ramp with Eli's grip on the back of his neck, saying something in dwarvish into the metal. Up on the rim Teo sat in the black grit with both hands clamped round his thigh and blood coming through his fingers, swearing at the man he'd shot, who was dead.

They broke the chains. Nobody thanked them. Most of the freed ones ran, out across the floor and into the dark under the far wall, without a word to each other or to anyone. The two at the end of the line lay where the gun had left them, still chained together. Eli broke that chain too, and stood there afterwards with the pieces.

---

One stayed. An old man with no teeth, at the edge of the place where the floor went strange, staring at the air over it. The air there trembled, very faintly, like a pane of glass in wind.

"Someone's there," he said. "I know that shake. I made it once, from the other side. Someone's at the door."

Eli couldn't feel souls. Nobody had ever said he could. But five months on a cold floor had taught him to feel the world a little, and over the black floor it was thin, and it was bulging, slightly, toward him.

"They've been at it a while," Doc said quietly. "Look how the floor's worn. Poor thing."

"Can it be opened?"

The old man laughed with no teeth. "From this side? By who? It doesn't open. It takes."

Eli put out his hands.

Five months of moths and cups of water. He found the edge of the thin place and felt along it a long time, the way he'd felt for the needle's eye, and found a seam narrower than that and got his grip into it, and pulled in a direction that wasn't there. It pulled back. His nose began to bleed. His sight went white at the edges, and went on going white. Somewhere Teo was saying his name, and saying it again, higher.

The seam gave.

A woman fell out of the air onto her hands and knees, and it closed behind her with a sound like a cough.

Starved thin. Rags tied over rags. A short curved knife in each hand. She came up off the floor already moving and went past Eli without looking at him. The last two inquisitors were running for their station. She caught them at the door and went in close, a palm on a chest, fingertips at a throat, and they folded on the step.

She walked back. She looked at the blood on his lip, and then at the air behind him, which had gone still.

"You opened it," she said. Her voice was rough from not being used. "From out here."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Someone was pushing."

She looked at him with plain contempt.

"Thirty of us, when we started," she said. "No one escapes alone. That's what they say down there." She looked at the closed air. "I was a child."

Eli's hands were coming up again. He hadn't decided it. She looked at them, and then at his face, and he put them down.

"The road had most," she said. "The door had the rest."

When he looked round for the old man, he had gone into the dark after the others.

Her eyes were silver, like the eyes in every story the nurses in the house had ever told him.

*Oh*, thought Eli.

"What's your name?"

"When you've earned it."

---

They took the dwarven ship. It was low and black, Returned work, and Doc read the name off its flank in dwarvish, *Ledger Closed*, and said it was a very hopeful thing to call a ship. It could squeeze through the world the way dwarven ships did. Teo would fly it, with his leg tied off in a strip of somebody's coat and his face the colour of the station walls.

The dwarf in the good coat sat on the grit at the foot of his ramp where they'd put him. The grease pencil was still in his breast pocket. He watched their faces one at a time, as he'd watched the prisoners' mouths, and when he got to Eli's he stayed there, the eyes and then the chin.

"Leave him a water ration," said Doc. "There's nothing here, you see. Not till the ninth."

Heels on slag. A rig clicking on a dead man's back.

"No," said Eli.

Doc looked at him. "That's slower," he said mildly.

"No water."

The dark elf was sitting on the ramp with her knives across her knees. She looked at Eli then, properly, for the first time.

"They'll want this ship back," Teo said, in the pilot's chair, with his teeth together. "You know that. Whoever it belongs to, they'll want it."

"It's fast," said Eli.

They lifted with the flyer in the hold. The dwarf was a small dark shape on the grit and then he wasn't.

Two days out, Eli counted the stores. They were a water ration short. He counted again and got the same number. Down the corridor Doc was in the galley dressing Teo's leg and talking about goats, and Teo was telling him to shut up about the goats, and Doc was telling him this was a different goat. Eli stood outside the galley and listened to it. Then he went back to the hold.

He thought about the ones who had run into the dark. There was nowhere on that moon to run to. The station had the only air and water, and the dwarven ship had been the only ship, and he was standing in it. The Inquisition would be back on the ninth.

---

It took her four months to stop sleeping with a knife in her hand.

She ate fast and without a sound, with her arm curled round her plate, and she couldn't stand a closed door, or being thanked, so they left the galley hatch open and stopped thanking her for things. Jon still wasn't back. Eli got into the habit of sitting up late in the galley with the hatch open, in case she wanted to talk. Mostly she didn't, and he sat there anyway, with the tea going cold and the drive humming up through the deck into his feet.

Some nights she told him things. She had trained on the road, she said, fifty years of it, with whoever would teach her, and paid for most of it by fighting. The road ran between the towns down there, one farm to the next. One teacher had sold her to a pit for three years and kept the money.

"A pit down there," she said. "For the human crews who stop over on the crossing. They came down to bet." She turned her wrist on the table. The rings round it were from the pit chain. "Fair price, for what he taught me."

"You could have gone up with one of them," said Eli. "In a hold. Nobody looks in a hold."

"The crews are the farmers' customers. They count what they carry." She went on eating. "They sell what they find at the next port."

Since the door, she said another night, she could feel the elves. Never down there. Out here they were always at the edge of her, a light, the homeworld, trillions of them from across the galaxy.

"It never goes out," she said, not looking at him. "Worst thing they did. They let us keep that."

Teo came in for water one night on his bad leg and called her a dark elf without thinking. She didn't look up from her plate. "There's a sun down there," she said. "It's a world. Like these. They named it hell. Then they named us after it." Teo filled his cup and went out, and he didn't use the word again.

One good night she talked for most of an hour, about a river on the road where the water came up warm out of the ground, and how you could sleep in it if you tied yourself to a root. When she got up to go he said, "Thank you."

He heard it on its way out of his mouth. She stopped in the hatch. Then she went, and she didn't come to the galley the next night, or the one after.

He sat up anyway. Nine nights the chair across from him stayed empty, and he drank his tea and listened to the drive. On the tenth he looked up from the cup and she was in the chair. He hadn't heard her come in. Neither of them said anything about it.

"The ones they sent down for stealing souls kept on doing it," she said, some nights after that. "Nobody stops them. They farm the towns. Every street pays a share. My street paid in children. Children are small, and nobody misses them long." She went back to eating. Then, still eating: "The ones that get grown into things. What do your people do with them."

Eli opened his mouth.

"I don't know," he said.

She nodded, and finished her plate.

---

She practised alone in the hold at night and stopped whenever anyone came. One night in the second month she didn't hear Eli. He hadn't meant to be quiet. He was in his socks.

She stood in the middle of the empty deck with one hand raised, the fingers a little curled, and closed them slowly. The air in front of her folded in on itself with a small flat sound, like a book being shut. A cargo strap a yard away went into it and was gone. That was all. It was very quiet. Eli had broken walls from across a room, and he had never once made anything go so quietly.

She saw him and dropped her hand.

"Lirael's Collapse," she said. "Down there you learn her name before your own. Everyone wants that one." She looked at where the strap had been. "Forty years. It's the one thing of theirs we took."

Jon was in the doorway behind Eli. They had picked him up off a port that morning, two months gone. Teo had asked him where, and Jon had said elves, and that five weeks of it were nobody's business, and smiled, and gone straight to his bunk. He had been grey in the face then. He was grey now.

"That's a hard one to learn," he said. "Good for you."

Her face shut.

He went off down the corridor whistling. Halfway along he lost the tune, and stopped, and started it again from the top.

## FIVE · The Queen and the Giant

Eli put the ship down in the dry riverbed between the two camps and walked out alone under both sets of guns.

The dead were stacked in the dunes on either side, under sheets on the elven side and under nothing on the other. A colony at the far end of nowhere, cut off so long it had forgotten anyone else was out there, and an elven seedship come down in the middle of its oldest story with its back broken. The colonists called their world the Dry. Invaders, they said. Vermin, said the elves.

Jon had brought them there for water, he said. He stood at the top of the ramp in his coat in the heat, looking at the seedship, not at Eli.

This much the house had taught him. He found the colony's grandmothers and the elven captain's second and sat them across a folding table in the sun, and let each side describe the other until it ran out of words. On the second day the elves walked out. On the fourth a grandmother spat on the table and the talks stopped for two days, and Eli sat at the table alone in the heat through both of them, with a jug of water in front of him that he didn't touch. Both camps could see the jug.

On the sixth day they were back at the table, and a colonist boy shot an elf at the water line.

Eli walked the boy to the elven camp himself. The boy was perhaps fourteen and didn't struggle. He kept asking where they were going, though he could see. Eli handed him over and stood there while they did it. They knelt him at the water line and the captain's second cut his throat, and two elves held him by the ankles so the blood went into the sand and not into the water. Eli stayed until it was done.

There was sand in his collar. It had worked down under his shirt to the small of his back, and for most of it that was what he felt.

One of the grandmothers was the boy's. She came back to the table the next morning and sat in her place and never spoke to Eli again. By the ninth day there was a line in the riverbed that neither side crossed, and nobody had died for three days.

---

On the twelfth day an elven ship came down out of the sky, and a woman walked down its ramp.

She was taller than any elf he'd met, armoured, young-looking in the way that meant nothing with elves. Her sword hung at her hip in a pale, faintly ribbed sheath, and the sheath shifted against her leg when she walked, not quite in time with her. The seedship elves straightened as she passed and stopped talking.

"They call her the Queen," Jon said quietly beside him. "Like on the board. The strongest piece in the box. Nothing to do with who her mother was."

She stopped in front of Eli and took in his face and his hands, and her mouth curled a little.

"You saved four hundred of my king's people," she said, "with a folding table. My king has heard of you. He hears you are going to the Tower."

Eli looked at Jon. Jon was looking at the seedship.

"My king has sworn to kill the man in that Tower," said the Queen. "So has every elf alive. In all the ages he has sat in it, not one of us has passed its door. Now a human walks toward it, a human who can talk his way across a riverbed, and my king would like me to walk behind him." Her eyes were flat. "No one said I must like it."

She went past all of them to their ramp. At the top the dark elf was standing in the hatch, and she stepped back out of it without a word and let her by. Jon was at the foot of the ramp. The Queen didn't slow.

"They tell me you answered well," she said.

"I always do," said Jon.

She took the best bunk. It had been Doc's. Doc moved his bag out without a word, and then with several.

---

That night in the galley Eli asked Jon about the sword. He'd been watching the sheath all afternoon.

"Grown," said Jon. He was peeling something with a small knife. "When they catch one of the hell-born outside, they grow a blade out of her. It takes about a year. She's alive for most of it; it holds better that way." He thought about it. "It's a very good sword."

The dark elf was in the galley doorway. Eli didn't know how long she'd been there. He turned in his chair, and it was empty.

Her cabin door was shut when he went past it. He stood outside it a while. Then he went on.

She didn't come out for two days. On the second he sat down on the deck in the corridor with his back to the bulkhead beside her door and said nothing, and after an hour her voice came through it, low down, as though she were sitting on the floor on the other side.

"I can feel her," she said. "Close. Loud."

A long time.

"From the sword, nothing. I knew there'd be nothing." The deck hummed. "There's nobody there."

He waited. There wasn't any more.

He found the Queen at dusk at the top of the ramp, watching the seedship elves fill barrels at the colony's well under the line. He chose the words on the way up the corridor, and he said them carefully. The crew decks were narrow. If she would keep the blade aft when she could, off them—

"No," said the Queen. She didn't stop walking.

On the third day the dark elf came out. Her hands weren't quite steady. In the corridor she passed the Queen with her back to the bulkhead, and she looked at the sheath the whole way past, and never once at the Queen. The Queen never looked at her at all. After that everyone else learned to wait until one of them had gone by.

---

The ship sat in the riverbed another week while the seedship took on water, a barrel at a time, with the grandmothers counting every one.

The Queen sparred with him in the hold every day and put him on the deck every day, without malice and without effort, and walked off while he was still getting up. She called him *the human*. "The human is bleeding on the floor," she told Doc once, on her way out, as she might have mentioned a leak.

"Why her?" Eli asked Jon one night, when his ribs hurt too much to sleep.

"Mika sent her," said Jon. "Mika's very much in charge." He patted Eli's arm. "I'm glad he did, though. She's better than you, and she hates you. You'll come on twice as fast."

Late, in the galley, with the dregs of the tea gone cold, the dark elf went past the open hatch with her pack on her back.

She didn't look in. He heard her bare feet go down the corridor, and then on the ramp. Past the ramp there was the riverbed and the line in it, and past that the dunes, and the dead under their sheets, and then more dunes to the edge of the world and nothing in any of them. He took hold of the edge of the table. He didn't get up.

He counted. He lost it in the usual place and started again.

An hour, near enough. Feet on the ramp, coming up. She passed the hatch going the other way, and she didn't have the pack.

In the morning she ate across from him with her arm round her plate, as before. He never found out where the hour had gone.

---

The giant was Eli's idea. A war needed ships, and the giants built the cathedral ships for the dragons and sold them and kept to themselves, fewer every century, everyone said so, and Jon knew one who would talk to a human.

From orbit the forge world was black rock under a red sky, and its yards held a dozen cathedral ships in their cradles, each the length of a city, ribbed and pale and half-made. Feed lines ran up into the cradles from a ring of dwarven bulk haulers in low orbit. From the shuttle window Eli saw what was in them. Orcs, single file, walking up the long ribbed tubes into the half-made hulls. None came back down. He watched until the shuttle turned and the window filled with rock.

The giant met them in a hall so big it had its own weather. Cloud hung under the roof, and at the far end, where nobody stood, a thin rain was falling on the stone and running off down channels cut for it. Sitting, he was the height of a four-storey house. When he spoke, Eli felt it in the soles of his feet.

"Jon," he said.

"Hello, old friend."

"I am not your friend," said the giant. "But I am friendly." He looked down at the rest of them. "Sit, little ones."

Doc sat. Teo sat, with his bad leg out in front of him. Eli stayed on his feet. He asked for passage, and a ship, and the giants' old quarrel with the dragons turned on the one man who sat above them all. The giant heard him out in silence.

"Do you know what those are made of?" he said. "Once, our own dead. Families. A grandmother in the keel, her sons in the ribs. There are not enough of us left to die, so now we buy. The dwarves bring them by the hauler-load, and they walk in, and the hull closes on them, and they stay. That is what makes it a ship and not a shell." The rain went on at the far end of the hall. "Every hull that leaves Hurrunaal goes to a dragon. We build them for the dragons, so the dragons will kill each other. You humans are their favourite pupils." He looked at Eli. "And why would I lend a ship to a human?"

"Because I'm the Emperor's son," Eli said.

Behind him the Queen went very still.

The giant didn't move. Then he told them about the golden age: the giants' laws and the giants' peace, and the dragons who had been their seconds and decided they would rule better. On betrayal day the emperor's closest advisor, a dragon, walked into the heart of the capital and breathed it to ash. In the last moment of his life the emperor took his whole city, every body and every soul in it, and shaped it into a shield.

"What was left is the Tower," said the giant. "Your people took it from the dragons, and your father built his seat in it. Your father's house. Do not call it a building. The souls of that day are still in its walls, all of them, run together."

Eli hadn't called it anything.

The giant looked at him a long while, without hurry, as he might have looked at a bill.

"A hostage is worth what the other side would pay," he said. "I have read your father's posters." The rain at the end of the hall. "Keep you, and we feed you. Let you go, and we lose nothing."

He leaned down until his face filled the air in front of Eli.

"And you would like me to send giants to knock on its door. No."

A servant showed them out by a side door. It was the one they used for deliveries. Jon stayed behind in the hall, and the great doors shut on him and the giant together.

---

The Queen came for him that night in the dark corridor outside the hall.

He didn't see the first cut. Cold. A line of it, across his ribs. The second he felt coming, the air moving ahead of the blade, the moth, and he put a wall of hard air in its way. The blade flexed against the wall and slid round it. He got his grip on her wrist. She broke it with a twist he didn't follow. Her knee in his chest. The floor. Her foot on his throat, and the pale edge an inch from his eye.

"His son," she said. Her voice was level. "My king sent me to guard the son of the man who—"

She stopped. The blade trembled. He had never seen it do that.

"He knew. My king knew, and sent me anyway, and did not tell me."

Eli looked down the corridor for Jon. Empty. At the end of it the hall doors were shut.

"Then ask him," Eli said. It came out a croak round her foot. "The relay's on the ship. If he says kill me, you've lost a day."

She looked down at him. Then the blade moved, very fast, and opened his cheek from ear to jaw, and she stepped back and sheathed it, and the sheath closed over it like a mouth.

"So you remember who you are," she said.

---

Doc stitched him on the ship, the ribs and then the cheek, and told him it would scar. He didn't make a joke. He didn't mention a goat. Eli lay there waiting for the goat the whole time, and it didn't come.

Jon came back near dawn, saw the stitches and asked what had happened, and when Eli told him he said, "Ah." He lifted a hand toward Eli's face, then put it in his coat pocket.

"The yard wants the berth back by morning," he said to Teo, brisk. "Can we?"

Teo said they could.

"What did you talk about," said Eli. "All night."

"Old times." Jon turned. "Teo, is there any breakfast?"

Eli put his fingers on the stitches and went down them, ear to jaw. Twenty-six. Doc had done them very small. A side door and twenty-six stitches: that was what the name had bought. He wasn't going to spend it again.

She sent the message from the relay before they were out of the system. The reply came in eleven hours. Eli never saw it. He saw her face when she read it. Then she went down to the training deck and put her fist through the bulkhead twice, and came back with her knuckles split to the bone and her face exactly as it had been.

"Continue," she said, to no one. Her mouth hardly opened on it.

Eli thought of the man on the stones in Seluvane, and didn't know why.

After that she stopped calling him *the human*, or anything at all. She sparred with him every day and put him on the deck as before. But she no longer walked off while he was getting up. She stood and watched until he was on his feet, and then she put him down again.

## SIX · The Strike

They were a day out from the forge world, with the stitches in Eli's cheek still pulling every time he swallowed, when every band on the bridge went to the same picture.

A long chamber, ribbed, built along the curve of a ship's spine. Crew strapped in at their stations, scaled and wingless and quick in the hands, and they kept looking at one another. A demonstration, the caption said, by House Vaskhir.

At the centre of the chamber stood the cradle. Eli knew it before he knew why. His duelling master from the dragon schools had drawn one for him on a slate, the winter he was fourteen: a body poured its whole strength in, and the ship gathered it up and threw it across the dark in one line. A focusing chamber, the dwarves who built them said. Crews took turns in it, a few shots each and then a day on their backs.

The ship's master was climbing into it himself.

"Master." One of the crew, half out of his straps. "The chamber is not rated for—"

"Then it will learn." He pulled the last strap tight with his teeth. "For my grandfather's grandfather, who died on betrayal day. Aim for the forge-heart."

He opened his mouth. The picture shook. The last of it was the long bones of his neck going one after another while he held the line anyway, and then the band went grey.

On the other screen, from orbit, the line. Straight as a rule. It went in at the forge-heart and the forge-heart was gone, and then the yards, the half-made hulls buckling in their berths one after another like ribs under a boot, and the feed lines from the haulers with everyone still walking up them. Then the crust came up in a ring and kept coming, red rock taller than mountains rolling out across the face of the world.

The yard wants the berth back by morning, Jon had said.

Nobody spoke. The dark elf was at Eli's shoulder, close enough that he could hear her breathing, slow and even, and then not. Teo was crying. He didn't wipe his face.

"The giant," said Teo. "Was he— did he get— he'd have been in the hall, wouldn't he, he'd—"

Nobody answered. Jon stood at the back of the bridge with a cup of tea he hadn't touched.

"His crew will have cooked," he said. "In their scales, at their stations. Ask any of them and they'd have told you they were dragons." He looked into the cup. "Nobody warned the giants' city either, on betrayal day. They'll be saying that to each other all week."

Three days later the giants came out. Hundreds of cathedral ships, out of yards on worlds nobody had known the giants still held. Eli counted them on the display until he lost count, somewhere in the four hundreds, and started again, and lost it in the same place.

*There are not enough of us left to die.* He had stood in that hall and watched the giant's face the whole time he said it.

A dwarven relay had the fleets hitting every dragon flag in reach, and every port that flew one, Earth among them. Then it went on to prices.

"So where do we," said Teo, "there's no ship now, is there, there's no giant, where do we even—"

"Back for the master," said Eli.

---

Jon got off at the rim port before the ramp was all the way down, his bag on his shoulder.

"Rig town," he said, looking out at the dock lights. "Companies, ledgers, cameras on the field. I'll find you."

It had taken them a month to get there through a sector coming apart. Rimtown was emptier. Three of the five companies had pulled their rigs and their boys and gone to the war, and their wells stood capped along the rim with nobody shelling them. The glass looked bigger without the guns.

The master was in his temple. He watched Eli come up the broken steps and didn't get up.

"You're better," he said.

Eli sat down across from him on the cold floor where he had sat for five months, and folded his legs the same way.

"You said nobody told you how far he got," he said. "Your master. I'm going up those stairs. Come and find out."

The master looked at him a long time.

"That's a cheap trick," he said. "Get out of my temple."

---

"Hear that?" Teo was on his back under the drive panel. "That whine. It's the coil, or the thing next to the coil, I can patch it but it won't hold, it wants a part, and the part's dwarven, two systems off. Four days." He slid out. "Or we go now and it holds. Probably holds."

"We wait," said Eli.

He didn't go back up the steps. He sat on the ramp in the cold four nights, and slept a little in the afternoons, and didn't look at the steps at all.

On the third day the last two rig students came down from the temple with their bags, in company straps, and walked across the field to a recruiter's hull with its ramp down. One of them looked back once. Nobody came down after them. The temple door stood open at the top of the steps on nothing.

The part came on the fourth night. Teo had the drive warm by morning. They had the ramp half up and the rain coming in under it when the master came across the field with a bag on his shoulder: old leather, split at the corners, the one that had hung on a peg by the temple door for five months and that Eli had never once seen him touch.

Eli opened his mouth. The old man went past him up the ramp.

"Your drive's whining," he said from inside. "Get somebody to look at it."

---

The cutter caught them over a gas giant whose name none of them ever learned.

The grapple hit like a dropped building. The hold went sideways and Eli went with it, across the deck and into a stanchion ribs first, and something in his side gave with a small wet sound he heard from the inside.

He lay there. Breathing came in pieces.

"How did they find us?" Teo, from the pilot's chair, shouting. "We squeezed, we were squeezed the whole way, how did they—"

"You ordered a part," said the master.

Across the passage Jon's bunk stood empty, the blanket folded. It had been folded since Tessid.

Eli got to the bridge along the walls. The hail light was blinking. Before it opened the Queen stepped out of the screen's line, two paces, to the bulkhead beside it, and stood there with her back to the metal. Eli saw her do it. Nobody else was looking at her.

The screen opened. A grey coat. A narrow face he had sat across a table from in Seluvane, three times through.

"Whose are you?" said the inquisitor.

On a panel beside him he had their faces up, the four who had come through his gate, Jon's among them, smiling. Under them a scanned sheet in a dwarvish hand, and in its margin, by Eli's face, a ring in grease pencil.

Eli had last seen that pencil in a breast pocket, on a moon with no water on it.

He didn't answer the question.

"The hull," said the inquisitor, "and the hell-born you carry. She will be pardoned. You may go on."

Everyone looked at Eli. The dark elf didn't move. She was standing where she always stood, by the open hatch.

"No," said Eli.

The inquisitor wrote something in his ledger. "Then you may stay aboard," he said, and the screen went dark.

"Stay aboard and he tows us to his station," said the master. He was looking out of the forward port at the grapple, the cable of it running a ship's length out to the cutter, and he looked at it a long time. Then he looked at the lifeboat hatch.

The Queen was still against the bulkhead. One step would put her in the screen's eye, the king's own piece. Eli watched her not take it. She didn't look at the dark elf.

They took what they could carry, water first. The Queen put her hand on the release and looked at nobody and pulled it.

"They don't board what they can starve," said the dark elf.

Through the lifeboat's port Eli watched the cutter take up the slack and tow the low black hull away into the bands of the gas giant, slowly, until it was too small to be anything.

---

On the seventeenth day they shared the last cup by count, a mouthful each, the master holding it. Doc made a joke about it. Nobody laughed, and he made another one.

On the nineteenth there was a hull on the scope.

A salvage tug, old and slow, with a crew of three. Its master hauled them aboard by their collars one at a time and gave them water out of a bucket.

"Paid to sweep here," he said, when Teo asked. "Didn't say for what."

Eli put that away with the rest.

Jon was on the dock at the next port with a bag of peaches. He was sorry. He said so, and looked it, and held Eli by both arms and saw how he was standing and said, "Ribs," and was sorry about that too.

"Nineteen days," said Eli. "Where were you?"

"Elves."

A drone over a river gorge, and Jon asleep. *A station keeps ledgers.* The rim port. The grapple. *They tell me you answered well.*

"You're never there when the grey coats are," said Eli.

"No," said Jon. "I'm not." He held out the bag. "Teo. How's that leg? Really, now."

It hurt Eli to reach. He took a peach anyway.

---

His ribs took four months to knit, and the war went on round them the whole time.

They crossed two systems in the hold of a dwarven arms freighter, sleeping on crates of flesh rifles that twitched whenever the drive spun up. GROWN FROM CERTIFIED CLONE STOCK, the stencil said. NO SOUL. One crate had split. The rifle in it had a fingernail in the grip, small and clean, where a thumb would go.

Eli looked for the Dry on the freighter's feed every night for a month. One night it was there. An elven fleet had come down for the seedship and cleared the dunes on both sides of the riverbed for a landing field. There was no colony in the picture. The line in the riverbed was still there. He turned it off.

At a dwarven port two systems on, the low black hull sat on the next berth along with fresh paint down its flank. Doc read the new name off it. *Fair Weight.* He didn't say what he thought of that one. The dark elf looked at it, and then somewhere else, and kept looking there until they were past.

At the port gate the guards stopped everyone in the line and went through bags and coats, Doc's and the master's too. One held a poster up beside Eli's face and looked from one to the other, chin and jaw. The poster had the chin scar. It didn't have the cheek. He waved Eli on. On the far side Teo was already waiting, with a crate on his shoulder he'd carried through behind them, eyes down. Nobody had stopped him. Nobody had looked.

The posters were on every world after that, and somebody had always got to them first with a key or a knife: the face scratched out to the backing, the seal left standing over the hole.

On a human world whose name he didn't catch they came out of a market into a square where a crowd was hanging a gnome from a lamp standard. He'd stolen souls, somebody said. It was the week's thing to say. The crowd sang to the Emperor's god while he kicked, under a broadcast wall with the seal where a face should be. The wall ran on a cored mind; it said so on the plate. Across the square was Eli's own face on a post, the eyes dug out.

His hand was half up. Teo was watching it. He put it down.

"Poor thing," said Doc, at the back with them. "They always get the wrong one."

The master stood beside Doc through all of it and didn't move at all.

On the next freighter a boarding party came over the side, and Doc fought, badly, slow and short of wind, with a reach that would knock a rifle aside and not much more. Then he finished the man with the troll's stick and complained about his knees. Afterwards he sat bandaging a dwarf's leg and talking to him about goats, and the master watched him, and the master's face went still and strange, as if he were listening for something in another room.

Doc looked up. "Hurt yourself?"

"No," said the master, and walked off rubbing his eyes.

---

They went through hell once, on a human transit hull, because there was no other way across.

When the hull dropped through, the dark elf went rigid. She sat with her back to the bulkhead and her eyes shut, and found his hand without looking, and held it.

The light had gone out, she said afterwards. The homeworld. And in its place the world outside the hull, hundreds of millions of her own kind, everyone she had ever got away from. Pressing.

Over the eleven hours she broke two of his fingers, one at a time. The first went early, with a sound he felt in his elbow. The second took longer. He didn't make a sound, and he didn't take the hand away. By the eighth hour he couldn't have said which finger was which, and there were three hours left.

When they came out the other side she let go and looked at his hand, and then at him.

Doc set the fingers. The next morning in the galley Eli flexed the splinted hand at her across the table, and it hurt, and he did it again.

She told him her name. Nobody else heard her say it. He carried it round with him all that day, and his fingers throbbed under the splint the whole time, and he didn't notice them until night.

---

Jon had left them two ports back, for Earth's edge. Somebody had to find them a way down, he said.

The dragon came for them on an airless moon where the freighter had put down for water.

It came down out of the black with a quarter-mile of wing spread over them, hunting gunrunners, and it had found one. Its first breath took the drive section off at the root. No sound. Nothing to carry it. Eli felt it come up through his boots. Its second breath took the dwarven crew, who had run for the hills.

"It's young," said the master on the suit band, very calm. "Make it breathe. It can't do that forever. Make it waste them."

He walked out from under the hull into the open dust and stood there.

The Queen went the other way, across the flat in plain sight, so fast she was in two places at once. The head swung after her. Breath, and a miss, and a canyon cut across the moon where she'd been, and the lip of it threw her twenty yards. She got up with a hand on her side.

The next breath came for the master and didn't get there. It bent off him into the ground like water off a blade, and a mile of moon went up behind him in a slow fan that never came down. His nose was bleeding inside his faceplate. The next one, his ears. The one after that put him on one knee, and he stayed there with his hand up.

Doc was in the freighter's shadow the whole time, out of the line, throwing rocks, his voice on the band gone high and cracked.

The dark elf came out of the dust under the head with one hand raised and the fingers a little curled, and closed them. A fistful of scales went out of the jaw into nothing, and she went down on her knees. Then she was up again, under the jaw, a step late, and laid her palm flat on the scales of its throat, and the whole head jerked aside. Not far enough.

Teo had left the master's cover. He was out on the open flat with a flesh rifle against his shoulder, firing into the flank, and then he was running back past the freighter's shadow.

"Do something!" Eli heard him on the band. "Doc! Do something!"

"Saving myself for something special," said Doc, puffing.

The line went past Teo a second later, out where the master wasn't looking, close enough to take the skin off his left hand and forearm through the suit.

After that the breaths came slower, and ragged, and short. The dragon's sides heaved. It stood on the dust with its head down, swaying.

"Now," said the master.

Eli had stood at the edge of it with his hands at his sides. He reached.

He went slowly and blind, as he had for the moth. Through the scales, which was like feeling through a mountain. Through muscle and bone and the lungs, huge and hot. He found the heart, the size of the flyer and labouring, and in it the narrow place where everything went through.

He closed his hand.

The dragon lifted its head and looked straight at him across the dust, as if it had only now noticed he was there. Then it fell, and the moon shook under his knees, and the dust went up over it in the airless dark and stayed up.

Eli was on his knees with his nose running inside his faceplate. The master was on his, ten yards off, blood dried black in both ears.

"That," said the master, "was fine."

Teo sat against the landing strut with his burned arm held out from his body, the suit seal hissing where the skin had come off with the cloth, and Doc was already cutting it open. Afterwards Teo went into the dead freighter and set off its beacon with his good hand. Dwarven beacons ran on the open band, so that anyone could sell a rescue. Nobody asked who would hear it.

---

They lived in the freighter's hold on dwarven rations. Over the hatch the beacon went red, and dark, and red.

Teo wouldn't let Doc dress his arm alone. He said it to Eli the first night, low, while Doc was laying out the dressings: would Eli stay, or the master, just while, just so—

"Of course," said Doc, easily, unrolling gauze.

Eli stayed, every time. When he shut his eyes the dragon lifted its head off the dust and looked at him.

On the fourth day the hold's one port went dark.

Something was lying alongside the moon, bigger than the moon's horizon. Eli put his face to the port and saw ribbing, pale and curved, running away out of sight both ways, and he knew it. His father's ship had hung at anchor over the capital every day of his life, filling the window of his old rooms.

They counted it out loud. The master couldn't stand. The Queen's ribs were taped. Teo had one hand. Six rifles, and the troll's stick. Out there, a ship that could take the face off a moon.

"Not this one," said the master, from the floor of the hold, to nobody.

Eli opened the hatch himself.

The lord's crew came in suited: humans, dwarves, an orc with the dead hunter's ruined wing-tip folded over his shoulders, and elves. The elves wore collars, plain grey bands with a mark stamped in the front. The dark elf looked at the collars and said one word.

"Pardoned."

On the bridge the lord sat on his throne at the head of everything, and his crew went down to their stations in front of him. Behind the throne, up on a raised gallery, ran the ring of the focusing chamber: two dozen cradles, most of them empty, the straps on the empty ones worn pale. Eli knew what it was before they strapped him in. He had seen one on a slate, and once since, breaking a man's neck.

From the gallery he could see over the throne's back: the folded wings, the long neck, and past them the open deck and the stations and the crew at them.

The dark elf let them buckle her in. She didn't look at Eli. The lord never once turned his head to see what had been wired in behind him.

The ship didn't leave. For days the sound of the hunter coming aboard came up through the hull, a quarter-mile of wing cut and winched in a piece at a time. A dragon's dead went home.

---

By the fourth day he had it ready: what they were worth, in what order, and the first sentence. He had learned the bridge from the gallery. He had held his cradle steady when it hummed, and been sick only once.

The bridge turned.

That was how he had it afterwards: every head at the stations turning at once toward the open deck between them and the throne. Someone was standing there.

He was taller than anything on the bridge but the lord. He didn't stoop. The ceiling had been grown for someone his size. At the far end, past the stations, a seam in the hull was closing, slowly, from the edges in. Nobody was near it.

He looked up at the dragon like a man come to collect, and opened his mouth.

The dragon breathed first.

Eli saw the great head go back and the jaw open over the back of the throne, and nothing come out of it that he could see. The air between the jaw and the god wavered once and lay still. Behind Eli the cradles stayed dark.

The god looked down at himself. His chest. His hands.

Nothing. Eli's cradle hummed against his spine the way the whole ship hummed. Somebody at the stations coughed.

Then the orc.

He was at the back, at a station against the hull, the one who'd carried the wing-tip in. He had spent four days scraping scale off the deck with a blade and humming something with no tune. He put his hand up to his face, and his face went grey and slack and old under it, and then he burst. All at once. He was standing at his station, and then his clothes were coming down on to the deck with dust going up out of them, and something wet under the dust.

"That's not heat," said the master, very low.

Then the humans.

A woman at the helm turned in her seat to look at the heap where the orc had been, and her hair was white before she'd finished turning. The man beside her stood up and got old standing. His back went. His hands went to knots on the board. When he opened his mouth to shout his teeth came out of it. They went down at their stations one after another, a dozen of them, the skin drawing in and then no skin, until what was left in the seats was dust in a uniform. Eli watched every one. He'd have been in one of those seats if the lord had wanted him there.

Teo was crying again, and again not wiping it.

Then the dwarves.

They lasted. One had time to get out of his harness and take three steps toward the throne, and his beard went white and came away on the third step, and he kept walking. He didn't make the fourth.

Then the elves.

They went slowest. Every one of them turned at their stations and looked up at the gallery, at the same place, at the two elves in the cradle ring. The dark elf's hands were locked on her straps.

"Still there," she said. "All of them."

They got old. Slow enough to watch, slow enough that Eli saw a collar go loose round a throat as the throat thinned inside it. The strap under the dark elf's right hand folded in on itself with a small flat sound and was gone. That was all it did. There was nothing in reach to use it on. The Queen's face didn't move. Her eyes were on the stations and they stayed there.

The elves went down.

"Gone," said the dark elf.

The god hadn't moved. He stood on the open deck in the dust of the whole crew with his hands at his sides, and he smiled up at the dragon.

"Your crew is dead," he said. The deck carried it. "How will you get home?"

The dragon didn't answer. From the gallery Eli could see the side of the great jaw. The scales along it were wet.

Then the god trembled.

It went through him once, all the way down, and he stopped smiling. He looked at his hand. He turned it over and looked at the palm, and back, and the fingers shook, and he watched them shake.

He turned his head and looked behind him at the stations. He looked at them in order: the heap at the back by the hull, the humans in their seats, the dwarf on the deck three steps short of the throne, the elves. As far as the elves. Then down at his hand again, and the skin on the back of it had gone fine as paper.

It took him longer than any of them. He got old standing up, as the man at the helm had, but slowly, every part of it there to be seen, and he never once looked away from his hands. Toward the end he was on his knees. Then there was dust on the deck where he had knelt, a great deal of it, settling.

Doc had watched the god the whole way down. He was leaning as far forward as his straps let him, with the bright, attentive face he'd worn over the miner's arm.

Dust at every station.

The dragon breathed out. Then, for the first time since they had come aboard, he turned his head and looked behind him, at the ring.

---

He looked along it at what was left. Then at Eli. He looked at Eli the way you look at weather.

They unstrapped him. His legs went, and he caught the gallery rail. Below him the dust in the nearest seat had kept the shape of whoever had sat there, shoulders and all, until air from a vent moved it.

His first sentence was no use now. He used the god's.

"You'll want a crew to get home," he said. "There are six of us. I'll take a station. He can fly anything with a seat, one hand or two. Those two can hold a station each, and the old man. The gnome keeps everyone alive. Take us as far as your house port. Your house will have hands waiting there, your own, and you won't want to feed strangers past that. We won't ask you to. The voyage. That's all."

The lord's head turned back to the front.

That was the answer. Somebody came along the ring and unbuckled the rest.

The dark elf didn't look at him. She took the station farthest from the throne, and sat in it, and didn't speak to him again.

Nine days to the house port, at the stations, in the dust of the crew, inside a hull grown out of dead giants. Teo flew one-handed and talked to the helm under his breath. The Queen held an elven station and didn't say whose it had been. She sat down on the dust in the seat and didn't brush it off.

Doc didn't talk for a whole day. Nobody had ever seen him go a day. "He hasn't said anything, has he," Teo whispered that night, "all day, not a goat, not anything, is he—" and in the morning Doc started a story about a goat over the ration tins as if nothing had happened.

The lord never asked their names.

---

Jon was on the quay when they came down the ramp, with a hired hop behind him and a bag of peaches.

The dark elf was first off. She went past Jon without looking at him and stopped by Eli.

"Next time ask me first," she said, and walked on.

Jon looked up at the hull a long while, all the way along the ribs to where they went out of sight. He didn't ask what had happened aboard. Eli waited for him to ask.

He put his hand on Eli's shoulder. "I knew you would," he said.

"How?"

Jon opened his mouth and shut it. He looked up at the hull again.

"I don't know," he said. He took his hand away. "Teo. Let me see that arm."

Eli stood on the quay a while with a peach in his hand that he didn't remember taking, and didn't eat it, and didn't ask again.

---

A month on, on another freighter, the hull took a hit and the cold got into the hold where forty chipped orcs were being shipped to the Legion yards.

Teo found them. He came back to the bunks shaking and couldn't speak, and sat on the end of Eli's bunk with his good hand between his knees and got it out in pieces. They'd been told to stand in rows. Most of them had frozen standing, in rows. Near the warmest bulkhead the rows had leaned in toward it as far as the chips would let them, and some of them had got an arm round the next one.

Eli didn't go and look. He stayed in his bunk.

Jon went and looked.

That evening Teo went down the passage toward the purser's hatch with his savings in a twist of cloth, the one that lived in his boot. Jon was in the passage before he got there, and put a hand on his shoulder, and sat him down on a bench under the lights.

He sat with him an hour, an arm round him. Eli heard it from the bunk room: the first dead Jon had ever seen, a long time ago; how it never stopped mattering; how that was how you knew you were still a person. Teo cried into his coat. Something about a list, and his mother.

"They'll ask me where he is," Teo said. "If I go back. And I'll tell them. I know I'll tell them."

Jon said his parents must be very proud of him.

When Eli came out into the passage it was nearly done.

"You'll stay?" said Jon.

A long time.

"Yeah."

The purser came by on his rounds. Jon, without taking his arm off Teo, booked the whole crew's passage to the next system, in that same hold.

"At a discount," he said. "They won't be needing it."

The purser wrote it down.

---

Eli stepped out in front of him in the passage.

"You held him for an hour," he said. "Then you sold their deaths for a fare. You talked him into staying. For what?"

Jon smiled at him, kindly, as if he were glad to be asked.

"You didn't go and look," he said.

Eli said nothing.

"The man at the fence," said Jon, gently. "Nine at the crater, chained together. That boy at the water line, and his grandmother back at the table next morning." He waited. "I held Teo for an hour, and I bought a fare. Both things are true. Would you like me to pick one?"

Eli's hand opened and closed on the splint. It hurt. He did it again.

Jon patted his arm. "You're doing beautifully," he said, and went back to Teo.

Eli stayed where he was. After a while he went to his bunk and lay down, and the freighter went on toward Earth with him in it.

The orcs were delivered at the Legion yards eight days later, at the same weight, and signed for.

---

On the last night before Earth they sat in the dark of a borrowed lander's cabin. It was cold. Eli's mended fingers ached with it, and he kept opening and closing them, and stopped when he noticed, and started again.

Earth was under the port, the night side. He had lived his whole life on it and never once seen it from above.

"When we get there," he said. "What do we do?"

Jon looked up. "We tire him out."

"I didn't come all this way to tire him out. I came to take the chair."

"I know," said Jon. "That's why." He held up a hand as if to count on it, and didn't. "Your father has sat in that chair longer than there are records of anyone sitting anywhere. People have tried to kill him. Every kind of people, with every kind of—"

"So we're not even," said Teo, "we're not even trying to— so we don't kill him?"

"So we don't. We go in, and every one of you does the best thing you've ever done, and he has to answer every single one." Jon looked round the cabin at them. "He'll keep a hand for me. You get the other one. At the end of it he's tired. Tired men can be held. Tired men can be talked to. Tired men give up chairs."

"In his own house," said the master, from the dark. "In the strongest place on earth."

"It's strong for everyone," said Jon. "It makes everyone bigger. It doesn't make anyone less tired."

"And when he's tired," said Eli, "I talk to him. That part's mine."

Jon looked at him. "Then we'll see," he said.

Later, when they were lying down, Teo said very low, "How do you talk to him? The first time. What do you call him?"

Eli told him, as the stewards had drilled it into him at eight for a man he would never be let near: the title, every word of it; the bow from the waist and not the neck; eyes on the second step, never the first; then whatever you'd come to ask, short. He didn't ask why Teo wanted it.

Teo said it over under his breath, and got it wrong, and started again from the title, until his breathing changed.

---

The master got up after that, slowly, and stood looking down at his bag.

"Your master—" Jon began, gently.

"Don't," said the master. "You didn't know him."

Jon shut his mouth.

The master picked up the bag and went to the hatch and opened it, and stood in it with the cold coming in round him. Nobody spoke. Eli thought of three things to say and said none of them.

In the end the master shut the hatch and came back and shoved the bag under his seat, and didn't speak for the rest of the night.

Somewhere in the dark Doc laughed softly.

"Sorry," he said. "I was thinking about my brother's goat."

## SEVEN · The Tower

Earth was one city, edge to edge, and it was on fire.

The Tower stood at the heart of it, pearl-white, its top lost, and the war broke round its base like surf. Giant ships hung in the upper air. Dragons burned lines through the cloud. The Legion's guns fired from every roof, and down among the roofs one Council tower still had its flags up, a whole row of them, every one alight.

Jon had the lander's door open and the wind had his coat. He looked back along the cabin at them once, and stepped out.

They went after him. The master's bag stayed under his seat.

They fell no faster than he did. A mile of it. Smoke, and then no smoke, and a gun on a roof coming round to follow something else, and a dragon's line going by so close that Eli tasted metal, and Teo's mouth wide open with nothing coming out of it, and Doc with his eyes screwed shut and both arms round his bag.

Overhead a cathedral ship broke in half.

Eli saw the ribs part. He knew them. He had held a station inside ribs like those, and now they were coming down in pieces the size of streets, burning. Jon moved one hand, and the wreck went past on both sides close enough to touch. On the far side the master had both hands up and blood on his lip already, turning a sheet of burning hull off Teo and Doc, and then another behind it.

The spar came from Jon's side. Edge-on, out of the smoke, as long as a mast.

Jon's eyes went to it. They went away again.

It opened the Queen's shoulder to the bone and was gone. She made no sound. Jon didn't look at her, then or the rest of the way down, and he didn't speak.

They came down on a terrace two-thirds of the way up, hard, most of them on their knees. In the wall of the Tower a door stood open. There was no guard on it. There was nothing on it at all.

The Queen looked at the door. Blood ran off her fingers onto the white stone.

"Then he was right," she said, to no one.

Doc bound the shoulder with her standing. He had to reach up to do it. He told her about a goat the whole time, and she let him do that too.

One step inside and the war was gone. Eli looked back. The sky burned in the doorway with ships coming apart in it, and none of it made a sound. His ears rang anyway. His legs were still braced to fall.

A pale corridor, seamless, curving away. Light from nowhere. The air didn't stir when he breathed it. His whole life under this thing, its shadow in his bath water, and he had never been through one of its doors.

"We're not where we were," said the master. He was wiping his lip with his thumb.

Eli reached without meaning to. It went further than it ever had, and fine, so fine that he had the threads in Teo's sleeve from across the corridor, one at a time, the weave of them. He looked at his hands. Same hands.

Doc laid his palm flat on the wall and held it there.

"Hello, all of you," he said to it, softly. He took the hand away and looked at it.

Jon was already walking.

---

"You let that spar through," Eli said, to his back.

Jon walked on.

Eli waited for the smile over the shoulder, or something about food, or the hand. He said it again, louder. Teo looked at him, and at the dark wet binding on the Queen's shoulder, and at Jon's back going away round the curve.

The rooms along the corridor weren't puzzles until you were in them. The first had a door in every wall, and every door opened back into the same room. Teo chalked them, a cross on each, with the chalk in his good hand. The crosses came back on the wrong doors. He chalked two more, and then he put the chalk away.

The master sat down in the middle of the floor and shut his eyes. He sat long enough that Doc started on a story and gave it up. Then he got to his feet and walked to the one door nobody had tried, because it was the one they had come in by.

"Here," he said.

A room full of chairs, next, that nobody could leave until everyone was sitting, and it was always one chair short. He shut his eyes for that one too.

"Like this," he said. They sat. He stayed where he was on the floor, cross-legged, and the door opened.

Teo nudged Eli and tipped his head. The old man was coming out of the room of chairs with his eyes half shut and his mouth not quite straight.

Eli knew that face. He'd had it himself, mornings, on a cold floor, with the guns going far off along the rim.

He had it, and it went. The spar. The sound the Queen hadn't made.

---

The third room had a far door and nothing else in it. The master stopped just inside, with his eyes on that door.

"Something up there's waiting," he said.

Then the ceiling started down. An inch a breath, a whole floor's weight of stone, in no hurry. The master shut his eyes. He was a while.

"No trick," he said. "It's only weight. There's a slot by the door that stays open while something holds the roof."

He looked at Jon.

Jon was looking at the far door.

The master rubbed his eyes. "Well," he said to Eli. "That one's yours."

Eli held it.

With the Tower behind him it was possible, just. He had it in his teeth and his spine and the backs of his eyes. Teo went through the slot, and the Queen with her arm strapped across her, and Doc, puffing. The dark elf went through backwards, looking at him. Jon went through without looking back.

The master stopped in the slot. He looked at the stone edge of it, and up at the ceiling, and at Eli underneath with his legs shaking.

"He went alone," the master said.

Eli had no breath to ask who.

"Let it down slow. Don't drop it. Walk out from under."

He did. It met the floor behind him with a sound like a door shutting in another house.

---

The last room was round and white and empty, and the door on its far side wouldn't open. Then it wasn't empty. Something sat in the middle of the floor.

It was hard to look at: a little like a man and a little like a spider and a little like a hole, with too many joints in the wrong order. It sat there as though it always had.

"Visitors," it said, in a dry, creaking voice. "It's been so long." It unfolded a little. "The man upstairs put me here. I came for him once. I built him a room just like this one and put him in it, and he walked in and smiled, and told me he'd had this conversation thousands of times and had got very good at it." It made a sound that might have been laughter. "So now I'm the one in the room. Until a new foe comes in, and loses."

"And if one does?" said the master.

"Then I'm free." Its heads turned toward the rest of them. "Free, in here, with all of you."

"And the door?"

"Opens when the room is done with. One goes in. The rest wait." It settled. "If no one goes in, I take you as you stand. One at a time. I've had a long while to think about how."

The master looked at Jon.

Everyone did. Jon was standing a little apart with his hands in his coat pockets, looking at the beast.

"Can you do it?" said the master.

"Yes," said Jon. "And then I'd have nothing left for upstairs."

He kept his eyes on the beast as he said it. The master nodded.

"I'll go," said the master.

"No," said Eli.

"Be quiet." The old man was watching the beast. "Your room. Nothing leaves it?"

"Nothing."

"No light. No heat. Nothing."

"Nothing," said the beast, with enormous contempt. "It isn't even attached to your universe any more. It's mine. And I'm bound to it until someone loses."

"Swear it," said the master. "Do you swear this room will not allow any energy to leave?"

The beast laughed. "I swear it."

"Again."

"I swear it, old man. Nothing gets out. Not a spark. That's the point."

"Once more."

The beast stopped laughing and looked at him.

"I swear it," it said. "Nothing leaves this room."

"Good," said the master, and the lines in his face eased. "Then nothing reaches them. And nothing gets out. Including you."

He turned to Eli.

"You'll never be fine," he said. "But you're better than you were."

He walked into the middle of the round white room, and the beast unfolded around him, and a wall closed between them and the rest.

A pane, milky and smooth. Nothing could leave that room, not even light. What showed on it was the beast's picture, painted for them to watch: the beast setting out its game, and the master sitting down on the floor cross-legged, as he had sat in the temple every morning, and closing his eyes.

Then the Tower shuddered.

It came up through Eli's boots first, a hum, and the lights along the curve of the corridor behind them dimmed all together. Something went past him toward the pane. It came out of the walls, a long slow draining, and under it there was a thing he had felt once before, on a practice field, the morning a loader was set down like a cup. On the rim it had stopped a loader. Here he couldn't find the bottom of it.

Teo had hold of his sleeve.

In the picture the old man's face went grey, and then slack. Blood at the nose. The ears. The eyes.

The picture began to fail. It failed from the middle outward, where he sat, and Eli never saw the rest of what he did to himself. It frayed and pulled, as if the hand painting it had started to shake. At the rim the beast was stretching, its limbs reaching back for the walls of its own room. The edges curled in toward the centre like paper in a flame. The beast's mouths were open.

The pane went black.

No sound came through, and nothing else. The hum stopped under his boots. The lights came back up.

When the pane cleared, the round white room was empty and the door on the far side stood open. The floor where he had sat was smooth and bare. There wasn't a mark on it.

Eli stood in front of it. A cheap trick, the old man had called it.

The Queen went past him to the door, holding her shoulder, and didn't look at the place on the floor. Jon went after her. Teo still had Eli's sleeve, and didn't seem to know it, and Eli walked him through like that.

---

The stair wound up through the middle of the Tower. Arches opened off it on rooms half seen: racks of grown weapons, pale and ribbed and old, some of them still stirring; a hall of banners from wars nobody had written down. Nobody stopped. He counted them on the stair without meaning to. Six. By the second turn Doc needed the rail.

At the top a door stood open, like the one below.

Jon's hand came down on Eli's shoulder, warm, where it always went. They climbed the last steps that way.

At the threshold he took it off.

Eli went in. The others went in round him. Jon stayed in the door.

The room was enormous and almost empty. The ceiling went up into dark. One wall was window, the whole height of it, and the war turned in the glass without a sound. At the far end there was a dais, and a chair, and a man sitting in it with his head on his right hand.

Older than Eli had let himself expect. Lean. Grey at the temples. Plainly dressed. An intelligent, tired, unremarkable face, and under it the jaw off the post.

Eli had never seen his father's face. Nobody had. He knew it anyway.

Beside the chair stood a younger man in black armour with a ribbed sword at his hip. Human, broad, scarred, built as if he'd been bred for it. He was looking at Eli's face, and then at the face in the chair, and back. His eyes were red at the rims. He looked scared, and angry.

"Jon," said the man in the chair. He was looking past all of them, at the door.

Jon said nothing.

Eli was still on the jaw. On the grey, and whether it would come to him at the temples first too.

Teo was halfway across the floor.

He had gone the way he went through port gates, quick and soft along the edge of things, a little bent, and nobody had looked. He stopped short of the dais. He bowed from the waist and not the neck. Eyes on the second step. He began the title, and his voice was very small in all that room, and it shook, and he didn't lose his place.

The man in the chair said it with him. Every word, in Teo's rhythm, a hair ahead of him by the end, flat, to the door.

They finished it together. Teo's eyes came up off the second step. His mouth was already open on the next part, the thing he had carried across a whole war.

Jon didn't turn his head.

The man in the chair lifted his left hand a little off the arm of it, without looking, and closed it.

Teo's head snapped back as if a great palm had cupped his face, fingers over the eyes and mouth, and he went up off the floor by it. One short wet sound.

Eli reached. It went out of him like a shout, everything he had and everything the Tower would give, and it went at the grip, at the thing round Teo's head, to get in under the fingers and pry. Teo's feet were still moving.

The left hand opened, and Teo dropped. The same hand flicked, backward, and it got to Eli before his reach got anywhere.

Wall.

He was on the floor at the foot of it, fifty feet from where he'd stood, at the end of the great window where the glass stopped and the stone began. Stone was still coming down on him in small pieces. Something in his side had gone again, more than one of them. He felt for his reach and there was nothing in his hands.

Across the floor Teo lay where he had been let fall. His left hand was open on the stone, the stiff one, the one that had never closed right. Like a mean man would put down an unruly dog.

Everything was moving. The Queen's sheath, sliding. Something low gone into the shadow under the window. Doc coming at him across the floor at a trot with his bag bumping.

The man in the chair still had his head on his right hand.

"You brought children," he said, to the door.

## EIGHT · Everyone

The sheath came off like skin.

The blade under it was pale and alive, a long ribbed thing that flexed in her hand. She had one good arm, and she went straight at the chair.

"Yours," the Emperor said to the younger man, without interest. "Go on."

The apprentice came off the dais like a falling wall.

Dust. His head against the wall. Something moving along the foot of the glass, low, under the sill, rags and a thin shoulder. She stopped at him. Her hand found his, and the two fingers, the mended ones, and pressed them once.

"Don't," he said.

She went on along the wall.

Small hands on his ribs. "Lie still. Lie still, now. That's three gone, could be four, don't breathe so much." Doc wasn't looking at him. He was looking at the dais, and his lips were moving, counting something.

A year of her putting him on the deck, and he'd thought he knew. In here she was better. Every block exact. Her blade into the gaps in the apprentice's guard and out again, four cuts in the first ten seconds, and the blades made a soft wet sound when they met. He bled and kept coming. She breathed through her nose, short and even, and Doc's binding on her shoulder went dark. There was a coin on the floor near the foot of the dais, small and dull, a long way from anywhere a pocket would have been.

Something bright turned over in the air on the far side of the chair. A knife, short and curved, thrown flat at the side of the man's neck.

His left hand came off the arm of the chair and met it, and it rang away across the stone. His eyes hadn't left the door. His right hand hadn't come out from under his head.

*He'll keep a hand for me.*

Nothing moved behind the chair after that.

The apprentice looked back at the dais. In the middle of it, bleeding from four places, he looked back.

The Emperor was yawning and resting his head on his hand. He wasn't watching the duel. He was watching the door.

The apprentice's face changed.

He reached down. Into the floor, into the walls. The lights dimmed all together, as they had below, and the floor shivered under Eli's hip, and grit came down off the wall into his mouth. On the dais the Emperor's head came up off his hand.

The apprentice began to shine. Light came up through his skin and his blade went white, and he was faster than she was, faster than anything Eli had ever seen, and he struck, and she caught it.

She caught every blow. He had the whole dead city behind him and her blade was there every time, and every time Eli heard her bones breaking under the stress of blocking, the wrist, the forearm, the opened shoulder, and she went on blocking with what was left, a step back and a step back, straight, until her heels touched the foot of the dais. Ten feet from Eli's face.

Then the light round the apprentice turned to fire, and the blows came through.

She took them standing. On the last she went to her knees, and Eli saw that she had let him drive her there. She had waited until he was all the way up. Her arms rose, bent where arms don't bend, and she threw.

The living blade went up the steps at the man in the chair.

He caught it by the blade. With the right, the hand that had been under his head. It bit him and twisted in his grip, and opened his palm to the bone before he crushed it, and he let the pieces fall. He was breathing through his mouth. His eyes went back to the door.

One sliver went on curling and uncurling on the step by his foot.

She was still falling.

Behind the chair, a hand. Raised, the fingers a little curled.

She wasn't looking at the Queen. She was looking at the step, at the small pale thing opening and closing on it.

He said her name. There was no air behind it. Doc's hands pushed him flat.

She closed her hand.

The space round the Emperor folded inward, all of it at once: the air, the light, the chair, the dais. The stone of the floor ran toward him like water toward a drain. In the Tower, it had him. His sleeve went first, then the skin of his forearm, drawn toward a point in front of his chest.

The Emperor stood up.

He came up out of the chair as it folded behind him, and turned, and took hold of what she had made with both hands. It took both. His teeth showed. His opened palm left a smear on the air. He was looking at it, and at her. He wasn't looking at the door.

At the door Jon had taken a step. One. He stood with one foot in front of the other.

The Emperor turned the thing, slowly, like a man forcing a rusted wheel, inside out.

Everything that had been falling toward him fell toward her.

Her silver eyes found Eli along the floor. Then the space round her folded in and took her whole, toward a point just behind her heart, with a small flat noise like a book being shut.

Nothing where she had been. A little grit slid across the stone toward the place and stopped.

Eli had the two fingers in his other hand. He didn't remember taking hold of them.

The Emperor stood in what was left of his chair with his right arm hanging. The skin of the forearm was gone, wrung like a cloth. His eyes went back to the door. Jon was still standing with one foot in front of the other.

The Queen lay on her side at the foot of the step with her arms folded under her where arms don't fold, and her eyes open on the stair. The soles of her boots were toward him. They were worn through at the heel, both of them. He had been on the deck under those boots every day for a year and never once seen the soles, and he looked at them now for a long time, and thought she ought to have had them seen to.

The apprentice stood over her, burning, and turned to the dais with his arms spread. His face was wet and hopeful.

"Master," he said. "Are you proud?"

"Proud?" said the Emperor, to the door. "Proud of what, exactly? You only killed one, and now you're burning alive." He sighed. "You've always been a disappointment, and I can't believe you've embarrassed me again today."

The apprentice didn't hear it. The fire was inside him now, coming out through his skin and his mouth and the joints of his armour. The screaming went on a long time, and changed, and stopped. When Eli looked there was a shape on the floor beside her, black and smoking and very small. Some of the smoke went straight up and some drifted toward the window, and Eli watched the part that drifted.

The Emperor waited. No one had really heard him. He sighed through his nose and covered a yawn with his good hand. The hand was shaking.

He looked at Jon.

---

The hands left Eli's ribs.

Doc was laughing. He got up off his knees, one and then the other, with a hand on the wall, and walked out into the middle of the floor, past the Queen and the black thing that had been the apprentice, laughing a big happy laugh.

"Oh, you're good," he said. "Oh, you're very, very good." He cracked his small pudgy knuckles. "I saw this place come up, you know. The morning the capital burned. A dragon told me it was nice warm sunny weather for a quick ride out of the city, so off I rode, and I looked back from the top of the ridge, and there was the cloud, as far as I could see, and then up out of the cloud, this." He tapped the floor with his foot. "Like a pearl. I've wanted a look inside ever since."

He opened his arms.

Nothing came out of him that Eli could see. No light, no shape. Weight. It was on Eli's chest before Doc had finished lifting his arms, where the small hands had been, and then it leaned, more and more of it, a crowd shoving out through a door too small for them. The air round Doc wavered like air over a summer road. The floor under him scored in long lines toward the dais, and the Emperor took a step back.

He put up his good hand. The right came up after it as far as his waist, and fell, and hung.

"Giants and dragons," said Doc, conversationally. "Elves and humans. A lot of humans; you get everywhere." Something struck the Emperor and he rocked on his feet. "Dwarves leading orcs." The Emperor's hair blew back off his face, and nothing else in the room moved. "And fellow gnomes. Oh, a great many gnomes. Everyone brought me their sick, you see. Every family in the village. And I told every one of them the same thing."

He smiled up at the Emperor, very kindly.

"I wasn't able to save your loved one."

He glanced back at Eli. "The little one with the fever, at the clinic. She never woke up, you know. Her mother brought her back while you two were outside arguing about my price, and I told her too."

A girl hanging very loose in her mother's arms. *There's something wrong with him.* Teo's arm, and would Eli stay, just while, just so. The old man's face gone still and strange over a dwarf's bandaged leg. *They always get the wrong one.* Small hands on his ribs, all this time, and the lips counting.

The Emperor was going back across the dais a step at a time. Blood came from his nose, and then from his ears. His plain dark clothes tore along the seams as if hands were pulling at them. He held, and it kept breaking over him.

Doc was getting smaller. His face was sinking and the skin had gone loose on it, like fruit left too long in the sun. Eli had seen skin do that. So had Doc, leaning forward in his straps to watch.

"And last," said Doc, in a voice gone thin and old. "My brother."

The weight went out of the room. Nothing pressed at all, as if something had stopped on its way out and turned back to him. Doc looked at the empty air in front of his face.

Then it came once more, all of it, flung, and the Emperor, staggering, brushed it aside with the back of his good hand.

Doc sat down.

He was very old now, his face folded and folded again, his body shrinking inside his clothes. He looked round the throne room with milky eyes as if he had never seen it before.

"Oh," he said, small and surprised. "It's quiet."

He seemed to listen to it.

"First time in as long as I can remember," he said. "It's peaceful."

His face went slack and easy, like a man falling asleep.

Eli listened too. It was quiet. He found that he liked it. The girl, and Teo over there on the stone with his hand open, and he went on liking it.

Then Doc was dust, and the dust lay on the floor in the shape of a small round man sitting down, and then that slid apart too.

Jon was watching the dust. A single tear ran down beside his nose, and he didn't wipe it.

"Jon," said the Emperor.

Jon looked up. The tear was still there.

The Emperor stood swaying at the edge of the dais, slack-mouthed, bleeding from his nose and ears and his opened palm, one arm hanging, breathing like a man at the top of a long climb.

"It's just us," said the Emperor.

---

Eli got an elbow under him. It took three goes. He was very thirsty, he noticed, and it seemed important. Some of Doc had drifted as far as his sleeve.

"You put me on a post," he said. It came out wet. "Recover imperial property. They had my face down to the scar on my chin. You never once asked to see it."

His father's eyes stayed on Jon.

Eli put two fingers to his cheek and drew them down it, ear to jaw. "You've never seen this one."

Nothing.

"A man died at a fence for your warrant, and it was already on every wall. There was a boy in your yard with a rag round his hand, crying while your guard walked him at me." He spat blood on the floor and looked at it to see how much there was. "Teo carried something across your whole war to say to you. His mother and father are still in your house. He had the title right. You said it with him."

Nothing. A dragon had turned its head for him once. Weather, but it had turned its head.

"I asked them to come. All of them. I sat at their tables and asked." His voice went. He was embarrassed by that, with Jon there to hear, and pushed on. "Here's your property. It came all this way. Look at it."

His father didn't turn his head. His good hand had been coming up, open, toward the door. Toward Jon. It stopped halfway and stayed there.

Eli watched it stay. He couldn't have said what had stopped it.

Jon was already walking. He came down the length of the room, past Teo, past Eli, so close that the hem of his coat went over Eli's hand.

"He was watching you," Eli said, to his back. "The whole time."

Jon went on to the dais.

---

They fought. Eli saw three things and nothing in between.

The great window starred from top to bottom, and held.

Jon standing perfectly still in the middle of the floor with his eyes shut, while the far wall of the throne room came apart round the Emperor and put itself back together.

The Emperor down on one knee. Up. Down again.

Eli tried to stand through all of it. He got as far as the elbow each time.

At the end the Emperor was on both knees, and he stayed there.

Jon stood over him, breathing hard. He did something careful with both hands, and the Emperor's arms went behind his back, the good one and the ruined one, and stayed there, held by nothing Eli could see. His head went back. He stopped fighting it.

He was alive. Tired men can be held, Jon had said.

What was left of the chair sat folded in on itself on the dais. Nobody had looked at it since.

Jon hauled the Emperor up by the collar like a coat off a hook, walked him to the great window, and sat him on the floor beneath it with his back to the glass.

Then he sat down beside him.

## NINE · The Window

Eli dragged himself across the floor to them. Nobody helped him. It was farther than it looked, and he did it on one elbow and one hip, past the coin, past the foot of the step and the worn soles, through a drift of something he didn't look at. One of the Emperor's boots had come off in the fight and lay on its side ten feet from the glass.

The two men sat side by side under the great window. Outside, the sky over Earth was full of fire.

And the ground was opening. Down in the burning city, at the oldest wounds, the places where it had always been wrong, the ground was coming apart, and the split was running out through the streets. Further off there were more. Something was coming up out of them. He couldn't see what. Over the city the ships stopped firing at each other, one by one, and turned toward it. On the roofs the Legion's guns came round.

"Jon," said the Emperor.

He hadn't looked at Eli once since they came into the room. Eli lay three feet from his father's knee, and his father looked past him at Jon.

"You remember who our master is," said the Emperor.

Jon was looking out of the window.

"You know what you have to do," said the Emperor.

Jon sighed and leaned his head back against the glass.

"Just enjoy the show, old man," he said.

"What is that?" said Eli. "Coming out of the ground."

Nobody answered.

"Who is your master?"

Nobody answered.

"What does he have to do?"

Neither of them turned his head.

"Jon. Did you know about Doc? What he was. The whole time, at the clinic, did you—" His side caught. He waited for it. "What did the giant say to you? All those times you said elves. Where were you?"

The fire moved on Jon's face. Nothing else on it did.

Eli wondered what day of the week it was down there, and tried to count it out from the freighter, and lost his place.

He looked at his father.

"Why did you kill Teo?"

The two men sat with the fire on their faces and watched the opening spread through the city, street by street. After a while, without looking, the Emperor moved his bound shoulder a little closer to Jon's. Jon let him.

Eli watched them do it. What he felt first was left out. It was a small feeling, and he waited for it to turn into something bigger.

Below, the ground went on opening, toward the foot of the Tower.

"Why did you kill Teo?" said Eli again.

Neither of them turned.
