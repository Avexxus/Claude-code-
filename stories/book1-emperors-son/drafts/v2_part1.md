# The Emperor's Son

## ONE · Property

The guard didn't get tired.

Eli did. It had started in his thighs and now it was a blade under his ribs on the left side, and every breath came in hot and short around it. Nine days in a convoy hold with one bucket were still in his clothes, and he ran inside the smell of them. Behind him the guard's boots hit the slag in a rhythm that never broke, and the rig on the man's back, a ribbed hump of grown flesh in a steel cage, gave a wet click every few strides.

"Return what you took!" The voice came out of a speaker on the man's harness, flat and much too loud. "Return what you took!"

The thief-takers' shout. They had shouted it under his window once at a kitchen boy, over a ham.

One reach and the guard would be a stain on the wall. In the street. In daylight, with every window looking to see whose reach it was. He ran.

Towers on both sides, white, grown up out of the bedrock without a seam, the cheap kind every colony puts up, twelve floors and a flat roof. The one he'd grown up under had filled every window. He stopped looking up.

Wire ahead. Past it the yards: slag, cranes, open sheds, pens. Hot fat in his mouth, and under the fat ammonia, thick enough to chew.

He went over the fence. The wire opened his palm on the way, and he came down on one hip and felt nothing in that leg, and got up on it anyway, and kept going.

The pens were full. Orcs in rows under the shed roofs, hundreds deep, men and women both, heavy in the shoulder and bare to the waist, waiting their turn on the ramps. They stood to the ankles in what had run down their own legs. None of them had stepped aside from it. Each had a neat pale scar at the base of the skull, and in the cold the scars wept.

At the top of each ramp a press shut with a long sigh and opened again. A yard hand stood at each one with a bare palm flat on the housing and his eyes shut, lips going. Out of the far side, on rollers, came plate: ribbed, the size of a door, sweating. LEGION CONTRACT, CARRIER PLATE, in yellow stencil down every press. Under each a gutter ran grey, with a skin on it. The men stacking wore gloves gone stiff to the wrist. One of them leaned on a stack to rest, and the stack settled under him and let out air, and he took his hand away.

The fat in Eli's mouth. He shut it and breathed through his nose, and that was worse.

The orcs on the ramps could see the stacks.

The young ones, not yet full in the shoulder, were penned apart beside a shed with no windows and CORES stencilled over the door. A drain ran out under the door. At the rail one of them had a rag round his hand where the wire had cut him, dried into the cut, brown. He looked up at Eli going past, and at Eli's hand, which was dripping, and then down at his own feet. A blue comb lay in the slag by the rail with most of its teeth gone.

The guard didn't bother with the fence. He stopped at the wire, took a slate off his belt and spoke into it.

Every orc in the yard turned their head at once.

They came out of the pens in a slow wave, over the rails, hands open and reaching. The young one with the rag was crying. An old man near the front with a split lip was saying something over and over while his legs walked him forward, and when he got close enough Eli heard that it was a name.

Shed wall at his back. Nowhere.

Eli reached.

The air in front of him went hard and he swung it, and the front rank went over into the rank behind. He felt bones go, and heard them. More came on over the ones he'd dropped, treading on hands. The ones on the ground were trying to get up. Some of them couldn't. A woman sat in the slag holding her own shin in both hands where it bent, studying it.

He could have cleared the yard.

A man on the counterweight of the nearest crane, twenty feet up. Eating a peach. Two stones on the iron beside him already. A long coat buttoned to the chin.

"You look," he said, "like you could use an exit."

He dropped off the crane, landed hard, and came across the slag still chewing. A woman with burn scars up both arms got to Eli first. Her eyes were on his face and she was saying sorry, sorry, in a small ordinary voice, while her hands came up for his throat. Her breath was sour and empty. The man stepped in and laid two fingers on the back of her neck, on the scar, the way you'd test a child's forehead for fever. Her legs went. He let her down.

He took the next one by the wrist, turned him, touched, and let him down too. Then two at once, a hand for each, and he grunted at that and shook his fingers out afterwards. He kept himself between them and Eli and took them as they came. By the fifth or sixth he was breathing through his mouth, with juice drying on his chin. The old man with the split lip reached them still saying the name, and the man put a hand to the back of his head, and the name stopped halfway.

Then no more came. Over at the wire the guard had the slate pressed flat to his chest in both hands and was backing off a step at a time, his eyes on Eli's face. In the pens and on the ramps the rest stood where they'd been stopped, hands half up. The young one with the rag was still crying.

The man wiped his fingers on his coat and put a warm hand on Eli's shoulder.

"Breathe," he said. "You're doing beautifully." He nodded at the wire. "Nobody's after you yet. Not properly. But he's seen your face. He'll write it up, and it goes up, and up again, till it lands on the desk of whoever you're running from, and then they are." The hand stayed where it was. "Your call. I won't make it for you."

He had watched a steward's hands broken once, for theft. Watched.

He reached. Forty yards of slag, and then the guard's throat, and he closed his grip on it and held. It took longer than he'd thought it would. A dark patch came through the front of the man's trousers and ran down one leg before anything else about him changed. Then the tongue came out, further than Eli had known a tongue could come. The heels drummed. Somebody's lunch tin stood open on the step of the nearest shed with a fork in it. Beans. A skin on the beans. When it was over the body sat down against the wire, and the rig on its back kept clicking, and started to drip.

Eli's hand was shaking. He looked at it, the palm laid open and packed black with slag, and thought that he would have to wash it before he ate anything, and then about where there might be water out here, and whether it would be clean, while it went on shaking.

The man took the hand in both of his, cut and all, and held it until it was still. "That's how you know you're not him," he said. "Whoever he is." He let go, and found blood on his fingers, and used the coat again. "What did you steal, by the way?"

Eli wiped his mouth.

"Me," he said.

The man laughed so hard he had to bend over with his hands on his knees. Then he straightened up, finished the peach, and dropped the stone on the old orc with the split lip. It landed on his cheek and stayed there.

---

Teo was meant to be at the fence with the flyer. He wasn't.

Eli stood among the bodies and watched the sky over the sheds and counted to six hundred. The dead weren't quiet. They settled. One let go a long breath it had no use for, and a while later another answered from lower down, from the gut. The flies had been at the gutters for years and now they came over. Somewhere in the city a siren went up and down and quit. The man in the coat sat on an upturned bucket, cleaning his nails with a sliver of fence wire.

Somewhere in the four hundreds the count went out from under him. He was thinking about the bath off his old rooms, down on the side of the house where the Tower's shadow lay all day and the light came in grey: the tiles warm underfoot, the taps shaped like fishes, and which of the fishes ran hot. He couldn't remember which. He started again from one.

"He's not coming," the man said. "We could walk."

Eli stayed where he was. His hip had begun to hurt, which he supposed was good.

The flyer came in low over the sheds forty minutes late, an old plain-engine thing with a cracked canopy and a fresh dent down one side, and hit the slag hard. Teo leaned out, white in the face, talking before the engine had stopped.

"They put a checkpoint on the ring road, I had to go round by the drainage cuts, the whole way round, I thought you were dead, I thought they'd—" He saw the bodies. He saw the guard against the wire, and the tongue, and then the stranger.

"Did you do all that?" he said to Eli.

"No," said Eli.

Nine days ago Teo had forged the code that put the two of them in the hold of an empty food convoy, outbound. He had carried the bucket up the ladder every morning without being asked. His parents were still in the house.

"Jon," said the stranger, and climbed into the back of the flyer as though he'd been invited. "Friend of the family."

"Which family?" said Eli.

"Long story. Bad ending."

Teo lifted off. Below them the orcs were still standing in the pens. Another man with a slate had come out of the sheds, and a yard crew behind him with hooks. They worked along the dead in a line and dragged them up the ramps to the presses, because the yard paid by weight. The young ones had been turned the other way. They were going in at the door marked CORES in single file, and the one with the rag was fourth, wiping his nose on it.

"Where to?" said Teo.

Jon had found another peach somewhere. "Up," he said. "There's a hull going out tonight that takes flyers in the hold."

## TWO · The Master

They came down out of terraced hills on the fourth morning, green in rows on every slope. Teo said it was food. Growing, in the ground, where anyone could take it. Eli made him say it twice.

That afternoon a drone came down the road Jon had sworn the drones didn't watch, and hung in the air ahead of them.

Teo dropped them into the river gorge so hard that Eli bit his tongue. Under the overhang he killed the engine. Water underneath, brown and loud. Blood in his mouth and the canopy fogging. Somewhere above them the drone's hum, holding one note.

Jon was asleep in the back with his mouth open.

An hour. Teo's hands never came off the controls. The cabin filled up with the three of them, breath and feet and the hold bucket still in the seams of Eli's coat, and nobody could open a vent.

"Can't you do something?" Teo said at last, to the back.

Jon didn't open his eyes. "Then whoever's watching would know what's in here."

He went back to sleep. After a while he snored a little. Eli climbed out and sat with his back against the wet rock and watched him through the canopy, and listened to the drone. It left after dark. When Jon woke he said they must have changed the routes, and ate something.

That night Teo sat alone on the wing. When he came back in he said to Eli, low, "He just touched them and they died. He was eating a peach."

Jon, in his blanket, said that he hadn't killed all of them, only the ones that reached Eli. The rest would have woken up in the pens and gone up the ramps on schedule. "They're awake in there the whole time, you know," he said. "The chip doesn't put you out. It only does the walking."

Nobody said anything about the one at the fence.

Teo lay down across the front seats. After a while, half under, he said, "They were closing the house. Did you know? Before we went."

"Closing it how?"

"The stewards had a list. My mother was on it." A long breath. "The codes were easy, that's the thing. Nobody watches a servant's boy at a terminal. Nobody ever—"

He stopped there. Eli waited for the rest and it didn't come, and Teo's breathing changed.

In the morning Jon fried him an egg and asked about his parents and listened to the whole answer, and by noon Teo was laughing at his stories.

On the seventh morning Jon climbed out at a fuel stop in the middle of nothing and stretched until his back cracked.

"I have to go and see some elves about a thing," he said.

"What do you want from me?" said Eli.

Jon looked at him.

"Company," he said, and walked off down the road. There was nothing down the road. Teo counted out coin for the fuel, twice, and asked Eli if that looked right to him. Eli didn't know what anything cost.

---

After that he came and went. A week on a salvage hull where Eli and Teo were cutting cable out of dead ships for cash; the crews were still in their bunks, dried to the blankets, and the cable came out of the walls with hair in it. Nothing all winter. A transit platform, and a bag of peaches, and a month. When Eli asked where he went, he said, "Elves."

Eli went hungry three days on a station where nobody would hire a man with soft hands. His gums went tender and tasted of coins. He stood in the hiring hall breathing other men's fried onions. On the fourth day he broke a dock boss's knee for a job. It made the sound of a green branch, and the man stared at Eli, not at the knee, with his mouth working and nothing coming out of it. Eli took the job. Teo didn't speak to him for a week. Eli left half his bread on Teo's bunk every night and found it there every morning, harder.

Half the shopfronts on that station kept a little shrine to the Emperor's god, lit with a cheap bulb, and not one of them had the Emperor's face on it. Where a face would have gone there was the seal, the plain empty oval that was on the coins too. Eli walked past the shrines twice a day, to the docks and back, and it was most of a month before it came to him that he didn't know the face either.

On the docks they talked about elves. Raiders had hit an imperial convoy three days out from the capital, forty hulls, the whole string.

"Food," said the man next to Eli on the line. "Earth'll be eating its furniture."

"Earth's never missed a meal in its life," said somebody else.

Crates came off the transit hulls with well-company marks on them, POOL-GRADE CORE, THIS WAY UP. A dockhand kicked a cargo loader that had stopped to think too long. "Battery," he said, and spat.

Jon was in that month. He watched the loader trundle off. "Orc," he said. "Young one, by the size of the core. Flesh takes the shape better at that age. Pool fetches more, though, and nobody wants orc in their loader, so pool's what goes on the box." He offered Eli a peach.

Eli took it.

The station's legal exchange was two decks below the hiring hall, and Eli passed it every morning. A clean white front, a drain in the middle of a white floor, and a board of prices that changed through the day: eyes, kidneys, skin by the hand's width, ELF LIVER ENQUIRE WITHIN. Behind the glass the whole-body stock stood asleep in upright cases, tags at their throats, their own breath running down the inside of the lids. CLONE. NO SOUL. GRADE B. One morning a buyer had a case opened to look at a man's teeth, and the man woke. His face did what a baby's face does just before it cries. The clerk put him under again and swabbed the buyer's fingers. "Reflex," he said. "Nobody home." The buyer took the kidneys and one eye and left the rest on account. The eye went into a jar, and when the clerk set the jar on the counter it turned, slowly, and came to rest facing the door. It cost about what Eli made in a week. Two decks further down, with no front at all, the same stock sold for four times as much with papers saying born.

---

In the fourth month he found his own face on a post outside a customs hall.

It had been pasted up in the wet and the paste had gone to blisters, so that his forehead stood off the board in a bubble with rain running behind it. RECOVER IMPERIAL PROPERTY, it said, under the seal. Under that, smaller: Return what you took. It had him down to the scar on his chin. By the date, it had gone up seven days after he ran. Two days before the yard.

He stood in front of it while the shift changed in the hall behind him.

He'll write it up, Jon had said. And it goes up, and up again.

It had gone up already.

The print was cheap. The mouth was his, or near enough. But the jaw under the scar had come out harder and squarer than he thought of his own, and the longer he stood there the less it belonged to him. A stranger's jaw. He pressed the blister flat with his thumb and it came back.

Teo came up beside him, read it, and said nothing.

"Property," said Eli.

His father had never asked to see him. Tutors, reports, orders. Never a face. And now this one, on a post on every world, till somebody dragged him home.

"I'm going to the Tower," he said. "I'm going to take the chair away from him."

Teo laughed, then saw his face, and stopped. A customs man came out and told them they were blocking the board.

---

When Jon next turned up Eli told him before he had his coat off. The Tower. He'd need people. He'd need to be better than he was, a lot better.

"You knew it was already up," he said. "The warrant. You let me kill him for nothing."

"I knew it would be." Jon went on with his buttons. "Up that week, up the next. Does it matter much? He'd have written it either way. They always write it up." He put his hand on Eli's shoulder, where it had been in the yard.

"It matters to me. Say it."

"Yes. I knew."

The hand came off. Jon hung his coat on the hook, over Teo's wet one, and squared the two of them.

"Would you rather I'd done it for you?"

Eli opened his mouth. On the shelf the hot plate ticked, cooling.

"A great deal better," Jon said. "You're right about that. Yes. I'd think so."

"Then teach me."

"Oh, it wouldn't take. My kind of teaching, you'd come out the other end being me, and one of those is plenty." He was taking in the bunk room, the hot plate, the one window. "There's a trainer at North Wells on Tessid. Best hands on the rim. Ask for her. Teo, is there anything to eat?"

Teo said there was soup. Jon said soup was wonderful.

---

Tessid was grey and cold and too far from anything to matter, apart from the wound in the ground: three miles across, the rock gone to glass, the air tasting like a struck bell. The well companies pumped pool out of the glass like oil, five of them along the rim, and none of them had ever quite won. Glass grit lay on everything and went through boot leather in a week. One latrine trench served all five companies, and it had been shelled and redug so many times that Rimtown was laid out round the smell. Somebody had built a temple on the near edge, and somebody else had shelled it, and somebody had built it again.

There was no trainer at North Wells. She had been dead two winters. There was a sergeant with a stick who walked a line of boys in amplifier rigs and hit the ones who eased off, and for a week and most of his coin Eli stood in that line with no rig and was taught to push harder, which he could do already. The boys' necks were raw where the rig stalks went in, green at the edges. They compared them.

On the sixth day one burned out. He was perhaps nineteen, holding the lip of a well. A well-mouth shut if nobody held it, everyone on the rim knew that, and he was holding it open through his rig while the pump crew worked underneath. He held it twenty seconds longer than his body could carry. His face went grey, then slack. He sat down in the mud with blood coming out of his ears and his eyes, and then his bowels went, and two company men had the cold box open beside him while his feet were still moving. The eyes and the kidneys were in it before they had him off the field. The rig came off him warm and was buckled onto the next boy in line. Eli watched the new boy's hands doing up the straps. They were steady.

"Burnout," said the woman who sold Eli noodles that night. "Rig's fine. Rig's always fine, they'll have had it on three more by— you want the egg? Egg's extra. It's the meat that goes."

At the end of her counter two rig boys with their straps still on were arguing whether the Hand could take a dragon.

"He doesn't sleep," one said. "Carrier-born. Stands by the chair all night, every night, waiting to be told."

"Told what?"

"Anything."

Eli didn't have the price of the egg. In the morning he signed as a day pusher on the practice field under the temple steps, shifting loaders bare-handed for a company short of rigs, at less than the docks had paid.

It got away from him on the third load. He was tired and he pushed the way the sergeant had wanted it, and the loader, twice the height of a man, went up on one track and started over toward a line of rig students drilling by the steps. Slowly. He grabbed for it and got the whole field, grit and air and the boys' shadows, and nothing in it with an edge.

An old man was sitting in the temple door. He looked at the loader. It stopped leaning. It came upright and settled back onto its tracks as gently as a cup set down, and the boys stood under it with their mouths open.

The old man rubbed his eyes. When he took his hand away there was blood on his lip, and he wiped it off with his thumb before any of the boys turned to the steps. Eli had turned already.

Then he came down them. The foreman watched him come with his pen stopped. Men along the field took their caps off, not knowing what else you did. He said four words to the foreman, and the foreman put a line through Eli's name.

"Who taught you?" Close to he was spare and small and smelled of cold stone, and it was Eli's hands he was asking.

The three best tutors in the Empire, Eli told him, and a duelling master from the dragon schools.

"They taught you to shout."

"Then I'd like to be taught the rest."

"I teach boys. I can still bend a boy." He had started back up. "Go and shout somewhere else."

Eli sat down on the temple steps. It sleeted for three days. Teo brought him a blanket and told him he was an idiot, a proper one, did he know that, and then brought him another blanket. On the third evening the old man opened the door.

"You're loose on my rim with that, and blind," he said. "You'll kill one of mine by the thaw. That's the reason. There isn't a second one." He stood aside. "You'll never be fine. You started too late. Sit."

---

He kept Eli five months and didn't let him push.

Every morning the master sat cross-legged on the cold floor of the temple with his eyes shut, and Eli sat across from him and did the same, until his knees locked and had to be straightened by hand. Then the master tied a cloth over Eli's eyes in the middle of the room and told him to find the one moth in it, and when Eli couldn't, he left him sitting until he could.

On the fourth day Eli pulled the cloth off and said that at fourteen he had broken a wall from across a room.

"Anyone can lift," said the master. "Everyone out here who could lift is dead. Learn to listen. Put that back on."

It took eleven days. When it came it was nothing like pushing, only a small dry flutter up near the broken roof, and Eli was so surprised that he lost it again and had to sit most of another afternoon to get it back. After that the master had him lift a cup of water without spilling it, and then lift the water without the cup.

The rig students came up at noon for their own hour. The two eldest had been under the loader. They gave Eli the far end of the floor.

Teo had found work on the company docks patching flyers and came up the steps at night with whatever there was to eat. One night he came up with a rig over his shoulder, a condemned one off the scrap line, grey at the hump, that he had patched with canopy sealant. "It holds, I tested it, it pulls a bit to the left but it holds, I thought if we ever—"

"Throw that in the glass," said the master. His eyes were shut.

Teo kept it in the flyer, under the seat.

Nobody on the rim knew whose son Eli was, and the master didn't ask. He slept on the temple floor in his coat, with lice in the seams that he gave up hunting after the first month, and chilblains that split and wept whenever the stove was lit. He woke before light without anyone calling him. The glass outside went from black to grey to a colour like weak tea, and the company guns started up along the rim at the same hour every day, far off, almost friendly. Some mornings, sitting in the cold with his eyes shut, he forgot for an hour at a time what he had come there for. He didn't tell Teo.

"You've the strength of an army," the master said, "and the hands of a man in mittens. One day it'll kill you."

In the fifth month Eli threaded a needle across the room without touching it, and sat staring at it, thread and all, until the master came over and took it away.

"You'll be less likely to kill your friends by accident," the master said, and stuck the needle back in his collar.

---

Before he left, Eli told him who he was. The Emperor's son. Where he was going, and what he meant to do there. He hadn't said it out loud to anyone since the house. He asked him to come.

The master went to the temple door and stood with his back to the room, facing the glass and the far fires of the company lines.

"My master went up to that Tower," he said.

Eli waited.

"Fifty years ago. Best I ever saw. Said he'd go and see who sat in the chair." A gun went, down the rim. "Nobody ever told me how far he got."

He scratched his neck.

"No. I won't walk into that place behind a boy with a grudge. Go and die somewhere better than this."

Eli didn't argue. The master went back in.

Teo had a job off the company board, which was their fare off the rim: four burned-out rig boys to the gnome healer at Holding Thirty-One, paid on delivery, by count alive. The company man who signed them out said the gnome sat up with the dying, and came cheap. They crossed in a hauler's hold with the stretchers racked two and two in the back of the flyer. The boys had been washed, not well.

"We'll find someone else," Teo said. "There's other masters. There's got to be. Isn't there?"

Eli sat with his eyes shut and tried to find the walls of the hold, and couldn't. He could hear the four behind him. One of them was breathing wrong.

## THREE · The Healer and the Statue

The miner screamed the whole time the gnome was setting his arm, and the gnome talked the whole time, about a goat.

"Ate my whole herb garden, that goat. My brother's goat, properly; he was the clever one. The winter half the village came to me with fever, and there's me with not a leaf. I could have strangled him. Didn't, mind. I was very fond of him. Hold still." The end of the bone went back in under the skin with the sound of a boot coming out of mud.

The line went out the clinic door and down the street: a boy with a crushed foot, an old dwarf woman coughing black into a cloth that she refolded each time to a clean place, gnomes with a stamped tin tag on a cord, and a green ring on every throat where the tin sat. The dwarves called the quarter the Tins. A bucket of old dressings stood by the door, full, and the top of it moved. The dwarves in the line had their caps in their hands.

He was small even for a gnome, round in the face, bald on top, with enormous ears. When Teo's four came in on their stretchers he left the miner with a strap between his teeth and went along them thumbing up eyelids. He had the worst one carried behind a curtain, and went in and sat with him.

An hour. Teo wouldn't wait indoors. Out by the flyer he said, "There's something wrong with him."

"He's good."

"He's too good. Did you see that line? Nobody that good stays somewhere like this. Nobody. Why would you?"

When they went back in the gnome was coming out through the curtain, drying his hands.

"Three," he said.

"He was breathing," said Teo. "At the door, he was breathing, I heard him, you can't just— it's by count, they pay us by the count—"

"He was. I sat with him." The gnome signed the company receipt for three and blew on the ink. "Poor thing. Next."

At the pay window on the pad that was a quarter gone.

Next was a foreman's girl of five or six, carried in slick with fever and fighting her mother's arms. She went behind the curtain. She came out cool and asleep, and hung very loose, one heel knocking against her mother's hip.

The mother tried to thank him. He held up a small hand.

"Thank me when she's grown. Next."

His eyes went to Eli's chin and away again. He dried his hands a second time. They were dry.

"That's your hull on the pad," he said. "I saw it come down. I've had a mind to travel for some while now. I'll come along with you."

"No," said Eli.

"I can set a bone on a moving deck."

"We've no money and no bunk. I'm sorry."

"Ah, well," said the gnome. "Next."

They were on the pad with the engine warming when the noise started down in the Tins.

A woman came up the street first. It was the mother, and she had the girl, and the girl's heels swung as she walked. Her voice had gone to rags, with no words left in it. Behind her came the street: dwarves off shift with the dust still on them, gnomes, the old woman with the cloth. Most of the dwarves had a stone in the fist. And out ahead of all of them, in no hurry, a bag in one hand and a stick in the other, came the gnome.

He stopped once to get his breath. The street stopped behind him. Nobody came past.

"Look at that," Teo said. "They've rocks in their hands. Not one of them'll throw. She's dead, Eli, look at her, that's a dead child, and they're walking him out like a priest."

A council dwarf pushed through with his chain of office on over a nightshirt, blowing. He got in front of the gnome and took his cap off.

"You'll not stay."

"I'll not."

"There's the clinic."

"Keep it swept."

The dwarf stood turning the cap round by the brim. Then he stood out of the way.

The mother had reached the foot of the ramp. The gnome went back down to her. He had to reach up to do it. He put his hand on the girl's hair, where it had been combed since the clinic and the tracks of the comb still showed, and he said something Eli couldn't hear. The mother's mouth was open. She let him.

Then he came up the ramp past Eli's knee and sat down on his bag.

"I said no," Eli said.

"You did. Very civil about it, too."

"We've four bunks," said Teo. "There's four of us." He lifted off. Down on the pad a dwarf opened his fist and let the stone fall beside his boot.

The gnome watched the pad get small. Then he smiled up at Eli.

"Well," he said. "Where would I go?"

"What are you called?"

"Doc. The children call me that. Saves everyone the bother of a name." The stick across his knees had a nail through the end. That morning it had been in the toll box on the river bridge, under a troll's hand.

---

"If I get the chair, they're my war," Eli said. "I'd like to see them first."

Seluvane stood on a world of high cold rivers. It had been grown whole, finer than anything human, and its towers leaned in toward each other at the top. Eli stood on the landing field with his bag at his feet. After the Tins it took him some time to find what was wrong with the air. It had no smell. Not of stone or water or people. He breathed in and got nothing back.

At the gate an inquisitor in grey sat across a table from him with a ledger open at his elbow.

"Whose are you?" he said.

"My own," said Eli, and smiled.

The inquisitor didn't write anything down. He asked where Eli had been born, and Eli gave him a town that existed. What he carried, and why, and how long, and who would answer for him, and which of his companions he would leave behind if he had to. Eleven questions. Then the same eleven from the top, in the same order, in the same voice, and then a third time, and Eli spent charm on the first round and exactness on the second, dates and tonnages and the names of docks, and by the third was saying as little as a man could and still be answering, and could hear how it sounded. The inquisitor watched his eyes through all of it. Then he shut the ledger, and the gate shut.

They slept on the landing field under the flyer's wing. Eli sent to a box on a transit platform that took messages for a man called Jon and, a clerk had told him once, for eleven other names. Doc found something to eat every day. Teo wouldn't touch it.

Jon came on the fourth morning, on foot across the field, with nothing to say about five months.

"Your trainer's been dead two winters," said Eli.

"Is she. I am sorry." He was gazing up at the towers. "The only people left who can build a city that makes you want to cry, and they keep it shut."

He spoke to the inquisitor in elvish and showed him a token on a chain. The inquisitor turned the token over, then held out his hand and kept it out until Jon took the chain off his neck and gave it to him. He put it in his coat. Then he stamped the papers, three days and not one more, with a red bar across the corner, and wrote a line in the ledger beside each of their names.

"I helped the king once, when he was a boy," Jon said, inside. He touched his collar where the chain had been. "That was my only one. Try not to steal anything."

"Who was he?" said Teo.

"Inquisition. The king's. They like to know where everyone is."

A grey coat was behind them in the market that afternoon, and another at the end of their street that night, and it was never the same coat twice.

---

On the second day they crossed a square where an elf knelt on the stones with his hands bound behind him and his mouth sewn shut with silver wire. He was the first thing in Seluvane that smelled. The holes had gone bad. The lips had swollen up round the wire and split, and a reed stood in the corner of the mouth where they fed him, and flies walked up and down the reed. A woman in good clothes stood at the edge of the square with her hands folded, facing the fountain. She was his wife, Jon said later. She was required to come every morning and stand there until dark, and she was not permitted to wave the flies off.

"What did he do?" Teo whispered.

"Disagreed," said Jon. "Out loud."

Teo had his knife out. He'd gone two steps toward the man before Eli caught his arm above the wrist and held it.

"Three days," Eli said. "Not one more."

Teo stared down at the hand on his arm until Eli let go. He put the knife away, and after that he walked a little apart.

The king's name was on the arches and on the coins: Mika.

A young archivist had been sent to be helpful. Teo asked her where you could get something to eat that wasn't leaves. "The dark elves are born in hell among the soul thieves and the traitors, and they come out of it full of hate," she said, as though that had been the question. "They are not really elves any more." She didn't know about food.

On the last afternoon a grey coat stood across the end of their street, and the way round went through a long square with a statue in the middle of it.

An elven woman, cut in white stone at twice life size. She stood with one hand raised and the fingers a little curled, about to close them on something only she could see. Elves stood round her in silence. Flowers were heaped at her feet, today's on top, white. Under them yesterday's, brown, and under those the years, pressed down into a black slime that had crept out across the paving and been walked in. It was the second thing in the city that smelled. The water in the gutters ran quieter there, and the light lay on the stone without moving, and for the first time in three days Eli couldn't see a grey coat anywhere.

"Lirael," said Jon. "Hero of her people. The king's sister. A long time ago."

At the foot of the statue an old elven woman knelt in the black with her skirt soaked to the thigh, crying without a sound. Teo crouched beside her and asked in his bad elvish if she'd lost someone.

"Yes," she said.

"Who?"

She looked at him.

"I don't know," she said. "I never know. None of us do." She turned back to the statue. "We come here because it seems the place to bring it."

The others walked on. Eli turned to say something to Jon, and Jon wasn't beside him.

He was standing in front of the statue with his hands in his coat pockets and his face tipped up to the stone one. He stood there long enough that the old woman lifted her head to him, and Eli started to say his name.

Then Jon turned round.

"Dinner," he said. It came out hoarse. He cleared his throat and said it again. "Come on."
