## FOUR · The Beater

The culvert let out under the Spur's head, behind the lift, where the wall ran wet and the morning hadn't got down to yet. Jon went up the last of the steps and out into the street without looking to see if they were behind him. He'd finished the pear. He dropped the core in the gutter, and the gutter water took it and turned it round once and carried it off along the kerb toward the Tower.

Eli came up into the noise after him. The lanes overhead were going over three deep with the first of the day's traffic, and the feeds were coming on along the walls one after another as the light reached them, and somewhere a hose was running on stone. He'd been on the Spur a hundred times. He'd been here four nights ago with the purse knocking on his ribs. He stood on the last step and put the heel of his hand to his eye and pressed.

It was the headache. It sat in there behind the eye, and in both ears, and in the hinge of his jaw where he'd been clenching it all the way down. Everybody down here had it. He'd had it every time he came down, and gone home up the stair and not had it, and never put the two together. There was a stall on the corner with blue pills in paper twists laid out on a tray, and a woman was buying two and telling the man the last two had done nothing. Eli took his hand down. The headache stayed where it was. He could feel the shape of it now, the edges, and now he'd found the edges he couldn't stop finding them.

"Teo," he said. "Keep up."

Teo came up the steps one at a time with his good hand flat on the wall. The bad one was inside his shirt, against his chest, in with the slate, and he'd done the buttons up over both. He had no coat. His face was grey under the brown and wet along the hairline, and the morning was cold enough that Eli's breath showed. He got to the street and stood there and breathed through his nose.

"I'm fine," he said.

"I never asked."

Jon was going away down the middle of the street at the same pace he'd crossed the yard. Eli went after him. Teo came after Eli.

The street leaned. It had always leaned. The Spur ran out from the Tower toward the drop and the floor under it tipped back the other way, so that walking out along it you were climbing, a little, every step, and your ankles knew it before you did. Eli had walked it a hundred times and never once thought about it. He thought about it now because of Teo. Teo's feet kept going wrong. Twice they went from under him, and both times he went back, not forward, a step back down the street toward the Tower, and the second time Eli caught him by the good arm and held on.

"Got you."

Teo took the arm back and walked.

In the window boxes the plants grew out sideways over the street, every one of them pointing the same way, back over Eli's shoulder. He turned to see what they were pointing at. The Tower filled the street behind him, pale, going up out of the roofs and out of sight.

"Teo," he said. "The plants."

Teo didn't look at the plants.

At the second landing, where a side stair came up from the levels below, a young orc was sitting on the top step with his elbows on his knees. Yard trousers. No shirt. Wire wound round the knuckles of both hands, bright, and round his neck a line of stitches done close in the same wire. He was eating something out of a paper. He watched Jon go by, and Teo, and then he watched the coat.

He folded the paper up small. He stood, and went down the side stair two at a time, and the stair took him.

"Jon," Eli said.

Jon walked on.

Eli looked at Teo. Teo had seen. He had his eyes on the empty step, and then on Eli, and then on the road, and he walked.

"It's half orcs, the Spur," Eli said. "They sit on steps. It's what steps are for." He laughed, and it hurt the side of his face. "Teo. It's a step."

Teo walked.

---

The flesh market was open. They were hosing the slabs down, and the water ran off the trestles into the gutter and went off toward the Tower with everything else, pink. The cold-cases were up. CLONE STOCK — NO SOUL, black on white on every end, with the grade under. A boy went by with a basket of ice bigger than he was. A woman in an apron sorted kidneys into two trays, the good and the less good, quick, without looking at her hands, talking to the next stall about her sister.

Eli walked through it beside Teo, pointing.

"Eyes," he said. "He does them by colour. And the real ones are under the counter. You have to ask." He'd seen it in a feed. He'd been through here four nights ago with the tarps down and seen nothing at all. "You have to know to ask, Teo."

Teo was slower than he'd been at the culvert. Eli had to stop for him and wait, and walk, and stop again.

At the far end of the cases a man had a burner going under an iron plate, and strips of something on the plate, spitting, and a stack of cones twisted out of old feed sheet. Jon stopped there. First thing he'd stopped for since the kitchen.

"Two," he said.

The man turned the strips with his fingers. "Clone or the other?"

"Whatever's hot."

The man shovelled a cone full off the hot end of the plate. Jon paid him coin out of his trouser pocket and walked on eating, and left the change on the plate. A little way on he held the cone back over his shoulder without turning his head, and Eli, who was nearest, took a strip out of it.

Hot, and salt, and good. He ate it.

"That's good," he said. "What is that?"

Jon ate.

Eli held the cone out to Teo. Teo looked at it, and back at the plate with the man scraping it, and shook his head once, and kept walking with his hand in his shirt. Eli shrugged and gave the cone back over Jon's shoulder, and Jon's hand came back and took it.

---

The feeds ran with the sound off. A queue somewhere under an orange sky. A ship's hull going into dock. LEGION, and a figure. ELVEN DELEGATION · TALKS, and tall people coming down a white ramp in the rain, too far off to have faces. A minister smiling, then gone. TERRACE PROPERTY VALUES, and a line under it going down.

"Elves," Eli said. "Teo. Look. Elves." He walked backwards a few steps to keep them in sight. "Dace's father had one to dinner once. He said it stood the whole night. Wouldn't take a chair. They had to eat round it."

Jon finished the cone and folded the paper in four and put it in his pocket.

At the landings the walls went from paint to grey stone, and on every one of them there was the seal, stamped, an oval with nothing in it. Eli had passed under a thousand of them. He looked at one now going by. Plain. A raised rim, and inside the rim smooth, and a little worn where people had touched it, or leaned on it, or put their backs to it waiting for something, so that the worn part was cleaner than the wall round it. His coat swung as he walked and the corner of the letter dug in under his ribs. His hand went flat on the coat over it and stayed there till the next landing.

It was a long way out. They stopped for Teo three times. The first time he only stood. The second time Eli had the coat off one shoulder to put round him before he thought about the pockets, the sock in the one and the letter in the other, and Teo looked at the coat and said "No," and Eli shrugged it back on. The third time Teo sat, on a bollard, with his head down between his arms, and Jon stood a little way off with his back to them and his hands in his pockets, and waited, and when Teo got up Jon walked on.

Out along the Spur the buildings got lower and further apart and the street got wider and fuller: trolleys and carts, men in grey, dock hands with hooks hung on their belts, a gang of orcs on a scaffold scraping a wall white, a woman selling slates off a blanket with their screens all lit and all showing the same face. The air had a new smell in it, hot metal and something burnt, and under the noise of the lanes there was a deeper one, a hum that came up through the boots. The berths. Ahead the street ran in under a long low roof with lights in it, and across the mouth of it, where everything had to go through, there was a barrier, and a booth with a window, and beside the booth an arch with a reader set in it. A queue went under the arch one face at a time, and each face got its light and its soft chime.

Jon went down the side of the queue, past all of it, to the booth window. Eli and Teo went with him.

The watch said nine.

Eli saw it on his wrist, and he saw Teo see it too, and look away, back up the Spur, the way they'd come. Eli turned the wrist over and back. The little moon was low in its window.

"What," he said.

Teo didn't say what.

On the side of the booth somebody had pasted a notice. Paper, which you hardly saw any more, gone soft with the wet off the street and lifting at one corner. NOTICE, it said at the head, in plain black, and under that TERRORIST CELL, and under that UNREST IN THE SCALD · ANY INFORMATION · REWARD, and under that a row of grey boxes the size of a face. In each box, small, in the middle, it said TO FOLLOW.

Teo read it. He read the whole of it, down to the line along the bottom, which was very small and which Eli let go.

"No faces," Teo said.

"They never have faces. They put them up so people stop asking." Eli rubbed his eye. "Dace'll have one in a frame by the weekend."

The man in the booth was human, heavy, his collar open, with a cup of something steaming on the ledge by his elbow. He had a slate by the cup and he was watching the arch, and the faces going under it, and nothing much. Jon put his forearms on the ledge outside the window and said something through the gap, low. The guard's eyes came up to Jon, and went past Jon, and stopped.

Teo was standing behind Jon's shoulder. He'd gone very still, with his weight back on his heels.

The guard looked at the wet on Teo's face. He looked at the shirt done up to the throat in the warm under the roof, and the arm held across under it, and where the arm pushed, the shirt had pulled open between two buttons and the dressing showed in the gap, and the yellow on the dressing. His eyes stayed on that longest. Then he got up off his stool and stepped back in the booth, away from the window, with his cup.

"Not him," he said. "Not through here."

Eli stepped forward.

"He's fine," he said. "It's a cut. It's healing. He did it on a—it's a cut." His voice had gone clear and easy. He heard it go. "If there's some sort of form, I'm happy to—"

Jon came off the ledge and stood across him, between him and the window, not quickly.

"Wait there," Jon said.

Eli stopped with his mouth open on the next word.

When I say do a thing, you do it. Then.

He shut his mouth. He stood. Behind him Teo had sat down. He hadn't meant to, from the look of him; his legs had just stopped, and he'd gone down onto the kerb with his back against the booth, under the notice, and his head down between his knees. Eli went down beside him and got an arm round his shoulders. Teo was hot through the shirt. He was shaking, small and fast, all through.

"I'm fine," Teo said, to his knees.

"I know. Stay there. I know."

Over his head Jon was talking to the window, low. Eli caught none of it except the tail, which was "—off a winch. Docks," and then the guard saying something back, and somebody in the queue saying "Come on," and the guard telling him to shut it. Then a laugh, short. He couldn't tell whose. He held on to Teo. Teo's breath went in and out against his arm.

When he looked up the guard was on his stool again with his cup, and the barrier was still down, and the arch was busy with the next face in the queue.

"Round the back, then," the guard said, to the window, to nobody. "Not my line."

"This way," said Jon.

Eli got Teo up. Teo came up with his good hand on the booth and stood, and on his feet he lifted his head and looked at Jon's back going away along the barrier, and then at Eli. He held it there. Then he looked at the ground and walked.

---

Round the back was a gap between two stacks of crates as high as a house, and then a lane with a gutter down the middle and a white wall along one side, and in the white wall, a long way along, a gate with no arch and no glass, only a disc set in the stone at the height of an orc's chest. A line of orcs waited at it. Thirty or forty, loaders, most of them in canvas with straps over the shoulders, some with hooks, standing along the wall in the order they'd come, one behind the other, close, not talking.

Jon walked down the side of the line the way he'd walked down the queue.

The line watched the gate. When the front orc stepped up, the disc lit for him, low and steady, and he stood in it while it read him, and his hands opened and shut once at his sides, and he went through. Then the next.

The next was old, grey in the stubble. He stepped up into the light and stood, and his eyes closed. Eli saw them close. He stood there with his face in the light and his shoulders down and his eyes shut, and the disc had read him and he hadn't moved on, one breath, two, and the light in the disc came up brighter. The old one's head jerked back. His mouth came open and his knees went, both at once, straight down onto the stone with a noise like a dropped bag, and he stayed there, kneeling at the gate, with his chin going and spit running out of the side of his mouth and his hands shaking open on his thighs.

The light went down again.

The orc behind him stepped round him into it and stood and was read and went through. The one after that did the same. Nobody bent down.

Jon came to him and stepped over his legs, one long step, not breaking stride, and went through the gate.

Eli went round. He had Teo by the arm and he went round wide, by the gutter, and his boot went in the gutter to the ankle and came out wet. He looked down at the old one going past. The old one was looking at the stone between his knees.

"Is he—" Eli said.

The line watched the gate.

The disc stayed dark for Eli, a hand from his face. It gave him nothing. He came through into the hot metal smell and the long roof with Teo against his side, and the disc stayed dark for Teo too, and behind them the next orc stepped round the old one into the light.

---

The berths ran along the city's lip under the long roof, one after another, cradles of black iron set out over the drop, and in each cradle a ship. The roof stopped where the floor stopped, and past that there was nothing, only air going down into haze, and the light coming in white and flat from the cloud overhead. Eli had never been to the public berths. His flyers had come to the kerb.

They walked the length of it. A big grey hauler with a company's stripes down her flank, lit up, her hatches open on long clean holds. A passenger boat with a queue at the ramp and a girl at its head in a cap taking slates. Two cradles empty, with men hosing them out. A white private thing with a crest on her nose that Eli half knew, gold on blue, from a house on the Seventh, and a man in house colours on her ramp eating his breakfast off a plate, who saw the coat and stood up. A long old one at the far end of a side spur on her own, grey and lean and ribbed down her length so you could count the ribs, with nobody loading her and her ramp shut. Teo turned his head and looked at her going by. Eli was looking at the crest.

The last berth before the roof gave out had the beater in it.

She was long and flat-bottomed and wide in the beam, and she'd been four colours once and was now about nine, patch on patch, plates of grey on plates of a different grey, with a sheet riveted over her nose by somebody who'd done it by hand, the rivet heads standing up all round it in a ragged row. Her landing legs were three. Two were the same length. Under the third somebody had put a block of wood. She sat in her cradle with a lean on her, nose down to the left, and from twenty yards off you could smell her: fish, and wet grain, and engine, and something sweet and old under all of it that had soaked in and wasn't coming out.

Eli laughed out loud.

"Look at her," he said. "Teo. Look at her."

Teo was looking.

"She's got a lean on her. Like the Spur." He was walking faster. "She's on a block of wood. Teo, she's standing on a block."

The ramp was down at her stern, and six orcs were loading her off a stack at the rim of the berth, crates on trolleys up the ramp and into the dark of her and back down empty. Every crate on the stack had the same stencil on its end, BONE MEAL and a grade, and under that, smaller, ITHE.

"Bone meal," Teo said.

"Ithe," Eli read. "What's an Ithe?"

Nobody told him.

At the foot of the ramp stood a woman with a board.

She was human, forty or so, in a flight jacket that had had a name tape over the pocket once and had only the stitch holes now. Her hair was scraped back and tied with a bit of wire. She had a tin cup in the hand without the board, and her eyes were red at the rims. Each time a crate went past her up the ramp she made a mark on the board. She kept her eyes on it until they were close enough that she had to look up.

She looked at Jon. Then past him.

"You said one," she said.

Jon was wiping his fingers on his trousers. "Two."

Her eyes went to Teo and stayed a while, on his face, and on the arm held under the shirt, and she drank out of the cup. Then she looked at Eli, and at his coat, and her eyes went over it. The collar. The cuffs. The cloth across the shoulders where it hung right. The buttons.

"That's Terrace," she said.

"It's my second-best," Eli said, and smiled at her. "You should see the other one."

"I could run her three months on that coat. Fuel and berths." A crate went by and she marked it. "Keep that thing out of sight."

"Absolutely," said Eli. "Not a soul. I'm Eli, this is—"

"No names." Her eyes stayed on the board. "I don't want them."

He opened his mouth and shut it again and laughed, and looked at Teo to see if Teo was laughing, and Teo wasn't. Teo was looking at the nearest crate on the stack. He went to it and sat down on it, slowly, and then all at once, and put his good hand on his knee.

Jon stood at the foot of the ramp with the pilot. He said something to her that Eli lost, low, with his head turned to the ship and not to her. She answered with her head turned the same way.

"After this I don't know you," she said.

"You don't know me now."

She drank out of the cup.

A crate went up the ramp. The orc pushing it was big through the back and had his eyes on the trolley's front wheel. Eli stepped out of his way and the orc went past.

"Good man," Eli said.

The orc went on up into the dark with the trolley.

Jon turned round. He looked at Eli, and took his hands out of his pockets.

"Got elves to see," he said.

Eli laughed. It came up out of him and it hurt his face. "Elves," he said. "Of course. Elves. Give them my love."

Jon waited till he'd finished.

"Ithe," he said. "There's a man there'll find you."

"Ithe. That's—" Eli looked at the stack, at the stencils. "Where's that? Is that far? Is that the teacher? Is that—you said a crew. You said you were putting a crew together, you never said—" He stopped. "When will you—"

Jon's eyes had gone to Teo, on the crate, and the hand inside the shirt. They stayed there a little. They came back.

"That'll want looking at," he said.

"I know it will. I know. When will you—"

"Do as she says, son."

He went. He went back the way they'd come along the berths, not hurrying, with his coat open and his hands loose at his sides. Eli went after him one step, two, out of the ramp's shadow onto the bright floor, and stopped there.

Jon went past the ribbed ship and the white one with the crest. A cart came across the walk in front of him, piled with sacks, and he went round the back of it and into the queue at the passenger boat, and through the queue and out among the men in grey on the far side, a big grey head going away, and then a lot of heads, and then none of them his.

Eli stood and looked at the place. The queue shuffled up a step and closed it.

"When will you," he said, to the walk.

Behind him the pilot made a mark on her board.

"Ithe," she said, and breathed out once through her nose. It might have been a laugh.

He turned round. "You know it? Where is it? How long does it take?"

"Hold's back there," she said. "Don't touch the straps."

---

The hold was long and low, dark aft, and lit forward by two strips in the ceiling, one of which went off and on. Crates were stacked to the ribs down both sides and strapped, with a gangway between them a man wide, and halfway down, against the starboard wall, a bench that folded out on chains. The smell was worse inside. It had a sound too, a tick in the hull somewhere, and a hum that went up through the floor into his teeth and sat there, next to the headache.

Eli helped Teo down the gangway. Teo shook the arm off, and then let it come back. He sat on the first crate past the bench, not on the bench, and put his back to the stack behind him and shut his eyes, and opened them.

"Look at this," Eli said. He stood in the gangway and turned round, taking it in. The ribs. The patched plates, inside as well as out, and a seam over the bench with a strip of grey tape run along it, and a second strip over that where the first had lifted. He put his thumb on it. It gave. "Teo. It's tape. She's held together with tape."

Teo looked at the tape. Then past it, at a plate riveted by the hatch, small and brass and gone green round the rim, with letters stamped in it, and a number, and a little mark like a fish.

"Old hull," Teo said. "They stopped making these."

"How do you know that?"

Teo let it go by, and Eli was already moving. He went back up the gangway to the open ramp and stood at the head of it, in the hatch, out of the way, with the bright floor of the berths below and the orcs coming up and down.

They were nearly done. The stack was down to its last row. The six of them worked in two threes, one three bringing the loaded trolleys up, the other running the empties down and loading them, and they kept off the common talk, but now and then one said a word in the orc talk and the one it was said to answered with a grunt, or nothing, or a word. One of them had a strap on his harness that had gone and been mended. It was stitched back on to the buckle in a different colour, brown thread on the grey, big stitches, done by somebody who was no good at it and had done it anyway. He was young. He said something to the one beside him going down the ramp, quick and low, and the other snorted and shoved him with his shoulder, and the young one shoved back, and the pilot at the bottom said "You," without lifting her head from her board, and they both went quiet and went on down.

Eli watched them come and go. They went round him in the hatch. They looked at the coat, once, going past, or they looked at nothing.

The last loaded trolley came up with the big one behind it pushing. Halfway up, the strap on the top crate let go. It went with a crack and the crate slid, slow, then not slow, off the side of the trolley and over the ramp rail, down toward the berth floor, and on the floor under it the young orc with the mended strap was bending to the stack with his back to the ramp.

The big one had his head down behind the trolley. The pilot had her head down over her board. Nobody was looking up the ramp.

Eli's hand was up. The crate stopped.

It stopped in the air on its corner, a man's height over the young one's back, and hung there. Heavy. He had all of it, every bit of how heavy it was, in his arm and in his teeth and up the back of his head, and he held it, and brought it back. It came up, slow, and back over the rail, and settled onto the trolley with a knock, a hand's width from where it had been, crooked.

The big orc lifted his head at the knock. He looked at the crate, and at the strap hanging off it, and at the crate again, and round, down the ramp. The young one had straightened up from the stack and was looking up too, at the knock, with a crate in his arms.

Eli had his hand down at his side by then. He put it in his pocket. Under his fingers was the sock, low and heavy, the knot in it gone hard.

The big one set his shoulder to the trolley and pushed it on up past Eli into the hold.

"Strap," the pilot said from below. She'd seen the end of it, the strap. She marked her board. "That's off your day."

The big one went on into the dark without answering.

Something warm ran out of Eli's nose onto his lip. He wiped it with the back of his other wrist, and the wrist came away red. He turned to the open side of the cradle and the air past it, and wiped again, and again, and swallowed. His stomach came up into his throat. He swallowed that too. The headache had gone up a notch behind the eye and was beating now, quick, and his palm in the pocket was wet on the sock. With his back to all of them he looked out at the haze, white, a long way down, and a flyer going across it very small, until the bleeding stopped.

He wiped the wrist on the lining of the coat. He took his hand out of the pocket and looked at it then. It looked like a hand.

The young orc came up last with the empty trolley for the hold and the loading canvas over his shoulder, a big square of it, filthy, that they'd had over the stack. In the hold he ran the trolley in against the bulkhead and set its brake with his foot. Then he took the canvas off his shoulder and shook it out once. He folded it in half, and in half again, the long way and then across, corners to corners, quick and square, and put it on the crate by the hatch, and pressed it flat with the side of his hand, and went past Eli and down the ramp.

"Good—" Eli said.

It stopped there. He stood with it in his mouth. The orc went on, and the rest of it stayed where it was, and at the foot of the ramp the orc fell in with the others and they went off along the berth together, the six of them, toward the white wall.

The pilot came up the ramp with her board under her arm and stopped beside him in the hatch.

"In or off," she said. "I'm not asking twice."

Eli looked back along the walk. The passenger boat. The queue, with no big grey head in it. The white ship with the crest. The man in house colours had put his plate down on the rail and was standing at the foot of his own ramp with a slate in his hand, looking along the berths at the beater, at the hatch, at the coat in it.

Twenty minutes to the Seventh, in that. Less.

He looked down the hold. Teo sat on his crate with his eyes shut and his head back against the stack and his hand in his shirt.

Out on the white ramp the man lifted the slate.

Eli stepped back out of the light, into the hold.

The pilot went past him, and past Teo, and counted the crates down the starboard side with her finger, and down the port side, and hit a plate on the bulkhead with the side of her fist. Behind Eli the ramp began to come up.

"Mind your feet," she said.

---

She flew from a chair at the front of the hold, in a box the width of a cupboard, through a hatch in the bulkhead with a door on it hanging open on one hinge. The other hinge was in a tin on the floor by her foot. Ahead of her was glass, a long low curve of it, scratched, with a crack across one corner that somebody had filled with clear stuff and the clear stuff had gone yellow, and below the glass her board, lit, and two sticks, and a holder with her cup in it.

"Bench," she said. "Belts on the bench you can touch."

Eli got Teo onto the bench. Teo went where he was put. Eli got the belt across him, canvas with a hook on it that went into a ring in the wall, and got his own across, and his bad hand closed partway on the hook and stopped with the fingers curled, and he did it with the other, and it took him three goes to find the ring. From the bench, if he leaned forward, he could see past her shoulder through the hanging door: a slice of the glass and the white beyond it.

The ship shuddered. The tick in the hull went quicker, and quicker, and stopped being a tick. Something under the floor began to roar, a long way off and then not far off at all, and the floor came up under them, and from outside, under the stern, there was a crack and a clatter, and Eli thought: the block of wood.

They lifted.

Through the door he saw the roof go by, and the light change, and then the berths fall away under them, sideways, the whole long row of them with their ships, the grey hauler and the white one, small already, and the ribbed one out on her spur. The beater turned. She turned slowly, with the roar going up and down, and swung her nose round, and through the scratched glass the city came across it.

He'd seen it before. He'd had his flyers. He'd seen it from the lanes every day of his life, from the Vennicks' roof, from his own window. He'd never seen it falling away. The mid-levels went by under them as a plate of roofs, grey on grey, and the lanes went by in strings, and the beater climbed up through the lanes and the lanes went under, the lowest one, the next, the highest, the traffic in them going on along them small and bright and not looking up. Then there was nothing over them but the beater's own noise. Below, on the first rise of land, a line of white houses, very small, with all their windows turned one way.

All their windows turned to the Tower, and the Tower was in the glass.

It stood in the middle of it, pale, up out of the oldest ground, and the city kept back from it in a ring, and the beater climbed, and it went on up out of the glass. She climbed and climbed and the roofs went to a grey map and the map went soft round its rim, and the Tower got no shorter. The glass went on cutting it off. Then the cloud came round them, white, then grey, then white, and the whole hull shook and something in the stern went bang and went on banging, and they came out on top.

The cloud lay out flat in every direction, bright, all the way to the sky. Out of the middle of it the Tower came up, pale, smooth, in one piece, and went on up out of the cloud and out of the glass, and Eli bent forward against his belt to see more of it, and there was no more. There was the frame. He bent further. The belt stopped him.

"Teo," he said. "Teo. Look."

The pilot said something to her board, flat and quick, in the voice people used for numbers, and the board answered her in the same voice. With the banging, Eli caught none of it. On the board a line came up green.

Teo had his head back against the wall and his eyes half shut.

"Earth," Teo said.

Eli had his face to the glass. "What?"

Teo didn't say it again. On the board the line went away and another came up under it.

The beater was turning, swinging her nose off, and the Tower slid along the glass and off its side, and then there was only the cloud, and the sky going dark above it, and then the sky going black.

Eli put his palm on the wall of the hold beside him. The cold came through the plate into his hand, colder than anything all day.

"Teo," he said. "It's black."

Teo's chest went up and down.

---

Teo was sick before the hour was out.

He did it neatly, leaning out over the belt, between his own boots, and wiped his mouth on his sleeve and sat back. His face was the colour of the tape. When Eli put the back of his hand to Teo's forehead the heat came off him into it, and Eli took the hand away and looked at it.

"Let me see your hand."

"No."

"Teo. Let me see it."

Teo looked at the bulkhead opposite. He was breathing short through his mouth. Eli unhooked his own belt and got down off the bench onto his knees, and undid Teo's shirt, the bottom button, the next, and the slate slid down against Teo's hip, and Teo let him, and the arm came out. Teo held it out a little way, turned up, and looked at the bulkhead.

The dressing was his mother's knot. The linen had gone yellow right through and brown round the rim, and it had stuck. Eli picked at the knot with the nails of his good hand. It held. He got his teeth to it, and it went, and he unwound it, round and round, and the last of it was stuck to the palm and he pulled that and Teo made a noise in his throat, and it came.

The smell came with it.

The cut went across the palm from the root of the first finger nearly to the wrist. It had closed at the ends. In the middle it hadn't, or it had and come open, and the lips of it stood up, swollen, white and wet, soft-looking, and down in the bottom of it there was yellow, and the yellow moved when Teo's fingers moved. All round it the hand was tight and shiny and red, and the red went on over the heel of the hand and up the inside of the wrist, and up from the wrist there was a line in the skin, thin and red, going up the arm under the sleeve.

Eli pushed the sleeve back. The line went up past the elbow.

He sat back on his heels.

"Yes," he said. "Good. Yes." His eyes went round the hold, and to the pilot's back through the door. "I'll ring. I'll ring the account, they'll send something out, there'll be somewhere we can—"

"They log it," Teo said.

"I don't care if they log it."

"Nothing rings out of here," said the pilot, with her back to him.

"Then where's—have you got—there must be something on board, a kit, a—"

She reached under her chair without looking and brought out a tin box with a cross on it, and threw it back through the door over her shoulder. It hit the floor of the hold and came open and slid, and things came out of it.

"It's what there is," she said.

Eli gathered it up off the floor. A roll of tape, grey, the same as the tape on the seam. A tube of something with a split in its side, gone hard. A packet of pads with one pad in it. A blade in a paper sleeve. A card of pills with every pill on it punched out, all twelve, the foil standing up in little torn crowns.

He held the card up and looked at it.

There was a laugh in his chest. He could feel it sitting there. He put the card down on the bench.

"This isn't anything," he said. "This is nothing. This is a—" He got up. He went to the door with the card in his hand and stood in it, holding the frame. Past her the glass was black now, with a few hard points of light in it, and the board was lit green and amber, and her cup was in its holder, and she had one hand on a stick and the other on her knee. "He's got a line up his arm."

She didn't look round.

"Name it," Eli said. "Whatever it is. Whatever you've got. Name it."

She sat a bit with her hand on her knee. Then she took the other hand off the stick, and the ship flew on without it, and she reached inside the flight jacket and brought out a flat tin, the kind sweets came in, old, the paint worn off the lid, and held it on her knee unopened.

"Kit's a joke," she said. "This isn't."

"Yes. What is it? I'll pay. I can pay." His hand had gone into his coat pocket already, down to the bottom, to the sock. He felt the knot, and the coins under it through the wool, shifting.

The pilot turned her head and looked at him for the first time since the ramp. Then her eyes went down to his wrist on the doorframe.

"Not marks," she said. "That."

The four faces. The little moon in its window, low.

"It's only a watch," he said. He said it quick.

Behind him Teo said, "Don't."

He said it once, flat, from the bench, with his hand held out turned up where Eli had left it. Eli didn't turn round. His hand came out of the pocket and let go of the sock, and the sock settled at the bottom, and he undid the watch. The buckle was stiff with the dirt in it, and he worked it with the thumb of the bad hand until it gave. He held it out to her, and the strap hung down off his fingers, and she took it.

She paid no mind to the faces. She weighed it in her hand once, and turned it over, and looked at the back, and put it inside her flight jacket where the tin had been. Nothing went on her board. Then she opened the tin on her knee.

There were two kinds in it. White ones, flat and small. And three yellow ones, longer, in a twist of paper of their own. She counted white ones out into his palm, six, and then took the twist with the yellow ones and put that in his palm too, on top.

"Two white now," she said. "Two tonight. Two in the morning. Yellow if he goes off his head."

"Off his—"

"You'll know." She shut the tin. "Clean it out first. With the blade. All the yellow, down to where it bleeds. Then tape it shut. He'll shout." She put her hand back on the stick. "It'll hold him a few days. Then he wants a proper one."

"A proper what?"

She was watching her board.

---

He did it on the floor of the hold, on his knees, with Teo on the bench above him. He'd given him the two white ones first, and Teo had taken them dry, one, two, his throat working, and then put the back of his head to the wall and his good hand flat on the bench and shut his eyes.

"Ready," Eli said.

Teo said nothing.

Eli took the blade out of its paper. Very small. He laid Teo's arm across his own thigh, palm up, and put the bad hand round the wrist to hold it, and the hand closed partway and stopped, the fingers curled, and the wrist lay loose in it and slid.

"It won't," he said. "My hand. It won't—"

"Kneel on it," Teo said, with his eyes shut.

Eli looked at him. Then he laid Teo's forearm along the bench, palm up, and got his knee across the wrist, and leaned. Teo's breath went out of him.

He put the point in at the end of the cut, where it was white, and pushed, and the white opened and the yellow came, a lot of it, more than there could be room for, and ran out down the side of Teo's hand onto the bench and onto Eli's trousers, and the smell came up off it so that his eyes watered and he turned his head and breathed out through his mouth and turned back. Teo's whole arm went hard under his knee. He didn't shout. He made a sound with his mouth shut, high up in his nose, and kept making it, and his heels drummed twice on the floor and stopped.

"Nearly," Eli said. "Nearly. Nearly."

Along it, then. Down into it with the point, scraping, and he wiped the blade on his knee, and went in again. His good hand had stopped shaking. He noticed that, a long way off. He scraped until it came red at the bottom instead of yellow and then went on a little past that in case, and then it was bleeding, properly, red, running into the cup of Teo's palm, and he pressed the one pad down into it hard with the heel of his hand and held it there.

Teo had stopped making the sound. He was breathing in short pulls through his nose. His eyes were shut and the tears were coming out from under the lids and down the sides of his face into his ears, and he left them.

"Done," Eli said. "That's it. That's all of it. Done."

He got his knee off. He taped it. He went round and round the hand with the grey tape until the pad was buried and the tape went over the knuckles and round the wrist, and he bit it off with his teeth, and it looked like nothing. He laid it on Teo's chest. Teo's good hand came up off the bench and found it and held it there, at the wrist, and stayed.

Eli sat back against the crate opposite. He had yellow on his trousers and on his cuffs, the second-best coat's cuffs, and on his hands, and he looked at it, and wiped his hands on the coat, and looked at them again.

"There," he said.

The hull ticked. In the stern the banging had gone back to a knock, every so often, uneven, something back there loose and swinging. The roar under the floor had dropped to a hum. Through the door the pilot sat with one hand on her knee, and her cup in its holder, and the black in the glass.

Eli lifted his wrist to see how long it had been. There was nothing on it. Only the mark where the strap had been, a band of paler skin with a line of grey in the crease of it, and the hairs flat. He turned it to the light, and put the arm down.

"Teo," he said, after a while.

Teo's eyes stayed shut.

"The board. When we came out on top. What did it say?"

Teo lay with his head back against the wall and his good hand on the taped one. His mouth was a little open.

"Clear of Earth," he said.

Eli waited for the rest. The hum went on in the floor.

"Ithe," he said. "Have you heard of it?"

Teo's chest went up and down. He might have been asleep. Eli watched him to see.

The letter was against his ribs. He put his hand inside the coat and found it, the corner first and then the whole flat of it, warm from him, and the wax on the far side under his fingers, whole. He kept his hand there.

Aft, in the dark, the loose thing knocked, and was quiet, and knocked.
