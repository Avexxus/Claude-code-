## FOUR · The Beater

The culvert let out at the back of the lift, under the Spur's head, where the wall ran wet and the morning hadn't got down to yet. Jon went up the last of the steps and out into the street without looking to see if they were behind him. He'd finished the pear. He dropped the core in the gutter, and the gutter water took it and turned it and carried it off along the kerb toward the Tower, uphill.

Eli came up into the noise after him. The lanes were going over three deep with the first of the day's traffic, and the feeds were coming on along the walls one after another as the light got to them, and somewhere a hose was running on stone. He'd been on the Spur a hundred times. He'd been here four nights ago with the purse knocking on his ribs. He stood at the top of the culvert steps and put the heel of his hand to his eye and pressed.

It was the headache. It sat in there behind the eye, and in both ears, and in the hinge of his jaw where he'd been clenching it all the way down the steps. He'd had it all his life. Everybody had it. There was a stall on the corner with blue pills in paper twists laid out on a tray, and a woman was buying two and telling the man the last two hadn't done anything. Eli took his hand down. It was still there. He could feel exactly where it was, the edges of it, and he couldn't stop feeling them.

"Teo," he said. "Keep up."

Teo came up the steps one at a time with his good hand flat on the wall. The bad one was inside his coat, against his chest, and he'd buttoned the coat over it. His face was grey under the brown and wet along the hairline. He got to the top and stood there and breathed through his nose.

"I'm fine," he said.

"I didn't ask."

The lift's core sat in its glass box at the Spur's head where it always sat, the grey lump with the threads going out of it, wet. A car was coming down. The lump twitched. Eli stopped and bent to the glass and put his finger on it.

"Watch it when the next one goes," he said. "It's alive, you know. Sort of. Some of it." He looked round. Teo wasn't watching it. Teo was watching Jon's back, going away down the middle of the street at the same pace he'd crossed the yard. "It's all right, it can't see you."

He went after Jon. Teo came after him.

The street leaned. It had always leaned. The Spur ran one way, out from the Tower toward the edge, and the floor under it ran the other, so that walking out along it you were walking very slightly uphill, every step, and your ankles knew it before you did. Eli's ankles knew it. He'd never once noticed. He noticed now, and it went on, and there was nothing to do about it but walk. In the window boxes the plants grew out sideways over the street, every one of them pointing the same way, back over his shoulder, and when he turned to look where they were pointing the Tower was there at the end of the street, pale, filling it, going up out of the top of the street and out of sight.

"Teo," he said. "The plants."

Teo didn't look at the plants.

---

The flesh market was open. They were hosing the slabs down, and the water ran off the ends of the trestles and into the gutter and went uphill with everything else, pink. The cold-cases were up. CLONE STOCK — NO SOUL, black on white on every end, with the grade under. A boy was carrying ice in a basket bigger than he was. A woman in an apron sorted kidneys into two trays, the good and the less good, quick, without looking at her hands, talking to the woman at the next stall about her sister.

Eli walked through it beside Teo, pointing.

"Eyes," he said. "He does them by colour. There. And there's a dwarf at the end who sells hands in pairs. Matched. With a tag." He'd seen it in a feed. He'd seen it four nights ago, in the dark, with the tarps down. "The real ones are under the counter. You have to ask."

Teo walked. His feet were wrong on the wet. He went slower than he looked as if he was going, and twice Eli had to stop and wait, and the second time he took him by the elbow, the good side, and Teo let him.

At the far end of the cases a man had a burner going under an iron plate, and strips of something on the plate, spitting, and a stack of cones twisted out of old feed sheet. Jon stopped there. It was the first thing Eli had seen him stop for since the kitchen.

"Two," he said.

The man turned the strips with his fingers. "Clone or the other?"

"Whatever's hot."

The man looked at him and shovelled a cone full off the hot end of the plate. Jon paid him from his trouser pocket, coin, and walked on eating. He didn't wait for change. He didn't look round. A little way on he held the cone back over his shoulder without turning his head, and Eli, who was nearest, took a strip out of it.

It was hot and salt and it was good. He ate it.

"That's good," he said. "What is that?"

Jon ate.

Eli held the cone out to Teo. Teo looked at it, and at the plate back there with the man scraping it, and shook his head once, and kept walking with his hand in his coat. Eli shrugged and gave the cone back to Jon's shoulder, and Jon's hand came back and took it.

---

The feeds on the walls ran with the sound off. A queue somewhere under an orange sky. A ship's hull going into dock. LEGION and a figure. A man's face, smiling, a minister, then gone. TERRACE PROPERTY VALUES, and a line under it going down.

At the landings, where the side stairs came up from the levels below, the walls went from paint to grey stone, and on every one of them there was the seal, stamped, an oval with nothing in it. Eli had passed under a thousand of them. He looked at one now going by. It was very plain. It had a raised rim, and inside the rim it was smooth and a little worn where people had touched it, or leaned on it, or put their backs to it waiting for something, and the smooth part was cleaner than the wall round it.

"Teo."

"What."

"Nothing," Eli said. "Nothing. Keep up."

Out toward the end of the Spur the buildings got lower and further apart and the street got wider and fuller, trolleys and carts and men in grey and dock hands with hooks hung on their belts, and the air had a new smell in it, hot metal and something burnt, and under the noise of the lanes there was a deeper noise, a hum that came up through the boots. The berths. Ahead the street ran out under a long low roof with lights in it, and across the street at the near end, where everything had to go through, there was a barrier, and a booth, and an arch with a reader set in it.

There was a queue. Jon walked down the side of it to the end and stopped, and stood there eating, and Eli and Teo stood behind him.

The watch said nine.

Eli looked at it, and he saw Teo look at it too, at his wrist, and then away, at the booth. Eli turned the wrist over and back. The little moon was low in its window.

"What," he said.

Teo didn't say what.

On the side of the booth somebody had pasted a notice. It was paper, which you didn't see much, gone soft with the wet off the street and lifting at one corner. The seal was printed at the top of it, the empty oval, and under it in black, TERRORIST CELL, and under that, UNREST IN THE SCALD · ANY INFORMATION · REWARD, and under that a row of grey boxes the size of a face. In each box, small, in the middle, it said TO FOLLOW.

Teo read it. He read the whole of it. He read the line along the bottom too, which was very small and which Eli didn't bother with.

"No faces," Teo said.

"They never have faces. They put them up so people stop asking." Eli rubbed his eye. "Dace'll have them in a frame by the weekend."

The queue moved up. The reader in the arch gave each face that went under it its light, and its soft chime, and the man in the booth looked at each one going by and at nothing much, a human, heavy, his collar open, a cup of something steaming on the ledge in front of him. A woman went through with a trolley. Two dock hands. A man in grey who held his slate up to the booth and didn't stop walking. Jon finished the cone and folded the paper in four and put it in his pocket.

Teo was next after Jon. He'd gone very still in the queue, with his weight on his heels, and when the guard's eyes came to him they stayed.

The guard looked at the wet on Teo's face. He looked at the coat buttoned to the neck in the warm and the arm under it, held across. The coat had pulled open at the bottom button where the arm pushed against it, and the dressing showed in the gap, and the yellow on the dressing. The guard looked at that longest. Then he stood up off his stool and took a step back in the booth, away from the window, with his cup.

"Not him," he said. "Not through here."

Eli stepped forward.

"He's fine," he said. "It's a cut. It's healing. He did it on a—it's a cut." His voice had gone clear and easy. He heard it go. "If there's some sort of form, I'm happy to—"

Jon stepped across in front of him, between him and the booth, not quickly.

"Wait there," Jon said.

Eli stopped with his mouth open on the next word.

When I say do a thing, you do it. Then.

He shut his mouth. He stood. Behind him Teo had sat down. He hadn't meant to, from the look of him; his legs had just stopped, and he'd gone down onto the kerb by the booth with his back against the wall of it, under the notice, and his head down between his knees. Eli went down beside him and got an arm round behind his shoulders. Teo was hot through the coat. He was shaking, small and fast, all through.

"I'm fine," Teo said, to his knees.

"I know. Stay there. I know."

Over his head Jon was saying something to the window, low. Eli didn't hear it. He heard the end of it, which was "—on a hook. Docks," and he heard the guard say something back, and somebody in the queue behind them say "Come on," and the guard tell him to shut it. Then a laugh, short, he couldn't tell whose. He held on to Teo. Teo's breath went in and out against his arm.

When he looked up the guard was sitting down again with his cup, and the barrier was still down, and the arch was looking at the next face in the queue.

"Round the back, then," the guard said, to nobody, to the window. "Not my line."

"This way," said Jon.

Eli got Teo up. Teo came up with his good hand on the booth and stood, and on his feet he lifted his head and looked at Jon's back going away along the barrier, and then at Eli. He held it there. Then he looked at the ground in front of him and started walking.

---

Round the back was a gap between two stacks of crates as high as a house, and then a lane with a gutter down the middle of it and a white wall on one side, and in the white wall, a long way down, a gate with no arch and no glass, only a disc set in the stone at the height of an orc's chest. There was a line of orcs at it. Thirty or forty of them, loaders, most of them in canvas with straps over the shoulders, some with hooks, some with nothing, standing in a line along the wall in the order they'd come, one behind the other, close, not talking.

Jon walked down the side of the line the way he'd walked down the queue.

The line didn't look at him. It looked at the gate. When the front orc stepped up to the disc the disc lit for him, low and steady, and he stood in it while it read him, and his hands opened and shut once at his sides, and he went through. Then the next.

About ten from the front an orc had his eyes shut. He was old, grey in the stubble, and he'd been standing with his shoulder to the wall, and he must have stood one step too long, because the one in front of him had gone up and he hadn't. The disc had gone on looking. Eli saw the light in it brighten, a little. Then the old one's head came up off the wall hard and his mouth came open and his knees went, both at once, straight down onto the stone with a noise like a dropped bag, and he stayed there, kneeling, with his chin going and spit running out of the side of his mouth, and his hands shaking open in front of him.

The orc behind him stepped round him and closed up the gap. The one behind that did the same. Nobody bent down.

Jon came to him and stepped over his legs, one long step, not breaking stride, and went on toward the gate.

Eli went round him. He had Teo by the arm and he went round wide, by the gutter, and his boot went in the gutter to the ankle and came out wet. He looked down at the orc as he passed. The orc was looking at the stone between his hands.

"Is he—" Eli said.

The line looked at the gate.

At the gate the disc stayed dark for Jon. It stayed dark for Eli, a hand from his face. It didn't look at him. He came through into the noise and the hot metal smell and the long roof with Teo against his side, and the disc stayed dark for Teo too, and behind them the line closed up and the next orc stepped into the light.

---

The berths ran along the edge of the city under the long roof, one after another, cradles of black iron set out over the drop, and in each cradle a ship. The roof stopped at the edge and past it there was nothing, only air, and the light coming in white off the cloud. Eli had never been to the public berths. His flyers had come to the kerb.

They walked the length of it. A big grey hauler with a company's stripes down her flank, lit up, her hatches open on long clean holds. A passenger boat with a queue at the ramp and a girl at the top of it in a cap taking slates. Two cradles empty, with men hosing them. A white private thing with a crest on her nose that Eli half knew, gold on blue, from a house on the Seventh, and a man in house colours on the ramp eating his breakfast off a plate, who saw the coat and stood up. A long one, very old, at the end of a side spur on her own, grey and lean and ribbed down her length so that you could count the ribs, with nobody loading her and nothing on her ramp, and the heat coming off her in a shimmer you could see from the walk. Teo turned his head and looked at her as they went by. Eli was looking at the crest.

The last berth before the end of the roof had the beater in it.

She was long and flat-bottomed and wide in the beam, and she'd been four colours once and was now about nine, patch on patch, plates of grey on plates of different grey, with a sheet riveted over her nose by somebody doing it by hand, the rivet heads standing up all the way round like a row of teeth. Her landing legs were three. Two were the same length. Under the third somebody had put a block of wood. She sat in her cradle with a lean on her, nose down to the left, and from twenty yards off you could smell her: fish, and wet grain, and engine, and something sweet and old under all of it that had soaked in and wasn't coming out.

Eli laughed out loud.

"Look at her," he said. "Teo. Look at her."

Teo was looking.

"She's got a lean on her. Like the Spur." He was walking faster. "She's got a block of wood. She's standing on a block of wood."

The ramp was down at her stern and a gang of orcs was loading her off a stack at the berth's edge, six of them, crates on trolleys up the ramp and into the dark of her and back down empty. At the foot of the ramp stood a woman with a board.

She was human, forty or so, in a flight jacket that had had a name tape over the pocket once and didn't now, only the stitch holes. Her hair was scraped back and tied with a bit of wire. She had a tin cup in the hand that wasn't holding the board and her eyes were red at the rims. She made a mark on the board each time a crate went past her up the ramp. She didn't look up until they were close enough that she had to.

She looked at Jon. Then past him.

"You said one," she said.

Jon was wiping his fingers on his trousers. "Two."

She looked at Teo. She looked at him for a while, at his face, and at the arm under the coat, and the coat buttoned over it, and she drank out of the cup. Then she looked at Eli, and at his coat, and her eyes went over it the way the steward's went over a bill. The collar. The cuffs. The cloth across the shoulders where it hung right. The buttons.

"That's Terrace," she said.

"It's my second-best," Eli said, and smiled at her. "You should see the other one."

"I could run her three months on that coat." She made a mark on her board as a crate went by. "Fuel and berths. Three months. You keep it in the hold. I don't want it stood on my ramp at the other end."

"Done," said Eli. "Absolutely. I'm Eli, this is—"

"No names." She didn't look up. "I don't want them."

He opened his mouth and shut it again and laughed, and looked at Teo to see if Teo was laughing, and Teo wasn't. Teo was looking at the nearest crate on the stack. He went to it and sat down on it, slowly, all at once at the end, and put his good hand on his knee.

Jon stood at the foot of the ramp with the pilot. He said something to her that Eli didn't catch, low, with his head turned to the ship and not to her. She answered without turning her head either.

"After this I don't know you," she said.

"You don't know me now."

She drank out of the cup. She made a mark.

A crate went up the ramp. The orc pushing it was big through the back and he had his eyes on the trolley's front wheel. Eli stepped out of his way and the orc went past.

"Good man," Eli said.

The orc didn't answer. He went on up into the dark with the trolley.

Jon turned round. He looked at Eli, and took his hands out of his pockets.

"Got elves to see," he said.

Eli laughed. It came up out of him and it hurt his face. "Elves," he said. "Right. Of course. Elves."

Jon waited till he'd finished.

"Ithe," he said. "There's a man there'll find you."

"Where? Where's that?" Eli came a step toward him. "Is that far? Is that the teacher? Is that—you said a crew. You said you were putting a crew together, you didn't say—" He stopped. "When will you—"

Jon's eyes had gone to Teo, on the crate, and the hand inside the coat. They stayed there a little. They came back.

"That'll want looking at," he said.

"I know it will. I know. When will you—"

"Do as she says."

He went. He went back the way they'd come, along the edge of the berths, not hurrying, with his coat open and his hands loose at his sides. Eli went after him one step, two, off the end of the ramp's shadow onto the bright floor, and he stopped there.

Jon went past the ribbed ship and the white one with the crest and the passenger boat with its queue. He didn't turn round. He didn't lift a hand. A cart came across between, piled with sacks, and when it had gone by he wasn't on the walk any more, and Eli stood and looked at the place where he'd been, and the cart went on down to the far end and turned in at an empty cradle and stopped.

"When will you," Eli said, to the walk.

Behind him the pilot made a mark on her board.

"Ithe," she said, and breathed out once through her nose. It might have been a laugh.

He turned round. "You know it? Where is it? How long does it take?"

"Hold's back there," she said. "Don't touch the straps."

---

The hold was long and low and dark at the far end, and lit at the near end by two strips in the ceiling, one of which went off and on. The crates were stacked to the ribs down both sides and strapped, and there was a gangway between them a man wide, and halfway down, against the starboard wall, a bench that folded out of it on chains. The smell was worse inside. It had a sound too, a tick in the hull somewhere, and a hum that went up through the floor and into his teeth and sat there, next to the headache.

Eli helped Teo down the gangway. Teo didn't want helping and let it happen. He sat on the first crate past the bench, not on the bench, and put his back to the stack behind and shut his eyes, and opened them, and read the stencil on the crate across from him.

"Bone meal," Teo said.

"Is it? Good. Lovely." Eli stood in the gangway and turned round, looking at it all. The ribs. The patched plates, inside as well as out, and a seam over the bench with a strip of grey tape run along it, and a second strip over that where the first had lifted. He put his thumb on it. It gave. "Teo, look. It's held together with tape. Look. It's tape."

Teo looked at the tape. Then he looked past it, at a plate riveted beside the hatch, small and brass and gone green at the edges, with letters stamped in it, and a number, and a little mark like a fish.

"Old hull," Teo said. "They stopped making these."

"How do you know that?"

Teo didn't answer, and Eli didn't wait for him to. He went back up the gangway to the open ramp and stood at the top of it, in the hatch, out of the way, with the bright floor of the berths at the bottom and the orcs coming up and down.

They were nearly done. The stack on the berth's edge was down to its last row. The six of them worked in two threes, one three bringing the loaded trolleys up, the other running the empties down and loading them, and they didn't talk to each other, not in the common talk, but now and then one of them said a word in the orc talk, and the one it was said to answered with a grunt, or nothing, or a word. One of them had a strap on his harness that had gone and been mended. It was stitched back on to the buckle in a different colour, brown thread on the grey, big stitches, done by somebody who wasn't any good at it and had done it anyway. He was young. He said something to the one beside him going down the ramp, quick and low, and the other one snorted and shoved him with his shoulder, and the young one shoved back, and the pilot at the bottom of the ramp said "You," without looking up from her board, and they both went quiet and went on down.

Eli watched them come and go. They went round him in the hatch. They didn't look at him, or they looked at the coat, once, going past, and then not.

The last loaded trolley came up the ramp with the big one behind it pushing. Halfway up, the front strap on the top crate let go. It went with a crack and the crate slid, slow, then not slow, and went over the side rail at the ramp's edge and hung there on its corner, tipping out over the drop past the edge of the cradle, and under it on the berth floor the young orc with the mended strap was bending to the stack with his back to it.

The big one had his head down behind the trolley. The pilot had her head down over her board. Nobody was looking up the ramp.

Eli's hand was up. The crate stopped. It stopped on its corner out over the edge with the air under it, and sat there, and then it came back, slow, and settled down onto the trolley again with a knock, a hand's width from where it had been, crooked.

The big orc lifted his head at the knock. He looked at the crate, and at the strap hanging off it, and at the crate again. He looked round, down the ramp. The young one at the bottom had straightened up from the stack and was looking up too, at the knock, with a crate in his arms.

Eli had his hand down at his side by then. He put it in his pocket. Under his fingers in the pocket was the sock, low and heavy, the knot in it gone hard.

The big one set his shoulder to the trolley and pushed it on up past Eli into the hold.

"Strap," the pilot said from the foot of the ramp. She'd seen the end of it, the strap. She made a mark on her board. "That's off your day."

The big one went on up into the dark without answering.

Eli stood in the hatch with his hand in his pocket on the sock. His palm was wet. Down at the bottom of his head, behind the eye, the headache had gone up a notch and was pulsing now, quick, and he could taste something faint at the back of his jaw. He swallowed it. He didn't look at the hand. He looked at the drop past the edge of the cradle where the crate had been, the white, the cloud below the edge of the city, a flyer going across it a long way down, very small.

He took his hand out and looked at it then. It looked like a hand.

The young orc came up last with the empty trolley for the hold and the loading canvas over his shoulder, a big square of it, filthy, that they'd had over the stack. In the hold he ran the trolley in against the bulkhead and set its brake with his foot. Then he took the canvas off his shoulder and shook it out once. He folded it in half, and in half again, the long way and then across, corners to corners, quick and square, and put it on top of the crate by the hatch, and pressed it flat with the side of his hand, and went past Eli and down the ramp.

"Good—" Eli said.

It stopped there. He stood with it in his mouth. The orc didn't turn round, and he didn't finish it, and down at the bottom of the ramp the orc went in with the others and they went off along the edge of the berth together, the six of them, toward the white wall.

The pilot came up the ramp with her board under her arm. She went past him into the hold, and past Teo on his crate, and counted the crates down the starboard side with her finger, and down the port side, and made a mark, and hit a plate on the bulkhead with the side of her fist. Behind Eli the ramp began to come up.

"Mind your feet," she said.

---

She flew from a chair at the front of the hold, in a box the width of a cupboard, through a hatch in the bulkhead that had a door on it, hanging open on one hinge. The other hinge was in a tin on the floor by her foot. There was glass in front of her, a long low curve of it, scratched, with a crack across one corner that somebody had filled with clear stuff and the clear stuff had gone yellow, and below the glass her board, lit, and two sticks, and a cup holder with her cup in it.

"Bench," she said. "Straps on the bench you can touch."

Eli got Teo onto the bench. Teo went where he was put. Eli got the strap across him, a canvas belt with a hook on it that went into a ring in the wall, and got his own across, and his hands were shaking and it took him three goes to find the ring. From the bench, if he leaned forward, he could see past the pilot's shoulder through the hanging door, a slice of the glass and the white beyond it.

The ship shuddered. The tick in the hull went quicker, and quicker, and stopped being a tick. Something under the floor began to roar, a long way off and then not far off at all, and the floor came up under them, and from outside, under the stern, there was a crack and a clatter, and Eli thought: the block of wood.

They lifted.

Through the door he saw the edge of the roof go by, and the light change, and then the berths fall away under them, sideways, the whole long row of them with their ships, the grey hauler and the white one, very small already, and the ribbed one at the end of her spur. The beater turned. She turned slowly, with the roar going up and down, and swung her nose round, and through the scratched glass the city came across it.

He'd seen it before. He'd had his flyers. He'd seen it from the lanes every day of his life, from the Vennicks' roof, from his own window. He'd never seen it like this, going down. The mid-levels went by under them as a plate of roofs, grey on grey, and the lanes went by in strings, and the beater climbed up through the lanes and the lanes went under, the lowest one, the next, the top one, the traffic in them going on along them small and bright and not looking up. Then there was nothing above them but the beater's own noise. Down on the first rise of land a line of white houses, very small, with all their windows turned one way.

All their windows turned to the Tower, and the Tower was in the glass.

It was there in the middle of it, pale, standing up out of the oldest ground, and the city kept back from it in a ring, and the beater climbed, and it kept going past the top of the glass. She climbed and climbed and the roofs went to a grey map and the map went soft at the edges, and the Tower didn't get any shorter. The top of the glass went on cutting it off. They came up to the cloud and the cloud came round the glass, white, then grey, then white, and the whole hull shook and something in the stern went bang and went on banging, and they were out on top.

The cloud lay out flat in every direction, bright, all the way to the sky. Out of the middle of it the Tower came up, pale, smooth, in one piece, and went on up out of the cloud and out of the top of the glass, and Eli bent forward against his strap to see more of it, and there wasn't any more. There was the top of the glass. He bent further. The strap stopped him.

"Teo," he said. "Teo. Look."

The pilot said something to her board, flat, quick, in the voice people used for numbers, and the board said something back. On the board a line came up green.

Teo had his head back against the wall and his eyes half shut. He read it off the board from the bench, past her shoulder, without lifting his head.

"Earth," Teo said.

Eli turned to him. "What?"

Teo didn't say it again. On the board the line went away and another came up under it.

Eli turned back to the glass. The beater was turning, swinging her nose away, and the Tower slid along the glass and off the edge of it, and the cloud came round, and there was only the cloud, and the sky going dark at the top of the glass, and then the sky going black.

---

Teo was sick before the hour was out.

He did it neatly, leaning out over the strap, between his own boots, and wiped his mouth on his sleeve and sat back. His face was the colour of the tape. When Eli put the back of his hand to Teo's forehead the heat came off him into it so that Eli took the hand away and looked at it.

"Let me see your hand."

"No."

"Teo. Let me see it."

Teo looked at the bulkhead opposite. He was breathing short through his mouth. Eli unhooked his own strap and got down off the bench onto his knees in front of him, and unbuttoned Teo's coat, the bottom button, the next, and Teo let him, and the arm came out from under it. Teo held it out. He held it out in front of him a little way, turned up, and looked at the bulkhead.

The dressing was his mother's knot. The linen had gone yellow right through and brown at the edges and it had stuck. Eli picked at the knot. It wouldn't go. He picked at it with his nails and it went, and he unwound it, round and round, and the last of it was stuck to the palm and he pulled that and Teo made a noise in his throat, and it came.

The smell came with it.

The cut went across the palm from the bottom of the first finger nearly to the wrist. It had closed at the ends. In the middle it hadn't, or it had and come open, and the edges of it stood up, swollen, white and wet, soft-looking, and down in the bottom of it there was yellow, and the yellow moved when Teo's fingers moved. All round it the hand was tight and shiny and red, and the red went on over the heel of the hand and up the inside of the wrist, and up from the wrist there was a line in the skin, thin and red, going up the arm under the sleeve.

Eli pushed the sleeve back. The line went up past the elbow.

He sat back on his heels.

"Right," he said. "Right." He looked round the hold. He looked at the pilot's back through the door. "I'll ring. I'll ring the account, they'll send something, they'll send something out, there'll be somewhere we can—"

"They log it," Teo said.

"I don't care if they log it."

"Nothing rings out of here," said the pilot, not turning round.

"Then where's—have you got—there must be something on board, a kit, a—"

Without turning round she reached under her chair and brought out a tin box with a cross on it and threw it back through the door over her shoulder. It hit the floor of the hold and came open and slid, and things came out of it.

"It's what there is," she said.

Eli gathered it up off the floor. A roll of tape, grey, like the tape on the seam. A tube of something with a split in the side, gone hard. A packet of pads with one pad in it. A blade in a paper sleeve. A card of pills with every pill on it punched out, all twelve, the foil standing up in little torn crowns.

He held the card up and looked at it.

There was a laugh in his chest. He could feel it sitting there. He put the card down on the bench.

"This isn't anything," he said. "This is nothing. This is a—" He got up. He went to the door with the card in his hand and stood in it, holding the frame. Past her the glass was black now, with a few hard points of light in it, and the board was lit green and amber, and her cup was in its holder, and she had one hand on a stick and the other on her knee. "He's got a line up his arm."

She didn't look round.

"Name it," Eli said. "Whatever it is. Whatever you've got. Name it."

She sat for a bit with her hand on her knee. Then she took her hand off the stick, and the ship flew on without it, and she reached inside the flight jacket and brought out a flat tin, the kind sweets came in, old, the paint gone off the lid, and held it on her knee without opening it.

"Kit's a joke," she said. "This isn't."

"Yes. Yes. What is it? I'll pay. I can pay." His hand had gone into his coat pocket already, down to the bottom, to the sock. He felt the knot, and the coins under it through the wool, shifting. Leaning in the door, the corner of the letter dug into his ribs on the other side.

The pilot turned her head and looked at him for the first time since the ramp. Then she looked down at his wrist on the doorframe.

"Not marks," she said. "That."

He looked at his wrist. The four faces. The little moon in its window, low.

"It's only a watch," he said. He said it quick.

Behind him Teo said, "Don't."

He said it once, flat, from the bench, with his hand held out turned up in front of him where Eli had left it. Eli didn't turn round. His hand came out of his pocket and let go of the sock, and the sock settled at the bottom, and he undid the strap on the watch. The buckle was stiff. Grey had got into the links. He got it off and held it out to her, and the strap hung down off his fingers, and she took it.

She didn't look at the faces. She weighed it in her hand, once, and turned it over, and looked at the back, and put it inside her flight jacket where the tin had been. She didn't put anything on her board. Then she opened the tin on her knee.

There were two kinds in it. White ones, flat and small. And three yellow ones, longer, in a twist of paper of their own. She counted white ones out into his palm, six, and then took the twist with the yellow ones out and put that in his palm too, on top.

"Two white now," she said. "Two tonight. Two in the morning. Yellow if he goes off his head."

"Off his—"

"You'll know." She shut the tin. "Clean it out first. With the blade. All the yellow, down to where it bleeds. Then tape it shut. He'll shout." She put her hand back on the stick. "It'll hold him a few days. Then he wants a proper one."

"A proper what?"

She was looking at her board.

---

He did it on the floor of the hold, on his knees, with Teo on the bench above him and Teo's arm across his own thigh and Teo's hand turned up on his knee. He'd given him the two white ones first, and Teo had taken them dry, one, two, his throat working, and then he'd put the back of his head against the wall and his good hand flat on the bench and shut his eyes.

"Ready," Eli said.

Teo didn't say anything.

Eli took the blade out of its paper. It was very small. He held Teo's wrist down with his left hand and put the point in at the end of the cut, where it was white, and pushed, and the white opened and the yellow came, a lot of it, more than there could be room for, and ran out down the side of Teo's hand onto Eli's trousers, and the smell came up off it so that his eyes watered and he turned his head and breathed out through his mouth and turned back. Teo's whole arm went hard under his hand. He didn't shout. He made a sound with his mouth shut, high up in his nose, and kept making it, and his heels drummed twice on the floor and stopped.

"Nearly," Eli said. "Nearly. Nearly."

He went along it. He went down into it with the point and scraped, and wiped the blade on his knee, and went in again. His hands had stopped shaking. He noticed that, a long way off. He scraped until it came red at the bottom instead of yellow and then went on a little past that in case, and then it was bleeding, properly, red, running into the cup of Teo's palm, and he pressed the one pad down into it hard with the heel of his hand and held it there.

Teo had stopped making the sound. He was breathing in short pulls through his nose. His eyes were shut and the tears were coming out from under the lids and down the sides of his face into his ears, and he wasn't doing anything about them.

"Done," Eli said. "That's it. That's all of it. Done."

He taped it. He went round and round the hand with the grey tape until the pad was buried and the tape went over the knuckles and round the wrist, and he bit it off with his teeth, and it looked like nothing. He laid it on Teo's chest. Teo's good hand came up off the bench and found it and held it there, the wrist, and stayed.

Eli sat back against the crate opposite. There was yellow on his trousers and on his cuffs, the second-best coat's cuffs, and on his hands, and he looked at it and wiped his hands on the coat and looked at them again.

"There," he said.

The hull ticked. In the stern the banging had gone back to a knock, every so often, irregular, as if something back there was loose and swinging. The roar under the floor had dropped to a hum. Through the door the pilot sat with one hand on her knee, and her cup in its holder, and the black in the glass.

Eli lifted his wrist to see how long it had been. There was nothing on it. There was the mark where the strap had been, a band of paler skin with a line of grey in the crease of it, and the hairs flat. He looked at it, and put the arm down.

"Teo," he said, after a while.

Teo's eyes stayed shut.

"Ithe. Have you heard of it?"

Teo lay with his head back against the wall and his good hand on the taped one, and his mouth a little open, and his chest going up and down. He might have been asleep. Eli watched him to see.

Aft, in the dark at the end of the hold, the loose thing knocked, and was quiet, and knocked.
