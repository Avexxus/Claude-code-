## EIGHT · The door

The horn on its bracket by the chain post gave a hiss when Eli leaned on it, and he had his mouth to the brass before he'd thought about it.

"Jon. It's us. We're at the pit. Jon."

The sun was up over the heaps, low and red, and it gave no warmth at all. The frost on the wire fence had a skin of light in it. Teo stood back from the post with his coat buttoned to the neck and his bad hand in the pocket. He was not looking at the horn. He was looking at the crow on the far lip of the pit, which had not gone up.

Seffa had the canvas bag on one shoulder and the big pot on its strap at her hip. The lid knocked her with every step. Doc stood at the back with his leather bag at the knee. The tall boy had his right arm held against his ribs, the brown rag wound on it and gone stiff at the crease.

The relay clicked.

"I jumped from here," said Jon. His voice came up out of the brass flat, from a long way down. "Walk in front, son. Don't stop."

"Jon, where are—"

Click. Then the hiss, long and even, like a weir.

Eli said the name into it three times. He asked if it was the ninth, and if this was the pit, and whether Jon would be on the other side or whether they were to go in on their own, and whether they could come back up the same way if it went wrong. He asked it twice. The hiss went on. Nothing came back up the brass.

Doc had gone to look at the chain. Pask's chain lay off the post in the frost, both ends bright where the links had been pulled through, and nobody had hung it back. Doc looked at the ends for a while. He said nothing. He let his bag fall from his hip and pulled it up again by the strap.

Eli went to the ladder. It hung down the lip of the pit from an iron ring, the rungs furred white, and he had his good hand on the first of them when Teo's shoulder came into him on the bad-hand side and moved him off it.

"Teo."

Teo took the first rung himself. He went down one-handed with the coat flaring out behind him, and the rung took his weight, and he did not look up.

"I was going," said Eli.

"Go after."

"I'm only saying I was about to—"

Eli was already working it out. If the rungs held for Teo they would hold for him, and if they didn't he could put his reach under the ladder and hold the whole thing up for the rest of them. He did not say so. Teo was on the third rung, and the iron said nothing about him.

The ladder went down the pit wall in a long iron zigzag. Eli held it with the good hand. The bad one would not close on the rail. It stayed partway, the fingers bent in and kept there, and he pressed it against his coat so it could not catch on anything.

At the bottom the water was grey ice to the edge. A cart wheel stood up out of it, spokes first. The back wall of the pit had been cut into an arch with no leaf to it, a black seam in the rock, and the chain-off gate stood open against the bank with its hinge rusted back on itself.

Doc went to the arch. He put his palm out flat, close to the rock but not on it.

"It's warm on this side."

"Don't," said Seffa.

He drew the hand back. He turned it over and looked at the palm, and then the back of it. "Hm."

Eli came up to the threshold beside him, and the frost stopped on his face.

It did not melt. It was simply not there. He could feel the cold on his left side, where the lane was, and on his right the air was close and warm and smelled of wet stone and lye, with something sweet under the lye. He stood on the step with one foot in each. The cold stayed on the left. The warm stayed on the right. Neither of them moved over.

He turned round to the rim. The rim was still there: the top of the ladder, the rope end, the grey sky with the red in it. The horn was up there on its bracket. Its hiss had stopped.

"He'll ring," Eli said.

He said it once, to keep himself walking, and nobody answered.

Teo went through first, with his good arm out in front of him. Seffa came after with the pot knocking her hip and her face shut. The tall boy went next, holding his arm, and Doc came behind him with his eyes on the back of the boy's neck. Eli was last. The rock edges of the seam were drawing in on either side, slow, like a mouth closing. He stepped in because it was closing, and because there was nobody left to tell him not to.

---

Teo counted the room before anyone could tell him what it was.

One table. One ledger open on it, the pages gone the colour of tea. One pencil tied to the table leg by a string, and the string was short, so the pencil hung in the air over the ledger and could not reach the page. One arch on the far side. One lintel over the arch. One groove cut across the floor in front of the arch, a finger deep and as wide as a hand. No chair anywhere.

The light came from low slots in the wall, a warm colour with no flame behind it that Teo could find. He could see the slots. He could not see what lit them.

The lintel was cut in square capitals. Teo read it before Eli did, and Eli read it aloud, which was what Eli did.

"STAND. THE COUNT IS READ STANDING." Eli laughed. "Well. We can do that. We're very good at standing."

Under it, smaller, cut in the same square hand: CROSS ONLY WHEN READ.

Nobody laughed with him. He stopped on his own.

The wall beside the table was a wall of names. They were cut in columns, a tally down the margin of each and a name against every mark, and the names had been cut by several hands. Some were neat. Some were scratched in with the point of something that had slipped. Some had a line drawn through them in a different stroke. At the foot of one column there was a space with nothing in it, smooth stone, the width of two names.

Doc walked the length of the wall with his bag at his knee. He put his hand out to the names as he passed them and never touched one.

"Who's the blank?" said Seffa.

"Nobody's," said Doc, and went on.

Eli had the pencil. He had found the string and was trying to draw the pencil up to the page with two fingers, and the string pulled at the table leg and would give him no more length. "I'll put the account in," he said. "One line. So we're on it. So it's writable."

"No," said Teo.

"I only want to see that it's writable."

"It's tied short for a reason."

"It's tied short because it's a table," said Eli. He let go, and the pencil swung back on its string and tapped the page, once, and the sound was very small.

The tall boy had not come further than the table. He had his bad arm against his chest and the good one out straight, and he was looking at the groove. He had the look he'd had at the pallet, that Teo had seen from the crate: bored, stiff, with an edge under it he did not bother to hide.

"Rule's for slow ones," he said.

"Stand where I can see you," said Seffa.

"Yeah."

"Stand, love. I mean it."

"You said." He did not look at her. He looked at the arch. "You said that before."

He stepped to the groove. Teo saw the first foot go over the edge of it and saw the second foot come up, and the boy was past the line and on the other side with one arm out, quick as he had been at the bar.

Nothing had read him.

The stone did not come up off the floor. It made no noise but a click, small and dry, like a tally mark pressed into wood. The top joint of the boy's left little finger came away clean and dropped into the groove and lay there, pale and shut, for the length of a breath. Then it was not in the groove. Teo did not see where it went.

The boy held the hand in against his chest. He made no sound. He looked down at it. "I was quick," he said.

Doc came across the room and stood over him. He did not touch the hand. He bent and looked at the stump the way he had looked at the arm on the rail, and straightened, and put his own hands in his coat pockets.

"I've no bottle left," he said. "Keep it clean and keep it still."

"It's clean."

"It's bleeding clean. Keep it so."

Teo read the lintel again under his breath, the way he'd read a bill. He read the small line twice.

"We'll be fine," said Eli. "We just stand. It says stand."

"It says read. Under."

"So we stand and it reads us. That's all it is. We stand, and it reads—"

"It reads the ones that cross," said Teo. "It says cross when read."

Eli opened his mouth and found nothing to put in it. His good hand went to the back of his neck.

Seffa had not let go of the boy. She had him by the good arm above the elbow. She did not pull him out of the groove or push him in. She held him on the near side of the line with his bleeding hand against his chest and his face gone the colour of the stone. "You'll stand," she said. "Where I can see you. Till it lets us."

"You said."

"I'm saying again."

She held him a long time. Teo counted it. Eli talked in a low voice about the lintel and what it might have meant if they'd been slower on the stair. Doc took his coat off and folded it under the table leg. Nobody sat on it.

Then Teo felt the room read them, a small hitch in the air, like a pen lifting off a page. The arch was open. Beyond it a stair went down, lit by the same warm slots. The table stayed where it was, and the ledger, and the pencil on its short string. They went through, and the arch closed behind them on the table and the open page and the pencil, and Teo heard it shut.

---

The corridor below was cut from the same stone as the stair, and Eli could not stop looking at the joints.

"Look at that," he said. "Teo. There isn't a hair between them. Somebody cared about this. You don't get that anywhere now, not since the cranes. They just pour it and let it set."

Teo was counting. His lips were moving. "Nine. Ten."

The pillar was at ten strides, a square column cut with tallies from its base to the height of a man's head. There were more above that, scratched in later, in a hand Eli did not like. The corridor ran on under a low roof, and at the far end, a long way off, there was a gate of iron with a light behind its bars.

They walked. Eli talked. He said the light was the big lamp, the one you'd see from the top of the stair if anybody had any sense, and that they'd be through the gate and up the other side before the bell.

"Eleven," said Teo.

Eli looked up. The pillar was in front of them.

It was the same pillar, the same tallies. Eli knew it by a scratch on the left side that had been cut the wrong depth. A thin line of dust lay on the floor between his boots and the base of the column. They had made that line walking. They had not moved.

"We walked," said Eli.

"We walked eleven," said Teo. "It's back at ten."

"Walk again."

Teo did not walk. Seffa did. She went past Eli with the pot knocking and ran three strides, hard and short, the way she'd run for the boys' pot at the trough. Then she stopped with her feet apart. The pillar was behind her. Then it was nine strides behind. Then it was twelve.

The corridor had taken three back. Eli watched the dust go in lines under her boots.

"Don't run," said Doc.

The tall boy had stopped dead in the middle of the floor. He stood with the stump against his chest, and nothing happened, and nothing went on not happening, and the corridor stood with him, plain and silent.

"Don't stop," Doc said. "You'll read yourself."

Nobody answered that.

Teo had his eyes on the floor. "It takes back what it sees," he said.

"What?"

"Every step we look at. It takes one back." He was not looking at the gate. He was looking at the dust under Seffa's boots, and the line of it, and the pillar standing in its own line a long way behind them. "Look at the floor."

So they went the last forty strides with their faces down. Teo called the dust. Eli watched his boots and the boots in front and the grey lines in the floor, and did not look up at the light, and the gate came nearer one step at a time without going back. He had not known until then how much of his walking was looking. He did not like what it cost to stop.

The gate was as tall as two men and its bars were as thick as a wrist. The plate was set into the iron at the height of a man's chest. The letters were square and deep.

GIVEN, NOT TAKEN.

Doc tried the hinge with the point of his knife. The point went in and came out again, and the hinge did not move. Seffa got her fingers round two bars and pulled, her whole back in it. The gate rang once and held. The tall boy had nothing to lever with. He stood at the bars with his arm against his chest and looked through at the light.

"It says given," said Eli. "Nobody's given it anything."

He opened his good hand. He opened the bad one, and it would not open all the way. The fingers came out from the palm a little and stopped.

"I can give it."

"Don't," said Teo.

"It's a lock, Teo."

"It says given. It doesn't say you."

"I'll open it. You'll see. I said I would, and you'll see it done." He was smiling. He could feel the smile go up the side of his face. He put the bad hand flat on the plate.

He let it out. It was the thing he had always had, the thing that had made the cranes' men look up, and that had put the moths in his palm. He pushed it into the iron as a gift, wide open, the way he had always given things.

The first push went wrong. The plate hummed under his hand like a struck bell. The bolt jumped in its housing, and the reach ran through the tumblers all at once, too fast to steer, and the heat came back up his arm and into the grip. The fingers went white. He felt the grip go somewhere under the skin, a small sharp give, like a wire.

He tried again, harder. It overshot the same way. The bolt rattled, the plate went hot, and the tumblers ran past him one after another in the dark and he could not hold any of them.

On the third he went slowly. He felt for the one catch the lock let him feel, a cold one, low down, and he gave the reach to that and to nothing else. It went in without a sound. The bolt went back.

The gate came open a hand's width, and then it swung, slow, on its hinge, with the light coming through the gap.

"There," said Eli. "There. I said."

The hand shut on the plate. It did not shut the way a hand shuts. It locked, knuckle to knuckle, the fingers drawn in to the palm and the palm gone white. When he pulled, the wrist moved and nothing else did.

Something warm ran over his lip. He tasted it before he saw it, and then it was on his chin, and a drop fell from it and spattered on the plate. He sat down on the floor on the wrong side of the pillar and was sick between his knees.

Doc crouched by him. He looked at the hand. He looked at the plate, where the drop had gone dark in the iron. "Hm." He held out his own hand, palm up, and waited. "Let's see."

Eli held out his good hand. Doc did not take it. He looked at it a while, and then he took his own hand back and put it in his coat.

"Not you," he said. "You've bled on the lock."

"I'm fine. It's only—"

"Leave it shut. It'll open or it won't."

Eli laughed, and there was blood in it. "It'll open. I said it would."

Teo took him by the shoulder and lifted. "Stand up."

"I'm up. I'm fine. I'll keep it ready, Teo, for the next lock. My reach. If there's a next one, I'll have—"

"I'll keep the count," said Teo.

They went through the gate. The light was on the far side and the corridor ran on from it.

Behind them the gate swung shut on its own, and the bolt went home. Far back down the corridor a tally was cut into the last pillar, once, in the square hand. Eli did not look back to see it cut.

---

The landing below the gate was a square of stone with a lamp on a hook over it. The stair came down into it from above and went on from the far side. There were two ways. The lower gallery ran off to the left into the dark, and somewhere down it a great many voices were reading, one after another, a little late. The upper rail went off to the right: a catwalk on iron brackets along a row of cells, and every cell had a grille set into its front and no door.

Seffa put the pot down on the landing. Then she picked it up again, because she had not decided to put it down.

"Someone's reading," said Eli. He was already at the lower mouth with his face turned toward the voices. "They'll know the way out. They'll know where the door is. Listen to them. They'll be on their way up, I should think, or—"

"That's a count," said Doc. "Don't stand under it."

"We don't have to stand. We can go past—"

Seffa went to the upper rail. The cells were quiet. She could see that from the landing: no sound, no shadow moving over the bars. The tall boy went up after her with his bad arm in against his chest, slow on the brackets, and at the top he stopped and looked back the way they'd come.

"Up," said Seffa.

"Upper's the way I came in," the boy said.

It was not. He had come down the stair from the gate with the rest of them. Seffa let it go. He was white round the mouth, and he had his stump against him, and he was not going to be told twice.

"He shouldn't go alone," said Teo.

"He shouldn't go near me," Eli said. "Not with a count on. You'll be read for standing next to me." He was not looking at Teo. "Go up with Seffa. Go on. It's fine."

Teo looked at the lower mouth for a long time. Then he went down toward it with Eli, and said nothing.

Doc went after them. "I'll go with the one who's bleeding," he said, and his bag went down the step ahead of him.

So Seffa had the boy, and the rail, and the pot.

The rail went up the wall to a higher level by a ladder of iron rungs, and she could not climb it with the pot in her arms, and she could not leave the boy at the foot of it with his bad arm and his bad balance. She stood at the bottom with the pot against her hip. The lid knocked on the first rung. Knocked again.

She put the pot down on the flags under the landing lamp, the big pot with the lid tied to its strap, and she stood up without looking at it. The boy was on the second rung. It was the last thing from the yard. She left it, and she climbed.

On the ladder the stump reopened. She heard it before she saw it, a small tear as he flexed, and then the wet came through the rag and ran down the back of his wrist and dripped off the iron. She got her arm under his elbow and held him to the rung.

She did not ask him. She unwound the brown rag from his forearm. It had dried stiff at the crease, and the crease had gone dark. She looked at it, turning it toward the lamp below. Then she took off her own sleeve, the good wool one, and folded it round the stump and tied it off with her teeth. She put the rag back over her sleeve and knotted it where it had been.

"It's dirt," she said. "Not a thread."

Nobody asked her to say it twice.

From below, Eli's voice came up the stair, bright and loose. "We'll be just behind, love. You'll hear us."

Seffa said nothing. Teo was at the foot of the stair. His eyes came up, and went to her hands, and stayed there.

She counted on the upper rail, the boy's steps and then her own, to keep the number right. He stopped at every cell. He did not look in. At the fourth grille he put his good hand flat on the iron, and took it away again.

"You'll stay where I can see you," she said. "Nobody goes off this rail without me."

"You said that before."

"I'm saying it now."

The rail was dry. The cells were empty, every one, the sacks rolled in them and nothing on the sacks. The boy stopped shaking. He sat down on the walkway with his back to the wall and his stump on his knee and breathed through his nose. Seffa looked at the empty cells, and the dry, and she almost sat down too. Her knees had bent before she'd decided anything. She straightened.

At the ninth cell something moved at the back. A hand came through the bars at the height of her face, thin, the fingers open, the palm turned up. It stayed there a moment. Then it went back in.

"Don't," said the boy, from the walkway.

She did not stop walking.

The rail ended at a larger cell, the last on the level, and its grille was wider than the others. Through it there was a sack on the floor, and an old man on the sack with his knees drawn up under his chin. He was coughing. It went on and on, a wet dragging cough that did not stop when it should have stopped, and his whole chest went with it. His mouth was open. The gum behind his lips was pale and shining, with nothing in it.

A woman knelt beside him with her palm flat on his chest. She was thin to the bone. Her hair was tied back with a strip of something. She was not coughing. Her eyes were shut, and then they opened, and they were silver in the dark, like the back of a spoon.

The dark elf looked up at Seffa through the bars.

"Don't put a hand on the grille," she said. She said it flat, and not loud.

Seffa's hand was already on the grille. She had put it there before the woman spoke, the way her hand went to a pot handle. It came off slow. There was a line of grey across her palm where the iron had been.

---

Teo stood at the lower grille. The grille was set at the height of his chest, and through it was a cell as wide as the count-house and longer. The far wall was the wall of names. Thirty columns, with a blank at the foot of one. Above him, on the upper rail, Seffa was standing with the boy. He could see her shoulder. He could not see her face.

The old man sat on a sack in the middle of the floor. The dark elf was kneeling beside him. She did not look round when Teo came to the bars. She was counting.

"Five," she said. She said it to the old man's chest, between breaths. "You had a pot."

Nobody answered her.

"We're—" said Eli.

"Don't."

She gave no name and asked for none. She did not stop counting. Doc had come close to the bars, with his hand already through them and flat, reaching for the old man's chest.

"Stand back from the grille."

Doc did not move. She looked at his hand.

"Sitter," she said.

Doc's hand went back through the bars and into his coat. He did not say anything. He went and stood against the wall.

The old man coughed. It came up from low in him and went on, and his knees pulled higher, and he could not get his breath in front of it. She put her palm flat on his chest over the shirt. She shut her eyes. She held her own breath, and what came up out of his came into her. Teo saw her cheeks go in. He saw her throat move once and her mouth shut on it. Then she turned her face into her sleeve and coughed, hard, one time.

The cuff came away flecked. Red spots on the grey wool. She held the sleeve down against her arm and did not look at it.

Teo could see the knife in her left hand. The blade was short and dark. Her right hand she kept close to her side, the fingers curled in against the palm.

"Where's it run," said Teo. "The count."

"Ask when it's reading."

"I don't—" said Eli. "Where's the—"

"Eli."

She got up. She stood with her back to the wall beside the grille and faced the bars. She put out the knuckle of her left forefinger and tapped the wall, once, and then again. Teo tried to count them and lost the number and started over. She did not look at the wall while she did it. She knew the names. She tapped down the first column and into the second, one knock for each, and at the top of the first she went past without touching it.

The first cut had a line through it, in the oldest hand on the wall. She left it alone.

Beside it, in the same column's margin, there was a second name, cut neat in another hand, squeezed in at the side with its own small tally. SIRRIN.

"Not one of the thirty," she said. "Nobody counts him. He's on the wall because someone cut him."

Eli had hold of the bars. The bad hand was on them with the fingers stuck, and the good hand was over it. "Right," he said. "Right. Let's get them open, then, and we'll—"

The lintel over the grille was cut in the same square hand. She read it out without turning round. "OUT WHEN EVERY NAME IS READ."

Then, flat: "The grille reads a gift as given. You haven't got one. You're the only key on this count, and it's the wrong key."

"Let me try," said Eli.

"Then try."

He put the bad hand on the lintel stone and pushed. Teo saw the pale bloom come up in the veins of the wrist. The iron took the grip and gave nothing back. The lintel did not warm. The bad hand shook on the stone, the fingers pulling at nothing. A thin line of red came out of Eli's nose and ran to his lip, and he did not wipe it.

"Try again," she said.

He tried again. It went the same way. The bars did not give, and the lintel stayed as cold as the wall. He stopped. He stood with his forehead against the iron, breathing through his mouth. She did not look at the hand. She looked at the bars in front of her face, and she tapped the knuckle of her left forefinger once against one of them.

"Jon would have said—" Eli began.

"Jon isn't here," she said. "Five came in. Six go out. One stays."

"Nobody stays," said Seffa, from above.

The old man said nothing. He had his palm flat on his own chest where hers had been, and he kept it there.

"We'll carry him out," said Eli. He had turned from the bars, and his face was loose and bright and wet at the nose. "We'll carry him through. All of us. You don't have to—we'll carry him through."

"Who carries him," she said.

"I will."

"Then do it."

She did not look at him when she said it. She did not believe him, and she showed it by not answering.

Somewhere over their heads a bell went, once, in the lintel: a flat iron note that came down through the stone and into Teo's teeth. Everybody stood, because the lintel said to. Eli got to his feet. Doc stood up from the wall. Above them Seffa pulled the boy up by his good arm.

The dark elf stood with her back to the wall and faced the grille. She did not move anything but her eyes.

Teo stood with them. He thought about the count, and where it ran, and what it had said about the blank, which was nothing. He watched her hands. The knife was in the left. The right was held close, and he could not see enough of it to say why.

---

Her breath stopped on the bell.

She had her back to the wall, and the knife in her left hand, and the right hand tucked in against her side where it was safe from the grille. The old man was humming. He always hummed the lintel rule when the cough let him, low, the tune of it without the words. It got as far as STAND and the cough took it, and he bent over his knees with the sound going out of him, and she waited for the next line.

The counter came through the back wall at the ledger.

She had watched it come for thirty years, and she still did not watch it steadily. The wall did not part. The cut names went into the rock, column by column, and the frame came out of the place where the names had been: a shape like a standing desk that someone had folded wrong. It had too many joints. The arms were folded at the wrong elbows, and one of them doubled back on itself so the hand sat at the shoulder. The face was set sideways in the chest, at grille height. The skin was tally paper, pressed flat, ruled in pencil-grey, and the lines were the count.

It was taller in the cell than the cell's door would have let it be, if there had been a door.

The count began.

It came out of the chest in a voice made of many voices, each one a little late, the way a line of men calls a number down a shed. Names came first, and then the numbers. Her own chest moved with them. She felt each number go up in the bodies at the grille, and nobody had told them to stop breathing, and they stopped. The boy stopped. Seffa, above, went quiet. The sitter's hand stopped where it was against the wall.

It read the living first. It read Teo's name back to him off the lintel, the whole of it, in the flat of its many voices, and then it read his right hand. She saw the finger move at the grille. The first finger of the right hand lifted off the bar and set back down. Teo had not meant it.

Then it read the old man's teeth.

She had known it would. The old man's mouth drew in. Four teeth came out of the gum at once, and they were on its cord before he could shut his jaw. He did not cry out. He started, "Twenty—" and the cough took the rest, and he went over his knees with it, and she put her palm flat on his chest and took the catch up into her own chest. One breath's catch. She held it. Her teeth closed on it and she did not let it past her throat. She turned her face into her sleeve and coughed, once, and the red came through the grey.

The count went on.

It read on down the column, and past the last names that were cut, to the blank at the foot of it. The last name was not given. The counter stopped. Its face turned round in its chest to look at the blank, and it did not read.

The count could not finish without the name. The rule said it had to finish what it had begun. She knew the rule the way she knew the stone under her boots. The old man was the blank. He had given the count nothing in all the years she had known him, and he was not going to give it now, with his teeth on its cord and his mouth full of blood.

"Where does it run," said Teo, through the bars. It was not a question. She looked at his hand. The first finger was on the bar, and the groove in front of the grille was under his boot.

"The groove under your feet," she said. "Cross it before it reads the last. Cross it and it starts over from the top, with the one who crossed first."

Teo looked down at the groove. He looked at the blank, and at the face turned sideways in the chest. Then he put his right foot over the line, and reached through the bars with his right hand, over the groove, into the cell.

The counter did not hurry.

It took the top joint of Teo's right index. The joint went up onto the cord across its chest, one bead more, and the count stopped, and began again from the top. It read Teo's name first. It read it through, the whole length of it, and then the boy's name, and Eli's, and Doc's, and Seffa's. It read the old man again. It went back down the column to the blank, and the blank was still a blank, and the counter went into the wall with one more bead on its cord. It had not finished. It would finish at first light.

The grille opened a hand's width. The lintel went still, and then the cut letters went dark and light again, and it read Teo as given. The cell had opened to the one it had taken from.

She did not move to it.

Teo was on his knees at the bars with his hand held out through the gap. The finger was gone. Blood ran down the back of the hand and off the wrist and off the edge of his sleeve. Eli said, "Teo." Teo said, "Right."

Doc came and knelt and took the hand in both of his. He said nothing. Ysvet could see the cold go into the boy's skin off the sitter's fingers.

She held up her right hand.

It was the first time she had shown it to anyone. One finger short at the top joint. The stump had healed flat and pale. "The corridor's line took it," she said. "Not my own lintel. I crossed that line unread once, years back, to reach the lintel. I never crossed my own."

She said it once. She did not look at them while she said it. She looked at the boy's hand in the sitter's hands.

"How many more," said Teo.

"One less than it wants," she said. "It wants his."

She tapped the grille bar once with her knuckle.

Eli laughed. It came out high and wrong. "Well. That's it, isn't it. It's gone back. It's gone back in the wall, and it's— We're—" He looked at Teo's hand, and stopped. "Right."

Nobody agreed with him.

The old man's breath was ragged. It came in through the gap in his teeth with a catch at the top of every pull. The grille was open a hand's width. The party stood on the far side of it, at the groove, with their hands and their blood, and she stayed where she was with her back to the wall and the knife in her left hand. She did not go to the gap.

---

"I go where I like," she said. "Nobody hands me back. Nobody puts a door on me."

It was the first thing she had said since the bell that was not a count. The old man had heard it. Eli was already nodding.

"Yes. Yes. Of course."

"Don't say of course. Say it."

"Yes," said Eli.

Teo was on his feet at the bars, with the bound hand against his chest. "Yes," he said.

"Two," said Ysvet.

She went to the back wall of the cell. The rock was cold through her shirt. She found the second cut, neat, in another hand. SIRRIN. She put the point of the knife into the stone at the edge of the letters, and worked, and a flake came away, the size of a thumbnail, with the name on it. It was thin and grey and broke a little at one corner. She closed her left fist on it. She did not look at the old man while she did it, and she did not say anything about it, and nobody asked.

Eli and Doc lifted him off the sack between them. He weighed almost nothing. Ysvet knew it from the way their arms took him, and from the way Eli's face changed.

"Don't carry me," the old man said, into Doc's coat. "You'll read me again."

"We'll carry you out," said Eli.

"Who."

Nobody answered him. Eli had his mouth open to say it again, and did not.

The lintel read Teo's given hand. The grille opened the width of a man's shoulders, and they went through it. They walked back down the gallery and up the stair. The count-house was empty, the arch open, the table with its ledger and its short pencil standing where they had left them. The corridor did not take a step back for any of them. She did not know why. She had not known why the first time either, when it had taken her out and let her go down again.

The tall boy stood on the stair where Seffa could see him, and did not move until she moved.

At the seam the cold came through the arch from the pit side. It was hard and real, and it came in on her face like a slap of water. Behind them the warm lay on the stone, and the old man's breath was on the stone too. She could hear it. A catch, and a drag, and a catch.

Eli stopped at the threshold. He had the old man's weight across his good arm and the bad arm hanging. He hitched the old man up higher and put his locked hand flat on the arch.

"As a gift," he said. "It's a gift. Look. It's open, it's—"

He pushed.

The reach went into the stone. It did not go through as a grip. It went wide, the same as it had at the moths, and the arch went flat under it. The rock slackened out toward the edges of the seam, and then it came back up. Nothing gave. The bad hand locked on the stone and Eli's face went white, and he did not stop pushing.

"It reads only what is already read," said Ysvet. "He's read."

"He's not—"

"He's read."

The tally on the arch ticked once. It was a small dry sound. The old man's weight went to stone. Eli felt it go. His good arm went out from under it, and the old man sat down hard on the threshold on the warm side, with his heels in the dirt and his back to the arch. The seam did not close on him. It did not need to. It would not have him, and he was not going to get up and walk through it.

They put him down where the weight had stopped.

The cough started. Eli's arms gave, and he sat down on the stone beside the old man and held his own wrists in his lap. "It's— we can—" he said, and did not say what. "We can," he said again. Nobody told him no.

The old man looked up. It was the first time he had looked at her with his eyes and not his palm.

"Go," he said.

"No."

"Go. Or you're counted with me."

"I'm the one that stays," he said. "I'm counted already."

She did not answer him. She went down on her knees on the stone. She put her palm flat on his chest, over the shirt, the way she had every night for six years, and held her breath, and waited for the catch. It came up through her palm with the weight of a fist. It came into her chest, and her lungs closed on it, and she held it there. She turned her head and coughed into her sleeve, and blood came through the grey wool in small dark spots. She held the rest of the cough until she was sure it was hers. It was. She had known it would be. The breath under her hand went on, lighter, and it was his.

"Don't count me," he said.

She took her palm off him. She stood up. She did not look at him. She stood with the cold on her left and the warm on her right, and the flake of stone in her fist, and she stepped over the threshold.

The cold changed sides on the step. It came round on her left, the hard side, and the warm stayed on her right, where the cough was.

She was the last through the seam. She did not count it. She did not look back at the arch.

They came out at the foot of the pit, under the back wall, with the frost on the ruts and the ladder going up the grey rock. The light over the heaps was too big for her. It came down from the top and went on and on, and she had nothing to put it against. She did not think about the place it belonged to.

The horn on its bracket gave a hiss as they came up over the lip.

"That's you, then," said Jon. That was all.

"You're not handed back," said Eli. He said it kindly, to her, the way he said everything. Nobody answered him.

Ysvet turned her back to the horn.

The lane ran down off the pit toward the bridge over the cut on Ithe. The frost was on the ruts in the red light. Across the water there was a cart with a lantern on it, and men standing beside it who had been told to wait and had not been told for what.

The tall boy stopped at the rail before the bridge. He put his good hand on it. Seffa stopped with him. The others went on a step and stopped too.

"This is where you stay," said Seffa.

"Where you can see me."

Seffa looked at him for a long time. She did not put her hand on him. He did not lift his. He stood at the rail with the stump against his chest and let her look. Then the cart on the far bank began to move, and Seffa turned and came on across the bridge with her bag on her shoulder, and the tall boy stayed at the rail, and did not look round either.

Ysvet walked after the others with the flake of stone in her fist.

Behind her, at the foot of the pit, the seam had gone back to being rock. The light was coming up over the heaps. The count would be read through to the end behind that rock, a little late, and then the door would be done. She could not hear the count from here. She could hear the cough. It went on.

It went on.
