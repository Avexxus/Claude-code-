## EIGHT · The door

The horn on its bracket by the chain post gave a hiss when Eli leaned on it, and he had his mouth to the brass before he'd thought about it.

"Jon. It's us. We're at the pit. Jon."

Somebody had run a line down from the row man's hut to this post, years back, for the chain-man. Green had got into the brass lip. They had come down the lane past Pask's gate in the grey, Teo at the front and Seffa with the pot knocking her hip and Doc with his bag at the back of his knee, and Eli talking the whole way. The tall boy had come down the lane after them from the yard gate, a long way behind, and he had not been sent home.

Low over the heaps the sun was up, red, and it gave no warmth at all. The frost on the wire fence had a skin of light in it. Back from the post, Teo stood with his coat buttoned to the neck and his bad hand in the pocket. He was not looking at the horn. He was looking at the crow on the far lip of the pit, which had not gone up.

Seffa had the canvas bag on one shoulder and the big pot on its strap at her hip. Doc stood at the back. The tall boy had his right arm held against his ribs, the brown rag wound on the forearm and gone stiff at the crease.

It was the ninth. Teo had read it off the row man's book that morning, with his finger on the line, and Eli had said good without hearing what he was saying good to.

On the brass, the relay clicked.

"I jumped from here," said Jon. His voice came up out of the brass flat, from a long way down. "Walk in front, son. Don't stop."

"Jon, where are—"

Click. The hiss came up long and even, like a weir.

Into it three times Eli said the name. He asked if this was the pit, and whether Jon would be on the other side or whether they were to go in on their own, and whether they could come back up the same way if it went wrong. He asked it twice. The hiss went on. Nothing came back up the brass.

Doc had gone to look at the chain. Off the post, the pit's chain lay in the frost, both ends bright where the links had been pulled through, and it had not been hung back. For a while Doc looked at the ends. He let his bag fall from his hip and pulled it up again by the strap.

At the ladder, Eli had his good hand on the first rung when Teo's shoulder came into him on the bad-hand side and moved him off it.

"Teo."

Taking the first rung himself, Teo went down one-handed, with the coat flaring out behind him, and the rung held, and he did not look up.

"I was going," said Eli.

"Go after."

"I'm only saying I was about to—"

If the rungs held for Teo they would hold for him. If they didn't, he could put his reach under the ladder and hold the whole thing up for the rest of them. He did not say so. Teo was on the third rung, and the iron said nothing about him.

Down the pit wall the ladder ran in a long iron zigzag. Eli held it with the good hand. The bad one would not close on the rail. It stayed partway, the fingers bent in and kept there, and he pressed it against his side so it could not catch on anything.

At the bottom, grey ice lay on the water to the edge. A cart wheel stood up out of it, spokes first. The back wall had been cut into an arch with no leaf to it, a black seam in the rock, and the chain-off gate stood open against the bank with its hinge rusted back on itself.

To the arch Doc went, and put his palm out flat, close to the rock but not on it.

"It's warm on this side."

"Don't," said Seffa.

Back came the hand. He turned it over and looked at the palm, and at the back of it. "Hm."

Beside him at the threshold, the frost stopped on Eli's face.

It did not melt. There was simply no frost there. The cold lay on his left side, where the lane was. On his right the air was close and warm and smelled of wet stone and lye, with something sweet under the lye. He stood on the step with one foot in each. The cold stayed on the left. The warm stayed on the right. Neither of them moved over.

Behind him was the rim: the top of the ladder, the rope end, the grey sky with the red in it. The horn was up on its bracket. Its hiss had stopped.

"He'll ring," Eli said.

He said it once, to keep himself walking, and no one answered.

Through first went Teo, with his good arm out in front of him. Seffa came after, the pot knocking her hip and her face shut. The tall boy went next, holding his arm, and Doc came behind him with his eyes on the back of the boy's neck. Eli was last. The rock edges of the seam were drawing in on either side, slow, like a mouth closing. He stepped in while it was closing.

---

Before anyone could tell him what it was, Teo was counting the room. Eli watched his lips go, one thing after the next.

One table. One ledger open on it, the pages gone the colour of tea. One pencil tied to the table leg by a string, and the string was short, so the pencil hung over the ledger and could not reach the page. One arch on the far side with a lintel over it. One groove cut across the floor in front of the arch, a finger deep and as wide as a hand. No chair anywhere.

Low slots in the wall gave the light, a warm colour with no flame behind it. Eli could see the slots. He could not see what lit them.

Square capitals ran across the lintel. Eli read them aloud, which was what he did.

"STAND. THE COUNT IS READ STANDING." He laughed. "Well. We can do that. We're very good at standing."

Smaller, under it, in the same square hand: CROSS ONLY WHEN READ.

No one laughed with him. He stopped on his own.

A wall of names stood beside the table. They were cut in columns, a tally down the margin of each and a name against every mark, and several hands had cut them. Some were neat. Some were scratched in with the point of something that had slipped. Some had a line drawn through them in a different stroke. Doc walked the length of it with his bag at his knee, and put his hand out to the names as he passed, and never touched one.

With the pencil in his fingers, Eli had found the string and was drawing the pencil up toward the page. The string pulled at the table leg and would give no more length. "I'll put the account in," he said. "One line. I'd like my name in it, Teo, with the rest of you. Somebody ought to write down that we came. Seffa could carry this whole Cut on her hip if she wanted. Put that in too."

"No," said Teo.

"I only want to see whether it's writable."

"It's tied short for a reason."

"It's tied short because it's a table," said Eli. He let go, and the pencil swung back on its string and tapped the page once, and the sound was very small.

Past the table the tall boy had not come further. He had his bad arm against his chest and the good one out straight, and he was looking at the groove with the look he'd had at the pallet: bored, stiff, and an edge under it he did not bother to hide.

"Rule's for slow ones," he said.

"Stand where I can see you," said Seffa.

"Yeah."

"Stand, love. I mean it."

"You said." He did not look at her. He looked at the arch. "You said that before."

Over the groove he stepped. Eli saw the first foot go over the edge and the second come up after, and the boy was past the line on the far side with one arm out, quick as he had been at the bar.

Nothing had read him.

Off the floor the stone did not come up. It made a click, small and dry. The top joint of the boy's right little finger came away clean and dropped into the groove and lay there, pale and shut, for the length of a breath. It had gone from the groove. Eli did not see where it went.

With the hand held in against his chest, the boy made no sound. He looked down at it. "I was quick," he said.

Across the room Doc came, and stood over him, and did not touch the hand. He looked at the cut as he had looked at the arm on the rail, and straightened.

"I've no bottle left," he said. "Keep it clean and keep it still."

"It's clean."

"It's bleeding clean. Keep it so."

Under his breath Teo read the small line again, as he'd read a bill. He read it twice.

"We'll be fine," said Eli. "We just stand. It says stand."

"It says read. Under."

"So we stand and it reads us. That's all it is. We stand and it—"

"It reads the ones that cross," said Teo. "It says cross when read."

His mouth opened on nothing. His good hand went to the back of his neck.

Seffa had the boy by the good arm above the elbow. She did not pull him out of the groove or push him in. She held him on the near side of the line, with the bleeding hand against his chest and his face gone the colour of the stone. "You'll stand," she said. "Where I can see you. Till it lets us."

"You said."

"I'm saying again."

For a long time she held him. Eli talked in a low voice about the lintel, and what it might have meant if they'd been slower on the stair. Doc took his coat off and folded it under the table leg. It stayed folded.

"You're a saint, Doc," Eli said. Doc did not answer that either, and Eli took the silence for modesty.

A small hitch went through the air, and the arch was open. Beyond it a stair went down, lit by the same warm slots. The table stayed where it was, and the ledger, and the pencil on its short string. They went through, and the arch closed behind them on the table and the open page, and Eli heard it shut.

---

Below, the corridor was cut from the same stone as the stair, and Eli could not stop looking at the joints.

"Look at that," he said. "Teo. There isn't a hair between them. Somebody cared about this."

"Nine. Ten," Teo was counting under his breath.

"You don't get that anywhere now, not since the cranes. They just pour it and let it set."

At ten strides there was a pillar, a square column cut with tallies from its base to the height of a man's head. There were more above, scratched in later, in a hand Eli did not like. The corridor ran on under a low roof, and at the far end, a long way off, there was a gate of iron with a light behind its bars.

They walked, and Eli talked. He said the light was the big lamp, the one you'd see from the top of the stair if anybody had any sense, and that they'd be through the gate and up the other side before the bell. He said the boy's arm would be right in a week, and they'd make him a sling out of a bit of cloth when they were through, and the boy would be grateful, you'd see.

"Eleven," said Teo.

Looking up, he found the pillar in front of them.

Same pillar, same tallies. He knew it by a scratch on the left side that had been cut the wrong depth. A thin line of dust lay on the floor between his boots and the base of the column. They had made that line walking. They had not moved.

"We walked," said Eli.

"We walked eleven," said Teo. "It's back at ten."

"Walk again."

Teo did not walk. Seffa did. She went past Eli with the pot knocking and ran three strides, hard and short, the way she'd run for the boys' pot at the trough. She stopped with her feet apart. The pillar was behind her. Nine strides behind. Twelve.

Three had been taken back. Eli watched the dust go in lines under her boots.

"She's quick, Seff," he said. "She'll have us out before the bell. You'll see."

"Don't run," said Doc.

Dead still in the middle of the floor, the tall boy had stopped. He stood with the stump against his chest, and the corridor stood with him, plain and silent.

"Don't stop," Doc said. "You'll read yourself."

On the floor, Teo's eyes were fixed. "It takes back what it sees," he said.

"What?"

"Every step we look at. It takes one back." Teo was not looking at the gate. He was looking at the dust under Seffa's boots, and the pillar standing in its own line a long way behind them. "Look at the floor."

So they went the last forty strides with their faces down. Teo called the dust. Eli watched his boots and the boots in front and the grey lines in the floor, and did not look up at the light. The gate came nearer one step at a time, and did not go back. He had not known how much of his walking was looking.

Two men tall, the gate, and its bars as thick as a wrist. The plate was set into the iron at the height of a man's chest, and the letters were square and deep.

GIVEN, NOT TAKEN.

With the point of his knife, Doc tried the hinge. The point went in and came out again, and the hinge did not move. Seffa got her fingers round two bars and pulled, her whole back in it. The gate rang once and held. The tall boy had no lever. He stood at the bars with his arm against his chest and looked through at the light.

"It says given," said Eli. "Nobody's given it anything."

Both hands went out. The good one opened. The bad one would not open; the fingers came out from the palm a little and stopped.

"I can give it."

"Don't," said Teo.

"It's a lock, Teo."

"It says given. It doesn't say you."

"I'll open it. You'll see. I said I would." Smiling, he could feel the smile go up the side of his face. He put the bad hand flat on the plate.

Out it went, the thing he had always had, the thing that had made the cranes' men look up and that had put the moths in his palm. He pushed it into the iron as he had always given things, wide and all at once, and kept hold of the edges of it.

That first push went wrong. The plate hummed under his hand. The bolt jumped in its housing, and the reach ran through the tumblers too fast to steer, and the heat came back up his arm and into the grip. The fingers went white. He felt the grip go somewhere under the skin, a small sharp give.

He tried again, harder. It overshot the same way. The bolt rattled, and the plate went hot, and the tumblers ran past him one after another in the dark, and he could not hold any of them.

He stopped holding. He let go of the edges and opened the hand fully, and gave the lock the whole of it. The reach went in without a sound. The bolt went back.

Open by a hand's width, the gate swung, slow, on its hinge, with the light coming through the gap.

"There," said Eli. "There. I said."

His hand stayed shut on the plate. It did not shut the way a hand shuts. It locked, knuckle to knuckle, the fingers drawn in to the palm and the palm gone white. When he pulled, the wrist moved and only the wrist.

Something warm ran over his lip. He tasted it before he saw it, and it was on his chin, and a drop fell from it and spattered on the plate. He sat down on the floor on the wrong side of the pillar and was sick between his knees.

Crouched by him, Doc looked at the hand, and at the plate, where the drop had gone dark in the iron. "Hm." He held out his own hand, palm up, and waited. "Let's see."

His good hand went out. Doc looked at it a while, and took his own hand back and put it in his coat.

"Not you," he said. "You've bled on the lock."

"I'm fine. It's only—"

"Leave it shut. It'll open or it won't."

A laugh came out with blood in it. "It'll open. I said it would."

By the shoulder, Teo took him and lifted. "Stand up."

"I'm up. I'm fine. I'll keep it ready, Teo, for the next lock. My reach. If there's a next one, I'll have—"

"I'll keep the count," said Teo.

They went through the gate. The light was on the far side, and the corridor ran on from it. Behind them the gate swung shut on its own and the bolt went home. From far back down the corridor came a small scrape, a blade on stone, and Eli did not turn round to see what it was cutting.

---

Under the gate was a landing, a square of stone with a lamp on a hook over it. The stair came down into it from above and went on from the far side. There were two ways. The lower gallery ran off to the left into the dark, and somewhere down it a great many voices were reading, one after another, a little late. The upper rail ran off to the right: a catwalk on iron brackets along a row of cells, and every cell had a grille set into its front and no door.

Seffa put the pot down on the flags and knelt by the boy. He had sat down on the bottom step without being asked.

Again the stump had opened on the stair. The wet had come through the rag and run down the back of his wrist. She unwound the brown rag from his forearm without asking him. The crease had dried stiff and gone dark. She turned the arm to the lamp and ran her eye up it, slow, from the wrist to the elbow, looking for something Eli could not see.

"Dirt," she said. "Not a thread."

What a thread was, he did not know. He nodded as if he did.

Her good wool sleeve came off, the one from her coat, and she folded it round the finger and tied it off with her teeth. She put the rag back on his forearm and knotted it where it had been. When she stood up she left the pot where it stood on the flags, with the lid tied to its strap, and did not look at it. Seffa was the best of all of them at this, Eli thought, at tying things up and carrying them. He said so, to the landing. Nobody looked up.

"Someone's reading," said Eli. He was already at the lower mouth with his face turned toward the voices. "They'll know the way out. Listen to them. They'll be on their way up, I should think, or—"

"That's a count," said Doc. "Don't stand under it."

"We don't have to stand. We can go past—"

Seffa had her hand on the first rung of the iron ladder up to the rail. The cells above were quiet. Eli could see that from the landing: no sound, no shadow moving over the bars. The tall boy went up after her with his stump against his chest, slow on the brackets, and at the top he stopped and looked back along the path they had come.

"Up," said Seffa.

"Upper's where I came in," the boy said.

It was not. He had come down the stair from the gate with the rest of them. Eli saw Seffa's face take it in, and let it go. The boy was white round the mouth.

"He can't do a ladder with that arm," Seffa said, from the rung. "And he's not going down that gallery after you, not with his hand open. I'll keep him where I can see him."

"Seff—"

"You'll go where the voices are, love. You always do. Go on."

Teo looked at the lower mouth a while. Then he went down toward it, and Eli went with him. Teo did not speak, and Eli did not ask him to.

After them came Doc. "I'll go with the one who's bleeding," he said, and his bag went down the step ahead of him.

Once from the lower mouth he looked back. Seffa had the boy's good arm and was steering him up the ladder, and the big pot sat on the flags under the lamp where she had left it. They went up the wall until the lamp was between them and he could not see either of them. "We'll be just behind, love," he called up. "You'll hear us."

Nothing came back from the rail.

---

Ending the lower gallery was a cell wider than the count-house. Its grille was set at the height of Teo's chest, and through it the cell went back as wide as the count-house and longer. The far wall was the wall of names: thirty columns, and a blank at the foot of one. Above, on the upper rail, a second grille gave onto the same cell, and he could see the boy's head through it, and Seffa's shoulder against the bars.

Lamps in the wall slots had dimmed while they came down the gallery and had come up again, warm. Doc said it was night. Eli did not argue.

On a sack in the middle of the floor the old man sat, with his knees drawn up under his chin. He was coughing. It went on and on, a wet dragging cough that did not stop when it should have stopped, and his whole chest went with it. His mouth was open. The gum behind his lips was pale and shining, with four teeth loose in it.

A woman knelt beside him with her palm flat on his chest. She was thin to the bone. Her hair was tied back with a strip of something. She was not coughing. Her eyes were shut, and they opened, and they were silver in the dark.

"Don't put a hand on the grille," she said, up to the rail. She said it flat, and not loud.

The iron rang over him, and he looked up. Seffa's hand had been on the upper grille, and it came off slow. There was a line of grey across her palm where the iron had been.

"Five," the woman said. She said it to the old man's chest, between breaths. "You had a pot."

That got no answer from him.

"We're—" said Eli.

"Don't."

No name came from her, and she asked for none. She did not stop counting. Doc had come close to the bars with his hand already through them and flat, reaching for the old man's chest.

"Stand back from the grille," she said.

Doc did not move. She looked at his hand.

"Sitter," she said.

Doc's hand came back through the bars and went into his pocket. He went and stood against the wall with his face shut like a door. Eli did not know what the word meant, and did not ask.

A cough came up from low in him and went on, and his knees pulled higher, and he could not get his breath in front of it. She laid her palm on his chest over the shirt. She shut her eyes. Eli watched her cheeks go in. He watched her throat move once and her mouth shut on it. She turned her face into her sleeve and coughed, hard, one time.

Flecked, the cuff came away, red spots on the grey wool. She held the sleeve down against her arm.

"She's wonderful," Eli said quietly, to Teo. "Look at her. She's just getting it up for him. Like a nurse. She's got him sorted."

No answer from Teo. He was watching her hands.

The knife was in her left hand. The blade was short and dark. Her right hand she kept close to her side, the fingers curled in against the palm.

"Where'd you get that?" said Eli.

"Paid for it," she said. That was all.

"He's been down here years, Teo," Eli said, low. "He must know every inch of the place."

"Where's it run," said Teo. "The count."

"Ask when it's reading," she said.

"I don't—" said Eli. "Where's the—"

"Eli," said Teo.

Up she got, and stood with her back to the wall beside the grille, facing the bars. She put out the knuckle of her left forefinger and tapped the wall, once, and again. Eli tried to count them and lost the number and started over. She seemed to know the names. She tapped down the first column and into the second, one knock for each, and at the top of the first she went past without touching it.

A line went through the first cut, in the oldest hand on the wall. She left it alone.

Beside it, in the margin, there was a second name, cut neat in another hand and squeezed in at the side with its own small tally. SIRRIN.

"Not one of the thirty," she said. "Nobody counts him. He's on the wall because someone cut him."

"Thirty what?" said Eli. She did not say.

He had hold of the bars. "Right," he said. "Right. Let's get them open, and we'll—"

Over the grille, the lintel was cut in the same square hand. She read it out without turning round.

"OUT WHEN EVERY NAME IS READ."

Flat, she said: "The grille takes a gift as given. Yours it'll take."

"Then it's open," said Eli.

"It doesn't open for gifts. It opens for names."

"Let me try," said Eli.

"Then try."

Onto the lintel stone went the bad hand, and pushed. Eli felt the reach go out of him into the stone. The iron took the grip and held it, and gave nothing back. The lintel did not open. The hand shook on the stone, the fingers pulling at the air, and a thin line of red came out of Eli's nose and ran to his lip, and he did not wipe it.

"Try again," she said.

He tried again. The bars did not give. He stopped, and stood with his forehead against the iron, breathing through his mouth. She did not look at the hand. She looked at the bars in front of her face, and tapped the knuckle of her left forefinger once against one of them.

"Jon would have said—" Eli began.

"Jon isn't here," she said.

From the bars he had turned, his face loose and bright and wet at the nose. "We'll carry him out," he said. "All of us. We'll carry him through. You don't have to—we'll carry him through."

"Who carries him," she said. That was not a question. He answered it anyway.

"I will."

"Then do it."

Her eyes stayed on the bars. Eli could not tell whether she believed him, and she did not say.

Somewhere over their heads a bell went, once, in the lintel: a flat iron note that came down through the stone and into Eli's teeth. Everybody stood, because the lintel said to. Eli got to his feet. Doc stood up from the wall. Above them Seffa pulled the boy up by his good arm.

Her back to the wall, the woman faced the grille. She did not move anything but her eyes. Her right hand was tucked in against her side, out of the grille's reach.

---

All the while the old man hummed. He did it when the cough let him, low, the tune of the lintel rule without the words. It got as far as STAND and the cough took it, and he bent over his knees with the sound going out of him, and the woman waited for the next line.

Out of the back wall at the ledger came the counter.

The wall did not part. The cut names went into the rock, column by column, and something came out of the place where they had been. A frame came out, the shape of a standing desk that someone had folded wrong. It had too many joints. The arms were folded at the wrong elbows, and one of them doubled back on itself so that the hand sat at the shoulder. The face was set sideways in the chest, at the height of the grille. The skin was tally paper, pressed flat and ruled in pencil-grey, and the lines were the count.

Taller in the cell than the cell's door would have let it be, if there had been a door.

Talk stopped in him. He had not known it could. He listened as he had been told to, and it was harder than the swing of the iron.

Its count began. It came out of the chest in a voice made of many voices, each one a little late, the way a line of men calls a number down a shed. Names came first, and numbers. Eli's own chest moved with them. He felt each number go up in the bodies at the grille, and none of them had been told to stop breathing, and they stopped.

Living first, it read. It read Teo's name back to him off the lintel, the whole of it, in the flat of its many voices, and it read his right hand. Teo's first finger lifted off the bar and set back down. Teo looked at it as if somebody else had done it.

Next the boy's name, and Seffa's from the upper grille, and Doc's. It read Eli's, and did not hurry over it. Eli heard his own name said in a hundred late voices and had to look at the floor.

Then the old man's teeth.

Eli had known it would. The old man's mouth drew in. Four teeth came out of the gum at once, and they were on its cord before he could shut his jaw. He started, "Twenty—" and the cough took the rest, and he went over his knees with it. The woman put her hand on his chest. Her shoulders went up and stayed up. Her mouth shut on something, and she did not make a sound. She turned her head and coughed once into her sleeve, and the red came through the grey. Eli thought, She's worn out. Anyone would be.

Down the column the count went on, past the last cut name, to the smooth stone at the foot of it, the width of a name. The counter stopped there. Its face turned round in its chest and looked at the stone, and it did not read.

"Where does it run," said Teo, through the bars. He did not make it sound like a question.

"Before the blank," she said. "Across the groove, before it reads the last one. Cross it and it starts over from the top, with the one who crossed first."

"What does it cost?"

"The nearest joint. Whoever broke it."

Over the bars Teo looked at the old man. The old man had his eyes shut and his palm on his own chest, and he was humming again, and the cough came in the gaps.

"It's taking him in pieces," Teo said.

"Teo," said Eli. "Wait. Teo, we can—"

"You can't," she said, to the bars.

With his right foot over the line, Teo reached through the bars with his right hand, over the groove, into the cell.

It took the top joint of Teo's right index. The joint went up onto the cord across its chest, one bead more, and the count stopped and began again from the top. It read Teo's name first. It read it through, the whole length of it, and then the boy's, and Eli's, and Doc's, and Seffa's. It read the old man again. It went back down the column to the stone, and the stone was still a blank, and the counter went back into the wall with one more bead on its cord. It had not finished. It would finish at first light.

Open a hand's width, the grille stood. For the one it had taken from, it opened.

"It has never taken anything from me in here," the woman said. She did not move to the gap.

On his knees at the bars, Teo had his hand held out through the gap. The finger was gone. Blood ran down the back of the hand and off the wrist and off the edge of his sleeve. Eli said, "Teo." Teo said, "Right." Eli looked for the finger in the groove and did not see it there. Only a joint, he thought. You would hardly miss a joint. He was ashamed of thinking it, and was not ashamed for long.

Kneeling, Doc tore a strip from the hem of his own shirt. He wound it round the wrist, slow, and tied it. He did not touch the stump. "Keep it clean," he said, "and keep it still," and Eli heard that he had no bottle to say it with.

Her right hand came up.

Eli had not seen her show it before. One finger short at the top joint. The stump had healed flat and pale. "The corridor's line took it," she said. "Not my own lintel. I crossed that line once, years back, to reach mine. I never crossed my own."

She said it once, and looked at the boy's hand, wrapped in a strip of Doc's shirt, and not at them.

"How many more," said Teo.

She did not answer. She tapped the grille bar once with her knuckle.

Laughter came out of him high and wrong. "Well. That's it, isn't it. It's gone back in the wall, and we're— We're—" He looked at Teo's hand, and stopped. "Right."

Nobody agreed with him.

Ragged, the old man's breath came. It came in through the gap in his teeth with a catch at the top of every pull. The grille stood open. The party stood on the far side of it with their hands and their blood, and she stayed where she was with her back to the wall and the knife in her left hand, and did not go to the gap.

---

"I go where I like," she said. "Nobody hands me back. Nobody puts a door on me."

Flat, through the gap, to Eli, she said it. Since the bell it had all been counts. This was the first thing she had said that was not one.

"Yes. Yes. Of course."

"Don't say of course. Say it."

"Yes," said Eli.

Up at the bars, Teo had the bound hand against his chest. "Yes," he said.

"Two," said the woman.

She went to the back wall of the cell. The rock was cold through her shirt. She found the neat cut at the side of the first, the one in another hand, SIRRIN, and put the point of the knife into the stone at the edge of the letters, and worked at it. A flake came away, the size of a thumbnail, with the name on it. Thin and grey, it broke a little at one corner. She closed her left fist on it and put the fist inside her coat. Eli thought she was keeping something for the road, a bit of the place to remember it by, and was glad for her, and did not ask.

Between them, Eli and Doc lifted the old man off the sack. He weighed hardly anything. Eli felt it through his good arm, and felt his own face change.

"Don't carry me," the old man said, into Doc's shoulder. "You'll read me again."

"We'll carry you out," said Eli.

"Who."

No answer came for that. Eli had his mouth open to say it again, and did not.

Wide enough for a man's shoulders, the grille opened, and they went through it. They walked back down the gallery and up the stair. The count-house was empty, the arch open, the table with its ledger and its short pencil standing where they had left them. The corridor did not take them back. Eli looked at the floor the whole way anyway.

Stood on the stair, where Seffa could see him, the tall boy did not move until she moved.

At the seam the cold came through the arch from the pit side. Hard and real, it came in on Eli's face like a slap of water. Behind them the warm lay on the stone, and the old man's breath was on the stone too. A catch, and a drag, and a catch.

First through was Teo. Eli saw the arch go soft at its edges for the length of a step, and then Teo was on the other side with his bound hand against his chest, and the arch was rock again. The seam took what the count had finished with. The count had finished with Teo. The boy went through after him, and Seffa, and Doc. Eli said Teo's name at the arch, once, the way you'd call up to somebody on a stair. Teo did not turn round.

At the threshold, with the old man's weight across his good arm and the bad arm hanging, Eli hitched him up higher and put his locked hand flat on the arch.

"As a gift," he said. "It's a gift. Look. It's open, it's—"

He pushed.

Into the stone the reach went. It did not go through as a grip. It went wide, the same as it had at the moths, and the rock slackened out toward the edges of the seam, and it came back up. Nothing gave. The hand locked on the stone and Eli's face went white, and he did not stop pushing. It had been a good reach. He had given it the whole of himself, and the arch had taken none of it, and no one had ever told him a gift could be refused like that.

"It takes a name only when it's read through," said the woman. "His never was. He's the blank."

"He's not—"

"He never gave it. He's the blank."

On the arch a tally ticked once. A small dry sound. The old man's weight went heavy, and stayed heavy. Eli felt it go. His good arm gave under it, and the old man sat down hard on the threshold on the warm side, with his heels in the dirt and his back to the arch. The seam did not close on him. It did not need to.

The cough started. Eli sat down on the stone beside him and held his own wrists in his lap. "It's— we can—" he said, and did not say what. "We can," he said again. No one told him no.

The old man looked up. The first time he had looked at her with his eyes and not his palm.

"Go," he said.

"No," she said.

"Go. Or you're counted with me."

"I'm the one that stays," he said. "I'm counted already."

She did not answer him. She went down on her knees on the stone. She put her palm flat on his chest, over the shirt, as Eli had watched her do it in the cell, and held her breath, and waited. Eli could not see the catch come up. He saw her shoulders take it. She turned her head and coughed into her sleeve, and blood came through the grey wool in small dark spots. She held the rest of the cough, and let it out, and it came out of her, and her hand stayed where it was. Eli did not know how she could tell which of it was hers. The breath under her hand went on, lighter, and it was his.

"Don't count me," he said.

Her palm came off him, and she stood up. Her eyes were on the rock. She stood with the cold on her left and the warm on her right, and the flake of stone in her fist, and she stepped over the threshold.

On the step the cold came round to her left, and the warm stayed on her right, where the cough had been.

Up he got. Doc came back to the arch and got a hand under his good arm and lifted, and the seam let him through, and he did not look back.

She was the last through. She did not look back at the arch. From the far side, with her back to it, she said to the seam, in the flat voice she used for numbers, "Seven went in. Six come out. One stays."

They came out at the foot of the pit, under the back wall, with the frost on the ruts and the ladder going up the grey rock. The light over the heaps was too big, Eli thought. It came down from the top and went on and on, and she did not turn her face up to it. He could tell that much from the set of her, and told himself he could not.

From the horn on its bracket came a hiss as they came up over the lip.

"That's you, then," said Jon. That was all.

"You're not handed back," said Eli. He said it kindly, to her, the way he said most things. She let it go past.

She turned her back to the horn.

Down off the pit the lane ran toward the bridge over the cut on Ithe. The frost was on the ruts in the red light. Across the water Oskel was on the far bank with the cart he had brought down from four, and the men who had come with him, and they had been told to wait and were waiting.

At the rail before the bridge the tall boy stopped. He had been left at the yard gate once already, with his hand up, and he had come after them anyway. He put his good hand on the rail. Seffa stopped with him. The others went on a step and stopped too.

"This is where you stay," said Seffa.

"Where you can see me," said the boy.

Seffa looked at him for a long time. She did not put her hand on him. He did not lift his. He stood at the rail with the stump against his chest and let her look. The cart on the far bank began to move, and Seffa turned and came on across the bridge. She had no pot. Eli saw her hands, empty, and her bag knocking at her hip. "You'll be right with the arm," he said to the rail. Nobody answered him, and he had not thought they would. The tall boy stayed at the rail and did not look round either. On the far bank Oskel came down to the cart with his hands in his pockets, and saw the bound hand on the rail, and said nothing about it.

After the others he walked, with his good hand in his pocket and the bad one hanging. Behind them, at the foot of the pit, the seam had gone back to rock. The light was coming up over the heaps. Somewhere past the rock the count would be read through to the end, a little late. He could not hear it from here. He thought he could hear a cough.

"We'll go back for him," Eli said. "When we've got the—"

Teo was walking. The horn on its bracket hissed behind them, and Eli waited for the hiss to stop, and it did not.
