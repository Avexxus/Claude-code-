## SEVEN · Listen

The frost was on the ruts of the Cut, and it went under their boots with a sound like paper tearing slow.

Seffa went first with the canvas bag on her shoulder. Teo came after her with his good arm across her back and the bad one held in against his chest, and Eli had the other side of him, taking the weight he was given and some he wasn't. Doc came last. His leather bag knocked at the back of his knee on every step and he never hitched it up. No one had said a word about the bag.

"They'll have the pot on," Eli said. "The boys. Won't they? Seff. They'll have something on the stove."

"They'll have cold tea."

"Well. That's something, isn't it. That's—"

"Quiet, love. He's heavy."

He was. The grey had gone out of his face in the night, and the brown had come back under it, and where the pink had run up under his jaw there was only a shine, tight, that would go.

Pask's gate was still chained, the chain furred white. Across the lane the old man's gate stood open a hand's width, the way it had been left the day they went for the barge, and he was standing in it.

He had his hands in his sleeves. Eli thought he had been there a while. He didn't come forward, and he didn't go back.

Teo stopped on his own. He took his arm off Seffa's back and stood on his own feet in the lane, and brought the bad hand out from against his chest and held it up, palm first, into the grey.

The palm was pink. It was closed at the edges. Teo shut it, and opened it, and shut it again, and the fingers did what they were told.

The old man looked at the hand. He took his time. His eyes came up off it, past Teo's shoulder, past Eli, and found Doc at the back of them with his bag. They stopped there.

Seffa looked back at Doc once, over her shoulder. The old man's eyes went where hers had gone.

Doc did not look up. He looked back at the lane.

"This is Doc," Eli said. He had turned round to them with his arm out. "I should've said about Doc. He's a healer. He sits with the—"

"Doc," said Doc. "Morning."

The old man held his tongue. His face did not move, all knuckle and neck, the grey hair cut close. His eyes were on Doc. The frost came off the wall in drips and the lane was quiet except for that.

Eli laughed. "He's all right. He's on his bag. He's been very good."

"Breathing," the old man said. He said it to Seffa.

"He's breathing," Seffa said. "He's eating."

"He's eaten everything," Eli said. "Teo. Tell him. You've been eating like a horse since the boat."

The old man's eyes went back to Teo's hand and rested on it. Teo kept his mouth shut. He put the arm down.

"Mornings," he said. "The cranes at the bell. You'll carry for Seffa. You'll sit on the crate till I say. You'll lift nothing."

"Of course. Of course, I'm not going to—"

"Nothing."

"No. All right. Yes." Eli was smiling. He could feel it going all the way up. "That's a yes. Isn't it? You'll take me on."

"Yes," said the old man. "On what I say. Not what you'd like."

"Of course. Absolutely."

"Seffa," said the old man, and she was already past him with the bag, into the yard. He turned and went back to the far shed, and left his gate where it was.

The yard was the yard. The pallet had gone from the place by the bench, and the bars were stacked against the long shed, and the water butt had a skin of ice that Seffa broke with the heel of her hand going by, without looking. The four boys were in their row under the eaves, on their heels. The tall one stood up when he saw them. The other three did not move.

"Seffa," the tall one said.

"Where's my pot?"

Doc went to the bench and sat down on his bag in the lee of the shed, with his hands on his knees, and sat there until the bell.

Eli stood in the middle of the yard with his shirt on and the cold in his teeth and talked to no one in particular. "There'll be a way to ring from here. There always is. I'll have the account rung the minute I find a line. The house account. It pays what's owed, and it pays for the rest. You'll see, Teo."

Teo looked at him, and then at the far shed, where the strapmaker's crate was, and the strap on its nail.

---

The cranes were at row four, down the Cut where the rails ran out over the heaps and the ground dropped to the old pit. Four of them, on legs as high as three houses stacked. They ran all night. There was always one going over somewhere in the dark with its hook out on the cable, and the drum grinding out a sound that carried up the Cut to the yard. The bundles of pig iron lay under them in rows, banded with wire, each the weight of a cart and a half.

"Listen to the swing," the tall boy said. "He says. You wait for the bottom of it. Then you go."

"I'm listening."

"You're not. You're looking at the iron."

Eli looked at the iron.

The old man sat on an upturned crate at the foot of four with his back to the leg and his eyes shut. He had been at it two years, the tall boy said, and the boys had learned to lift and nothing else.

Teo sat on the end of a bundle a little way off with his hands between his knees. He had the coat on, buttoned to the neck. He watched the hook.

"It's swinging," Teo said.

"I know it's swinging."

Teo kept the rest to himself.

"Count's on the ninth," the tall boy said, to no one. "Row man reads the dials, and the whole Cut stands in lines for its pay. You stand on the crate and say your number. You don't want to be asleep for it."

Eli was looking at the iron.

The hook came round out over the rows and went up, and hung, and came back. Not one of them was watching Eli. The tall boy had his eyes on the iron, and Teo had his on the hook, and the old man's were shut. Eli stood under it with his good hand on his hip and the bad one at his side, and waited, the way he'd been told, for the bottom. He could hear it coming. The drum laboured. The cable went taut and sang one thin note, and slacked, and came in, and the hook dropped, and at the very bottom, when it was nearly still—

He never waited. He hadn't meant not to. The thing in him went out all at once, wide and hard and eager, like a shout across a yard, and it found the hook, and the hook came round the wrong way and took the bundle off the ground.

It came up a foot. Two. It swung out over the boys instead of away from them, slow and heavy, the banded iron turning on its wire, and the guy-line on four went slack and snapped taut with a crack.

The tall boy got his arm under the first bar.

He never thought about it. He was quicker than Eli had ever been. He got his forearm under the iron and his shoulder to it before anyone had shouted, and the bar came down on the arm.

The sound it made was not loud. The forearm came out from under the bar with the skin gone off it in a strip from the wrist nearly to the elbow, and the boy stood there looking at it with his mouth open.

The bundle came down on the rig frame. Two bars slid off the top of the stack and went along the rows and stopped against a crane leg, ringing.

Eli's hand had shut.

He looked down at it. It was shut on air, on the place where the iron had been, and the knuckles stood up white under the skin. When he told it to open, it wouldn't.

"Don't use more than you can control," said the old man from the crate.

It was the second time he had said it. He had said it at the pallet with his back already turned, and now he said it from the crate in the same dry voice, the same words, without moving. His eyes were still shut. He did not get up.

Eli prised the fingers open with the other hand. He did it on his knees in the wet by the rig, one finger and then the next, and the last one was the hardest, as it had been the first time.

Doc came down the rail from the yard with his bag, not hurrying. He went down on his heels by the boy and took the arm in both his small hands, under the elbow, and turned it to the light, and looked at it for a long time.

"Water," Teo said, from the bundle.

"Not that," said Doc. "Clean water. I've no vinegar. I gave it to a woman." He turned the arm over. "Hold still, you."

"Doc, I never saw him," Eli said. "It looked clear. I thought it was clear."

"It was clear," said the tall boy, to the arm.

"I'm so sorry. I'm really—"

"He always says that," said the boy. Doc had the rag on it now. The boy hissed through his teeth. "My brother had a goat," Doc said, "would eat the label off a tin, and come back for the tin. Hold still. There."

The old man had opened his eyes. He was looking at Doc's hands on the arm, and then at Doc's face. Eli watched him watch.

"He sees you," Eli said. "Doc. He sees you."

Doc kept his eyes on the arm.

"That's the lot," Doc said to the arm. "Keep it dry. Not that you will."

The old man closed his eyes.

They lifted no iron for the rest of that day. They sat on the bundles with the cranes going over, and listened, and Eli listened, and was told he was not listening, and said that he was, and was told no.

---

The old man lit the lamp in the open front of the long shed at dark. It was a yellow one on a hook, the kind that ran on something bought, and it came up with a cough and a smell of hot wire. The moths came out of the dark to it from nowhere: from the heaps, from the frost, from under the eaves where the boys slept in their row. They came in ones and twos and ticked against the glass, and dropped off it, and came back.

Teo had walked up the Cut behind them at dusk. He sat on the step at the end of the shed with his back to a post and the coat round him, and his bad hand inside the coat against his ribs. He watched the moths and not the old man.

"Shut your eyes," the old man said.

"Why?"

"Find one."

"I can see one. There's about six."

"Shut them."

Eli shut them.

The shed went dark on his eyelids, and then it didn't. There was the cold on his face, and the lamp's smell, and the boys breathing in the row. Under all of it, a long way off or close to his ear, there was a small dry sound, like someone turning a page in the next room. Quiet. Then the page again.

"There," Eli said, and pointed, and opened his eyes. The lamp was in front of him with its glass going yellow and a moth on the glass, grey, its wings pressed flat. It was the wrong side of the lamp for anything.

"It's on the lamp."

"Shut them."

He shut them. He pointed. "Left."

"No."

"Right, then. By the—"

"Listen," the old man said. "Don't look. You're looking. Your eyes are in the way."

Eli stood with his eyes shut and his hand out and listened. The boys breathed. In the row one of them turned over and said something with his mouth full of sleep, and the old man said, not loud, "Still," and the boy went quiet. The lamp ticked on its hook. Somewhere in the dark the moth went round and round, not near and not far, and its sound came and went with the air moving over the frost. He pushed his thing out toward it, slowly. It went out wide. It came back empty. He tried again, smaller, and it went along the wall of the shed, past the post, over the dry dust of the boards, and found something, and closed.

It was in his hand. He could feel the shape of it against his palm, all its wings folded at once, and the fear in it, a flicker, and then less than that.

Then something else was in the reach. It was warm, and it was close, and it had not been there a moment before. Teo's hand, inside the coat. Eli felt it the way he'd felt the moth: the bones of it, and the skin gone tight over them, and the fingers curled in against the wool.

"Eli," said Teo, from the step.

"Hold on. I've nearly—"

"Eli."

He shut his hand.

The moth fell on the boards with its wings flat and lay there. It lifted one wing. It didn't lift it again.

When Eli opened the hand there was a grey dust across the palm, the kind that came off the wings. He rubbed at it with his thumb, and it went into the lines of his fingers and stayed there.

On the step the coat pulled tight across Teo's shoulder, and Teo made no sound at all. Eli opened his eyes. Teo had his good hand round the wrist of the other, under the coat, and was opening the fingers one at a time, the way Eli had on the rail. The last one was the hardest.

"I felt it go," Eli said. He was grinning. He could feel the grin pull at the swollen side of his face. "Did you see that? It went all the way along, like something running out through—"

"Your hand," said Teo. "It's still on."

Eli looked. His hand had shut round the moth and stopped partway, the fingers still bent, the knuckles pale in the yellow light. He opened it. It opened about as far as it had to.

"Oh. Sorry. Sorry, Teo, I didn't—"

"Again," said the old man.

"It's dead."

"Again."

The old man did not look at the step. Eli looked at the boards. The moth lay where it had fallen. Nobody picked it up.

Another came to the glass. Eli heard it before he had got his eyes properly shut, and then he had it, up under the cage where the light was thickest. Nobody had told him to look for it. He went for it before he was asked. This time he didn't point. He didn't ask where. His thing went straight to it, and he shut his hand on it hard, all at once, and kept it shut. He felt the wings go against his palm, twice, and then not.

He opened the hand. The old man only looked at him. Eli kept his eyes off the boards. He wiped his palm down the leg of his trousers, and the dust came off in a grey streak on the cloth, and some of it stayed in the lines.

When he looked up again the step was empty and the coat was gone from it.

In the morning there was frost on the inside of the shed walls, and Eli's hand was white and stiff and wouldn't do what it had done the night before. He tried it on the strap. Partway. He pushed, and it went no further.

Teo's hand would not open at all. He had it in the pocket of the coat, shut. At the stove he drank his tea out of the cup in the good hand, with the other one dead weight in the pocket, and the coat hung crooked on that side all morning.

"It's the cold," Eli told him.

Teo let that go past him.

At the trough that morning Seffa had her sleeves up to the elbow, wringing out the cloths from the boys' row, and Eli came and stood at her side and watched her do it.

"He's wonderful, isn't he, Seff? A proper teacher. Not like the ones at the Terrace, who taught you which fork. He said I was quick."

"He never said you were quick."

"He did. I heard him. I said barely teach me, I'm quick, and he didn't say a word against it."

Seffa twisted a cloth and the water came out brown and went down the trough. "He said listen," she said.

"Well, I am." Eli put his hand in the water and took it out again, quick, because it was cold. "Honestly. I'm listening all the time."

She put the cloth on the rail and let it go.

---

Oskel came up the Cut at noon at the same loose fast pace he'd come up it that first time. He had his shift cap pushed back and his arms going, and the chip behind his ear showed white where the hair had been cut away in its square. He came in at the gate without stopping. He never looked at the crate, or at Doc on his bag. He pulled up in front of Seffa, who was at the trough with her sleeves up, and put his hands on his knees.

"Seff. Seff. Listen."

"What's happened to you?"

"Nothing's happened. I'm on my break." He straightened and looked round the yard, pleased with it. "There's a call for you. Down on four. The row man put it through. He wrote it in his book first, who'd asked." He pointed at Eli without looking at him. "For the one in the coat."

"That's me," Eli said. He was off the crate before he'd decided to be. "That's me. I'm the one, I'm—"

Oskel looked at the shirt, and the mud still on it, and the bare wrists. "Nah," he said. "It said the coat."

Teo was in the lee of the water butt. He had the coat round him, the good cut and the yellow on the cuffs, and he hadn't moved since they came in. He moved now. He came out from the butt and stood in front of Oskel, and Oskel grinned at him, and stopped grinning when Teo didn't.

"Four," Teo said.

"Yeah. Come on, then. I'm on my break."

They went down the Cut. Oskel ran ahead and came back and ran ahead again. Eli talked.

"It'll be Jon. It has to be. He said he'd— Did you hear him say that? He said he'd—" He had to half run to keep up. "Teo. Did you tell him where we were? Did you say anything?"

Teo kept walking. The frost went off the rails in the low sun and the cranes came up over the heaps, one at a time, the first of them already moving.

The hut at the foot of four was a tin box with a slate roof and a door hanging off one hinge. Inside there was a stool, a stove gone cold, and a board on the wall with a row of brass dials and a lamp in a cage. The handset hung on a cord from a hook under the board. A book hung on a string beside it, open, with a stub of pencil tied to the string. There was no one there. The row man had put the call through and gone.

Teo took the handset off the hook. He held it out from himself a while, and then put it to his ear.

Eli got his head in next to it. The handset had a crackle in it like fat in a pan. Under the crackle something large was going on a long way off, water going over stone, steady.

"Is that the coat?" said Jon.

"Yes," said Teo.

"Thought it might be."

The water went over the stone. A man called out a number a long way off, and somebody answered him.

"That's come right, then," Jon said.

Teo didn't answer that. Then he did. "Yes."

"Good. Put the son on."

Teo held it out. Eli took it by the cord and got it wrong, and it knocked against the board, and he got his mouth to the end of it.

"Jon."

"Son," said Jon.

"Where are you? Are you on Ithe? We're at the yard. There's a teacher, Jon. He's taken me on. He said yes."

"Good."

"He said yes. Are you coming? You could come up, he'd—"

"Ya don't need me for the next bit."

"What's the next bit?"

Water went over stone. Somebody called a number, and somebody else answered.

"The door," said Jon. "By the old pit. On the ninth."

"What door? Which ninth? Jon, what— Where's the—"

"Mind yerself, son."

A click. Then a hiss, long and even, like a weir.

Eli said his name twice more into the hiss. Then he held the handset out at arm's length, and Teo took it off him by the cord and put it to his own ear, and listened.

Teo listened for a long time. Oskel, in the doorway, had stopped grinning and was looking at the floor. The hiss went on. Teo listened to the end of it, whatever the end was, until there was only the tin box and the cold stove and the lamp in its cage, ticking. He hung the handset back on its hook. The cord swung under the board and stopped.

"What did he say?" Eli said. "At the start. What did he say to you?"

"Nothing," said Teo.

"He said good. Didn't he? When I said yes."

Teo looked at the board with its dials.

"The ninth," Eli said. "Well. He'll tell us, won't he."

They walked back up the Cut. Eli talked. About the cranes, which were the cleanest thing he'd ever seen. About the tall boy's arm, which would be right in a week. About the old man, who would be pleased when he heard, you'd see, he'd be very pleased. About what they'd need for the door. The account did not come up.

The row man was gone.

---

Two days at the cranes, and it was Thursday.

The onions were on. Seffa had them cut small on the board by the stove, and the smell of them was in the long shed for the first time since the barge. There was no pie. There was no flour in the yard that anyone could find.

Eli had come to the kitchen door to ask about his supper. He stood in it with his back against the frame and watched the knife.

Oskel came in at the back of the kitchen with his spoon, and stopped beside him. He had been told to go back to his shift at two and had gone back, and had come up again at dark.

"You said Thursday."

"I said."

"You said the one with the onions."

"I've done the onions." Seffa turned them with the knife. The fat went up in a spit. "There's no flour, Oskel. Ferrel's got the flour."

Oskel laughed. It came out of him too long. It went on past anything that was funny, on and on, the spoon going up and down in his fist, and it stopped dead, and he looked at the onions in the pan.

"Are you coming back?"

Seffa scraped them out of the pan onto a cloth. She didn't answer that. She folded the cloth over the onions and tied the corners, and carried the bundle to the sill, where the cold came through the glass, and set it down there for the boys in the morning.

"You've got your shift," she said.

"I've got my shift."

"Then stop on it."

"Who's going to do the boys?"

"They'll eat the onions."

"They don't like the onions."

"They'll eat them." The scar across the back of her wrist was pink in the lamp, the shape of a stove's rim. She put the knife in the board and wiped her hands. "Go on, love. You'll be late. They'll have it off you."

He stood with the spoon. His neck had the old grease down it and his square behind the ear was red at the edges where the new shave had caught, and he put his free hand up to it, and took the hand down.

"Somebody's got to feed you," Seffa said, to the pot.

"Who?" said Oskel.

She kept her back to him. She took her bag off the hook by the door and put it over her shoulder, and went out between them into the dark of the long shed, toward the far shed, where the lamp was. Eli went after her as far as the edge of the light.

The old man was on his crate. The strap was across his knees, and his fingers went along it stitch by stitch. He didn't look up when she stood in front of him with the bag.

"I'm going with them," she said. "He had the fit on the barge, with his tongue in his teeth. Somebody's got to be on him when it comes back."

He waited for more. There wasn't any, and he let it go, and went on with the strap.

"The boys eat at dark," he said.

"They'll have the onions. I've put them on the sill."

Seffa stood a while longer. She went back out past Eli at the edge of the lamp, and past the bench and the row of boys under the eaves, and did not look at the gate.

---

Eli came in from the edge of the lamp, already talking.

"You'd come to the door. I'm not saying you have to. Seffa's going, and Teo's walking, and Doc's on his bag. I don't know what's at the door. Jon said. You'd know, wouldn't you? The whole yard would—"

"I don't leave the Cut," said the old man.

It came out flat. There was no question in it.

"Not even—"

"No."

"Not for the door. Not for the—"

"No."

Eli waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it. He laughed, and it came out wrong, and he tried another way round.

"Jon says it's on the ninth. You'd only have to stand there. You wouldn't have to do anything. I mean, you'd know what to look at."

The old man picked up the strap. His thumb found the place he'd stopped at and went on from there, one stitch and then the next.

"We'd come back," Eli said. "After. When it's done. You'd still have the place. It'd be the same."

"Then you're not taken on."

"That's not— You said yes."

"On what I say."

"Well," Eli said. "Then we'll see."

The thumb stopped on the stitch. It went on.

A moth came to the lamp and ticked against the glass. Neither of them looked at it.

---

They went out of the Cut at first light, before the horns.

The frost was thick on the ruts, and the sky over the heaps had the small red sun low in it, giving no warmth. The ruts had frozen in the shape of the barge cart's wheels, and their boots broke the skin of them one at a time.

Teo walked in front, alone, with his good hand in the pocket of the coat and the other in the pocket with it, shut. His boots were laced, both of them, to the top. Seffa had her bag over her shoulder, and the lid of the big pot was tied to its strap, so that it knocked against her hip with every step and she did not reach down to stop it. Doc had his bag, and it knocked at the back of his knee.

Eli came behind them with his shirt buttoned to the neck and his hands in his trouser pockets. He had no coat. The wind came up off the heaps and went straight through the shirt. The hand that had shut on the moths would not come out to swing. He tried it once, at the gate. It stayed where it was.

They passed the long shed first. The lamp was still on its hook in the open front, and it had gone out. Someone had swept the boards. The moth was in the sweepings by the step, with the others, one wing up out of the dust. The boys were at the trough with their backs to the yard, and none of them looked round.

At the yard gate the water butt had a new skin of ice, and a single brown leaf from the eaves was frozen into it, its edge up. Seffa went past it without touching it. Eli stopped to look at the leaf, and then he didn't.

The tall boy was at the gate with his arm wrapped in Doc's clean rag, and the rag had gone brown already at the crease of the elbow. He lifted his other hand. No one lifted anything back. The arm had gone stiff in the cold, and the boy held it against his side as if it were somebody else's to carry. Doc looked at it once, the way he had on the rail, and walked on.

In the lane the strapmaker's gate stood open a hand's width. He was in it, with his hands in his sleeves and his eyes shut, and he did not open them as they passed. Seffa stopped, once, with the bag on her shoulder, and turned her face back toward the yard. She stood long enough for the bag to slide, and hitched it up, and went on without a word.

Down past Pask's gate, with its chain furred white, the old pit lay on the left behind a wire fence. Nobody had shored up the far lip. Water stood in the bottom, flat, with a rim of grey ice, and a cart's wheel was sunk in the middle with its spokes up out of the water. Eli slowed at the fence and put one hand on the wire. A crow was walking on the far lip of the pit, and it did not go up when they came level with it.

"That's the one," he said. "Jon said by the old pit. Teo. That's it, isn't it?"

Teo didn't look. He walked on, and Eli had to come after him, and let go of the fence wire at the end of the post. He didn't talk again until the bottom of the lane.

Oskel was at the foot of four, under the nearest crane, with the shift lamp in his hand. He wasn't waving. He had the lamp held low by his leg, and his breath went up from him in pieces.

Eli looked back from the bottom of the lane. The old man was still standing in his gate, up the slope, listening to them go. Teo's boots went on the frost. Seffa's bag knocked. Doc's bag knocked at the back of his knee.

Somewhere up on four a horn went, a long one and a short. The first crane started, and its cable came in over the rows.
