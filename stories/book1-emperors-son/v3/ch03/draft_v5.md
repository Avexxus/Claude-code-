## THREE · Company

The cook came in at seven, and Eli watched her find her brush in the sink. She picked it up. The bristles were pink at the roots. She dropped it in the bin under the sink, got a new one out of the drawer and laid it on the drainer, and then she saw him at the table, and Teo on the floor with his back to the cold-box and his head on his knees and his hands still in his armpits.

She filled the kettle.

"Eggs," she said.

It wasn't a question. She did them, and put them in front of him, and he sat with them until they had gone cold and set shiny on the plate, and she took them away again and scraped them into the bin on top of the brush.

The steward came in at eight with his slate flat against his chest and stood by the table.

"He's not coming back," Eli said.

"Sir."

He stayed a little longer. Then he laid the slate down on the table by Eli's hand and turned it round to face him. It was the yard's page, the numbers in their column, and one line of it had gone amber. There was a box at the end of the line with nothing in it, and over the box it said CAUSE.

"For the yard, sir," the steward said. "They'll want it filled."

Eli looked at the box.

"I can put unknown, sir."

Eli didn't say anything. After a while the steward took the slate back.

"I'll leave it open, sir. Will there be anything?"

"No."

He went out, and Eli heard him cross the hall and go up.

Teo woke at nine, all at once, the whole of him. He looked at his hands. The cut across his palm had stuck to his shirt under the arm, and he peeled it off slowly with his teeth together, and it bled again, not much. The cook put a clean cloth by him on the floor without a word. He wound it round twice and held the end in his fist.

"There's a clinic on the Seventh," Eli said. "I'll ring the account. They'll send a flyer for you."

"They log it."

"So they log it."

"It'll close." Teo got up off the floor in stages, a hand on the cold-box, a hand on the counter, and stood. He looked at the cook's new brush on the drainer. "Right," he said, and went home.

---

Eli went up and lay on his bed in his clothes.

Behind his ear the cut had dried into his collar, and the collar pulled at it when he turned his head, so he lay on his side facing the wall. He shut his eyes. Hand up. The long thin hook off the belt, and a knee on the chest. He opened them. There was a crack in the paint he had never seen, running down from the corner.

The cook knocked and said through the door that there was water run.

He sat in the bath until it was cold. The water went grey around him, a fine grey that hung in it and didn't settle, and when he came up from under there was grit in his teeth. He scrubbed behind his ear and it opened, and a pink thread went out from his shoulder into the grey.

At the window, later, he made a fist. The hand closed partway and stopped with the fingers curled, and he stood and studied it.

He went down for water when the house was dark. The second-best coat was still on the step inside the yard door, folded in four, and there was a clean line on the tiles round it where the cook had swept.

That night the barracks sang.

He heard it from the bed with the window up. It came across the yard from under the long house, low, a lot of voices and none of them loud, in the orc talk, and it went on. He couldn't follow it. It had a tune that rose on the last word of every line and came down again in the next one. Over and over in it there was one word he knew, the name the two little ones shouted when they played in the yard, and every time it came round the voices leaned on it. Once a single voice went on alone, an old one, cracked at the top, and the others waited for it and came in again under it.

He lay and listened until it stopped.

---

Before the bell he was at the window, with the sash up and his arms on the sill and the cold on his face. The barracks door was open under the long house and the light from it lay out across the yard. Gravel lay along the path's edge, where it always did.

The bell went, and he went down.

He stood at the back of the yard by the wall in yesterday's shirt with the collar stiff, and the orcs filed out of the barracks in the grey canvas and stood in their rows with their breath going up. Some of them saw him there. They turned their faces to the front. On the step the steward had his slate up, and he didn't lift his head. He read a number, and an orc answered, and stood still a breath while the job went into his neck, and turned, and went. Eli counted along with them and lost it, and started again from the front, and lost it again. He waited for the number nobody would answer.

It didn't come. The numbers ran straight through to the end in the same voice, and the last orc went off toward the pumps, and the steward made a mark, and that was the count.

He went into the barracks. Eli gave it a minute and followed.

He had never been inside. It was low and long, built into the footings of the long house, with a floor of the same white stone as the paths gone grey from boots. The bunks were two high down both walls, iron, each with a blanket folded square at the foot, and it smelled of soap and feet and wet soil. The orcs had all gone out to their jobs. The two little ones from the end bunks hadn't. They sat on the bottom one together with their knees up and watched him come in.

The steward was at a bunk halfway down on the left, the bottom one. He had stripped it. The blanket lay folded on the bare mattress, and on the blanket he'd laid out what there was, in a line, and he was writing it on the slate.

"Comb," he said to the slate. It had three teeth gone. "Cup." Tin, with a dent in the lip. "Canvas, one set." Folded, with the knees mended. Last in the line was a twist of paper, done up tight. He picked it up and shook it by his ear, and it whispered. "Seeds," he said. "Unknown."

He put it back, and crouched, and reached under the bunk to the back, against the wall, and came out with the sock.

He weighed it in his palm.

"Marks," he said. He wrote. "Effects go up, sir."

"Give it here."

He stayed crouched, the sock in one hand and the slate in the other. "It's on the slate, sir."

"Then take it off."

"Effects aren't lates, sir." His thumb rested on the edge of the slate. "Unless there's a claim."

"A claim."

"A debt, sir. Owing to the house." The thumb didn't move. "Or to a person."

On the end bunk the little ones had stopped talking. Eli kept his eyes on the sock in the steward's hand, grey wool, darned at the heel in a different grey.

"He owed me," Eli said.

"Sir?"

"Eleven hundred."

He wrote it, slowly, the stylus going in small strokes. "Claim, eleven hundred," he said to the slate. "Against forty-one." He held the sock out. "Sir."

It was cold from the wall. The knot was pulled so tight the wool had felted round it. Eli held it by the knot.

As he went past the end bunk the bigger of the little ones said, out loud, to him, "Did they catch him?"

The smaller one hit him on the ear.

Eli stopped. "What?"

They studied their knees.

He went back up to the kitchen. He crouched by the step, and found the pocket of the folded coat without unfolding it, and pushed the sock in, down to the bottom.

---

The house bell went in the afternoon.

Eli was on the stairs. He had been going up or going down, and he stood on the turn with his hand on the rail, and the steward came through from the back and crossed the hall and took it.

"Ninth, the house."

A pause.

"Yes, madam."

A pause.

"No, madam. Nothing drawn on the account." Longer. "Not since the eleven hundred."

Eli held the rail.

"One short, madam." The steward's free hand was flat on the hall table, the fingers spread. "Since yesterday. There'll be one sent up for the count tomorrow. The fee's to the house, madam." A silence. "No, madam. No cause." A longer one, long enough for Eli to think she had gone. "Yes, madam."

The steward put it back and kept his hand on the table. When he turned and came up the stairs he stopped on the step below Eli.

"Was that the woman?" said Eli. "From the account."

"Sir."

Eli waited. The steward waited, a step down, with the slate. Eli moved over to the wall and the steward went on up past him.

---

Teo came at four with his slate and sat on the counter by the cold-box. The cloth was gone. His hand was done up now in a strip of white linen, neatly, the end tucked in under itself, his mother's knot, and he held it up against his chest as if he'd been told to. When he forgot and put it down on the counter he took it off again fast. A spot had come through the linen over the palm, the size of a thumbnail, yellow at the edge.

He didn't take an apple.

"Let me ring," Eli said.

"No."

"It never asks. It never once—"

"It'll close."

Teo had the slate on his knee and was going down it with the thumb of the good hand. Eli came and read over his arm.

It was the gate. The Ninth Terrace gate's own page, the one any house on Ninth could open: in and out by the hour, a line for everybody who went under the arch, and under it the kerb, every flyer that set down and every one that went up. Teo was running it back. The Ilders' people, in, with crates. A flyer at the kerb at two for the Ilders', down, and up again. The cook's girl with the bread, early, in, and out. At the top, the morning they'd come up, Eli's own line went past, the hour and no name, and under it GOOD MORNING, TEO.

"What are you looking for?"

"Who's come in."

"And?"

"Crates." Teo thumbed it back down to now. "Bread."

"Who'd come in?"

Teo put the slate face down on his knee. He picked at the skin along his thumb with the nail of the bad hand, and winced, and stopped.

In the hall the house bell went.

Eli was at the window. Out across the yard the steward was in the barracks door with the slate, a long way off, his back to the house, and he didn't turn round. The bell went on. Eli stayed where he was. Teo watched him from the counter. It rang a long time, and then it didn't, and the house was quiet in a different way from before.

The feed on the wall was running with the sound off. A queue somewhere, a long one, people with bags, under a sky the wrong colour.

"They've put it back," Teo said. "To the spring."

A boy from the Spur crews was stacking soil sacks by the slot, out past the white wall, one on another, squaring each one with his knee.

"The Ilders are having people tonight," Eli said. "Come."

"No."

"Ilder will be there."

Teo turned the slate back over.

"It's three doors up. You don't have to talk to anybody. You can stand by the wall and eat."

"Right," Teo said, and stayed on the counter till it got dark, and went home.

The good coat had come back from wherever the steward sent things, pressed, with a sheet of tissue over the shoulders, and it was hanging on its hook in the hall. Eli lifted the tissue. The coat was clean. At the left cuff, on the inside, where it turned back, there was a brown line the cleaning had missed, the width of a thread. He laid the tissue back over it and went to the Ilders' in his shirt.

---

The Ilders had lanterns strung over the lawn on wires, low, so that you walked about under them with light on the backs of your hands, and the lawn was full of people standing on it in good shoes with their glasses. The doors at the back of the house stood open onto it, and somebody inside was at the piano, playing something very fast and getting most of it. The Ilders' man stood at the edge of the paving with a tray, at the very edge, his boots on the stone, and when people wanted a glass they came off the grass to him and took one and went back on.

Eli came off the grass and took one.

The Ilders' man kept the tray level and his eyes on the glasses.

Dace was under the middle lantern in the plum coat with three people Eli half knew, and he saw Eli and put his arms out wide.

"He lives!"

People turned round.

Dace had him by both shoulders. He turned Eli's face to the lantern with a finger under the chin, the swelling gone dark down one side of it. "Oh, look at you. You went. Ilder! He went!"

"We went," Eli said.

"And?"

"We had soup."

Dace let go of him to tell the others. "Soup."

Through the open doors, on the wall of the Ilders' front room, a feed was running with the sound off. Grey men at a grey table again, or the same ones. The ribbon underneath said UNREST IN THE SCALD. It said TERRORIST CELL SUSPECTED. It said LEGION, and a line of figures, and went round again.

Dace read it out. "'Terrorist cell.'" He laughed into his glass. "Was it you, Eli? Was it your big one?" He turned to the people with him. "Eli's orc. He's huge. He wanted a better chip, and Eli's paid for it, and he's going to pay him back"—Dace put a hand flat on Eli's chest and held him there, for the line—"a mark a month."

A girl Eli didn't know did the sum on her fingers, and got lost, and looked up.

"Eighty-eight years," said Dace, flat and low, in the voice Eli had done on the roof.

They laughed. The girl laughed with her hand over her mouth. Dace waited for Eli to laugh, and when he didn't Dace laughed harder, at that.

Lisane wasn't there. Nobody said where the Vennicks were.

Ilder had come out of the house behind them with a glass in one hand and the other hand in his pocket. He hung back from the group, and he didn't laugh this time either. His eyes were on Eli's face, on the dark side of it.

"Did he get it?" Ilder said. "His chip."

"He got it."

"Good."

Ilder took the hand out of his pocket to change his glass over, and there was the mark on the heel of it, white in the lantern light, the ragged crescent, and he rubbed his thumb across it.

"Ilder," Eli said.

"Mm."

"When we were small." He stopped. The piano went up and came down. "Your yard."

Ilder waited, rubbing the mark.

"Do the voice," Dace said, and got hold of Eli's sleeve. "Eli. Do it for them, you do it better than me."

Eli pulled the sleeve out of his hand. Dace's elbow went, and his drink went down his own front, and somebody said oh.

Eli bent and put his glass down on the grass, carefully, upright, and walked off the lawn.

The Ilders' man stood where he had stood all night, with the tray, and his eyes were on the glass, out on the grass by itself in the middle of the lawn, three steps off the stone, with the lantern light in it.

Ilder caught him up where the hedge ended, on the white path, with his own glass still in his hand.

"My yard," Ilder said.

Eli stopped.

Ilder held up the other hand with the heel of it turned out, so the crescent caught what light came over the hedge. "You did that." He said it as he said everything, without any heat. "I'd had my father's fob out of his desk. Your one was on the grass, and I had him going a long time."

"Vosk."

"Till he wet himself. Everybody laughing. Me too. And you came off the step and got my hand in your mouth, and you wouldn't let go. My father had to put his thumb in your jaw."

"I don't remember."

"You were smaller." Ilder looked at the mark. "Every time I pick up a glass."

"He remembered," Eli said.

"Did he." Ilder rubbed it. "How is he? You came back with a face."

"He's dead."

Ilder stood there with the glass. Up on the lawn somebody shrieked and somebody else said no, no, in the voice for a good story. Ilder looked down into the glass, and drank what was in it, all of it.

"Come back in," he said. "Dace'll stop."

Eli walked on down the path. Behind him the piano started the fast piece again from the beginning.

---

In the morning the count had one more in it.

He heard it from the bed. The bell, and the steward's voice, and a number, and somebody answered, and a number, and somebody answered. Near the end there was a number, and a voice answered it that Eli didn't know, young, too loud, saying the whole of the number back, every figure of it.

"Trees," said the steward.

Eli went to the window. The new one was crossing the yard toward the slot, fast, nearly running, with his head down. He was small for an orc, narrow through the back, and at the nape of his neck there was a square of white paper stuck over the scar, and it had come up at one corner.

Later he was at Eli's front tree with the barrow. Eli stood on the path and watched him dig. The old dirt came out grey and broke when it hit the barrow. The new one felt Eli there and stopped, and stood with the spade upright in both hands, and kept his eyes on the hole.

"Go on," Eli said.

The new one went on. He dug too fast and hit a root, and stood with it, and looked round for somebody to tell him.

Eli walked on down the white path.

The Ilders' man was sweeping the paving along the Ilders' front hedge, with his boots on the stone and an inch off the grass. Last night's lanterns were down and coiled on the step. The glass was gone off the lawn. He didn't look up when Eli came along the path, and Eli would have gone by him, and the man spoke to his boots, in the common talk, low.

"He's asking."

Eli stopped.

He went on sweeping. He swept up to where the hedge met the low wall of the water channel, and he put a hand in his pocket, and took it out, and set something down on the coping of the wall, flat, and took his hand away.

"Came up this morning," he said. "From Hookline. Dwarf paid her cut in it."

"Who's asking?"

He swept round the corner of the wall and along the far side of it, away. Halfway along he said two more words, not turning round, and the broom went on, and Eli didn't catch it until the man was nearly at the Ilders' side door. It had been "Walk well."

The watch was on the coping. The strap was done up, buckled, as though somebody had taken it off a wrist, and it was going. The four faces were all going, four times on four worlds he'd never been to, and the little moon was up in its window. Grey had got into the links. He picked at it with a nail, and it came out as dust and went on his fingers.

He put it on. He did the buckle up on the hole it had always been on.

All the rest of the day he checked it. He'd lift his wrist for the time and it would be there, and he'd look at it, and put his arm down, and in a while he'd lift it again.

---

In the evening he was in the barracks yard when the new one came back through the slot from the stack outside with a sack on each shoulder, and the bell for the night count had already gone. Eli heard the slot before he saw it. Something hit the inside of the white wall, hard, and a sound came from the gap that wasn't a word, and after it the pleasant voice said, "Late. Logged."

The new one came through the slot on his knees. The sacks had gone down either side of him and one had split, and the dark new dirt was running out of it across the white stone. He had his hands out in front of him, open, held up, and spit was coming out of the side of his mouth and he couldn't stop it. The paper had come off his neck. Under it the scar was new and pink, closed with a strip, and the strip had lifted at one end.

He saw Eli. He tried to get up, and his legs wouldn't do it.

Eli got down by him in the mouth of the slot. The disc was set in the stone over them, at the height of the new one's chest, and there was a light in it, low and steady, on the new one. Eli's face was a hand from the glass. It didn't come on for him. It stayed on the new one's neck until the new one got his hands down, and then it went out.

"It's all right," Eli said. "It's all right. Stay there."

The new one stayed there.

Eli went over to the barracks step, where the steward stood with the slate.

"Take it off. His late."

The steward's thumb stayed still on the slate. "Sir."

"Take it off. He's new. He wasn't to know."

He wrote, and held the slate, and it chimed, and he turned it face in against his shirt. "Off, sir."

Eli went back to the slot. The new one was on his feet now, scooping the spilled dirt back into the split sack with both hands, fast, getting all of it, and when most of it was in he got down and picked the last out of the cracks between the stones with his fingers.

"Leave it," Eli said. "Somebody'll sweep it."

The new one went on picking it out of the cracks.

In the kitchen the cook had her coat on to go. She stopped at the table on her way to the door and took a plum out of the bowl, a dark one with the bloom still on it, and turned it over, and put it back with the others.

"I never got plums," she said, to the bowl, and went.

Eli lifted his wrist and looked at the time.

---

He didn't sleep. He lay with his wrist up in the dark over his face and watched the little moon go over in its window, so slow you could only see it had moved if you looked away and back, and he looked away and back.

At the bell he came down with his boots in his hand.

The kitchen light was off.

There was grey in the window from the yard, enough to see the table by, and a man sitting at it, in Eli's chair, the one that faced the yard door, with his coat off and hung over the back of it and his sleeves pushed up, eating a plum. He was fifty or so, big through the chest and the shoulders, with grey hair cut close to his skull and a short grey beard, and the plum was nearly done. Three stones lay on the table already, in a row, and he set the fourth down beside them and took another plum from the bowl. The bowl sat where it always sat. The letter was under it, with the pale corner showing.

He glanced up at Eli in the doorway and smiled.

"Morning," he said.

Eli's boots came up against his chest, both of them, and he held them there. The yard door was behind the man, and past it, across the yard, the steward read a number off the slate at a dark window, and somebody answered. Eli's eyes went to the door. He got his breath in.

"Sorry about Vosk," the man said.

He said it round the plum, and bit the plum.

The breath went out of Eli again. He stood in the doorway holding the boots.

"Look at the face on ya," the man said. "Sit down. It's your kitchen."

Eli put the boots down. He stayed standing.

"You knew him," he said.

"Knew of him." He turned the plum and bit it. "Did the trees here."

"Nine years."

The man nodded, chewing. "Then he wants a chip."

"I paid for it," Eli said. "Eleven hundred. It was my money. He was going to pay it back, a mark a month, he had it all worked out, he was going to—" Eli stopped. Outside, the steward said a number, and somebody answered. "I paid it."

"I know you did, son."

"There was one with a hook," Eli said. "At the end. He knelt on him."

"They'll have had the chip out." He found the last of the plum with his teeth and ate it. "Rest of him goes by weight, down there."

Eli's hand closed partway, as it had at the window, and stopped. He pushed it. It shook, and went no further, and he held it like that at his side.

Across the table the man wiped his fingers on the cloth the cook left folded by the bowl, and sat back.

"He's asking after you," he said. "You'll have heard."

"Who?"

"Big lad. Under the arch." He held his hands apart over the table, wide, and then wider. "Eats on Pole Street."

"The King," Eli said.

"He likes that."

"He's asking."

"He is."

"The Ilders' man told me." Eli came to the table with the hand still crooked at his side, and put his other arm down flat on the wood by the row of stones, and turned the wrist over so the faces showed. "He sent this up."

The man was looking at Eli.

"He'll go on asking," he said.

Eli sat down. The man took a plum and held it out to him across the table. "Go on. You've not eaten."

Eli took it. He held it in his hand.

Out in the yard the count came near the end, and the steward read the number, and the new voice answered it, too loud, all of it, and the man tipped his head at the window, and turned back.

"I'm putting a crew together," he said. "There's a job."

Eli waited.

"Ends at the top of the Tower."

Out past the window, past the long house roof, the Tower stood up out of the grey with the first of the light on it, pale, up and up into the cloud, where you couldn't see the end of it. Eli turned and looked at it. He'd looked at it every day of his life from this kitchen, from his bed, from the Vennicks' roof with his eyes shut and his face up.

"He looked at it," Eli said. "At the end. He was on the stones and he—" He stopped.

The man waited.

"What's up there?"

"Never been." The man looked at the plum in his hand and turned it to the light. "Better fruit than this, I'd hope."

Eli laughed. It came out of him before he knew it was there, one bark, and it hurt the side of his face, and the man laughed with him.

"I'd love ya to come." He reached into the bowl and took a plum, and turned it over, and put it back, and took a different one. "I would."

"What would you want me for?"

The man's eyes went down to Eli's hand on the table, the one with the plum in it, and stayed there, and came back up.

"I can't do it," Eli said. "Not when people are looking. I put my hand up and it just—" He took the hand off the table, plum and all. "Everything sits there."

"There's a teacher," the man said. He bit the plum. "You'll not like it much."

"All right," Eli said. "All right. What do you need? Is it money? I can ring the account, it doesn't ask, it'll be back before—"

"Keep your money, son." He chewed. "I'll tell ya some of what I want."

"Go on."

"You come today."

"Yes."

"You don't come back here after."

"Yes."

"And when I say do a thing, you do it. Then. Not after you've had a think." He looked at the plum and turned it to the next bite.

"Yes," Eli said. "Yes."

The man ate the plum.

"Teo comes," Eli said. "My friend. He was there. He was there for all of it, he got me out, he—Teo comes."

"If he wants."

"He'll want."

"Go and ask him, then." He set the stone down at the end of the row and squared it with a fingertip against the others. "I'll be here."

Eli got up and put the plum down on the table, whole, at the end of the row of stones. At the door he turned round, and the man was reaching for the bowl again. The letter's corner showed under it, pale, with the grey wax on the far side where you couldn't see it. Eli looked at it, and the man looked at it too, once, the corner, and took an apple from the bowl, and sat back with it.

Eli went out by the front with his boots in his hand, and put them on on the path. The watch said ten past six.

---

Teo's mother opened the door in her apron with a wooden spoon in her hand, and porridge on the spoon. She considered Eli's boots, laced wrong, and his face.

"He's up," she said. "He's to be on the Spur at nine. There's a man there doesn't write things down." She held the door. "He's to go."

"I'll tell him."

Upstairs the trunk was empty, with its lid down, pushed under the window. Everything that had been in it was in its place. The books were on the shelf, the cup was in its ring on the sill, the stone was beside the cup, the shirts were gone into the drawer, and the room looked like a room somebody lived in. Teo was sitting on the bed in his trousers with the slate on his knee and the bad hand turned up on his thigh. The dressing had gone yellow in the middle. The skin round it was shiny and pink as far as the wrist, and there was a smell in the room, faint and sweet, that hadn't been there before.

Eli shut the door behind him and stood against it.

"There's a man in my kitchen," he said.

He told it. He told it in the wrong order and went back. The plums. The King, and where the King ate. The Tower. The teacher. He held his wrist out and turned it so the four faces caught the window, and the little moon, and Teo looked at the watch, and lifted his eyes to Eli's face, and went back to the watch, and his mouth stayed shut.

"He knew Vosk," Eli said. "He said his name. First thing, before anything. Sorry about Vosk."

"He knew his name," Teo said.

"That's what I'm telling you."

Teo watched him. He put the bad hand down flat on the bed and thumbed at the slate with the other, slow, and read, and thumbed, and read. He turned it round.

It was the gate's page again, the one he'd had on the counter, run on to now. The Ilders' people out late, and their flyers up from the kerb. The cook's girl with the bread, in at five, out at ten past. After the bread there was nothing, not at the arch and not at the kerb, down to the bottom of the page, and the bottom of the page was now.

"Nobody came in, Eli," Teo said.

"So he came another way."

Teo kept the slate turned to him.

"So there's another way," Eli said. "There's the slot. There's the culvert, there's—it doesn't matter how he got in. Listen." He came off the door. His voice went clear and reasonable. "It's perfectly simple. He's asking. He sent my watch up, Teo. To the Ilders' man. Three doors from my house. He knows which house. You heard what they said about the coat. He'll come up." He held up a finger, and another. "And it's today. We go today, we don't come back here after, and when he says do a thing we do it. I've said yes. I've said it."

"You don't know what it's for," Teo said.

"No."

Teo put the slate down on the bed, face down.

"It was eleven hundred marks," Eli said. "And a chip off a dwarf. And my watch, and a soup stall. That's what it was. And I came home and had a bath." He stopped. "She sweeps round his coat. The cook. It's on the step and she sweeps round it."

Teo looked at the floor between his feet.

"Teo. Listen. Listen to me."

Teo sat and listened.

"You're not doing anything," Eli said. "You're sitting here. They've put you back to the spring, and in the spring they'll put you back to the winter, and you'll pack and unpack and put your cup back in its ring. You've nowhere to be."

Teo sat still.

"So come," Eli said. "That's all it is. Come with me. He said you could. Teo."

Downstairs the spoon went round against the side of a pot.

"Say something."

Teo sat.

"Say something, then! You've had three days. You said plenty in the Scald, you said plenty with your hand—" He stopped. He stood in the middle of the room and breathed. "Teo."

Teo's hair was wet at the front, though the window was open and the room was cold. He put his thumb on the skin by the edge of the dressing and pressed, and took it away, and the place stayed white. He watched it. It came back slowly, pink, then more than pink. On the bed his mother's slate was lit with one line, CLINIC, SEVENTH, NOON, and her mark beside it. He turned it face down.

Teo got up. He went round the trunk to the window, and stood at the sill, and picked up the stone. He turned it over in his good hand. He put it in his trouser pocket, and left the cup in its ring. He sat down on the lid of the trunk and put his boots on. He laced the left one. He tried the right one with the bad hand, and the hand wouldn't close on the lace, and he did it with the good hand and his teeth, slow, and stood up.

He took the slate off the bed and put it inside his shirt.

Eli had been talking. He heard himself stop.

Teo opened the door and went down. At the foot of the stairs his mother came out of the kitchen with the spoon.

"Your hand," she said.

"Right," said Teo, and went past her, and out.

Eli came down after him. She was still standing there. She held the spoon, and a drop of porridge went off the end of it onto the boards, and she watched Eli to the door.

"Nine," she said. "He's to be there at nine."

"I'll tell him," Eli said, and went after Teo.

---

The man was where Eli had left him. There were seven stones on the table now, in a row, and Eli's plum was gone, and past the last stone an apple core was eaten down to the pips, and the bowl had two pears in it and nothing else. The light had come up. The yard was empty, the count long done. The watch said twenty to seven.

He stood up when they came in.

"Morning," he said to Teo.

Teo stopped inside the door and said nothing.

"Jon," the man said, and put his hand out.

Teo's right hand was the one in the dressing. He kept it at his side. Jon looked down at it, and up at Teo, and took his own hand back and put it in his trouser pocket, and with the other he picked his coat up off the chair and shrugged it on.

"This way," he said.

He took a pear out of the bowl as he went by it, and went to the yard door. On the step inside it the second-best coat lay folded in four where Vosk had left it, and he stepped over it and out.

Eli bent and picked it up. He shook it out. The folds stood up in it in a cross, sharp. He put it on. It hung heavy on the left side, low, where the sock was down at the bottom of the pocket. His good coat was in the hall on its hook, pressed. He left it there.

Jon was out on the step in the light with his back to them, eating the pear, his face turned to the long house. Eli stood by the table with his eyes on Jon's back. He lifted the bowl. The pears rolled together in it. The letter was there, face down, the grey wax up, and he took it out from under, and it was as heavy as it had been on the first morning. Twenty-four years, he'd said. They could have tonight. That had been four nights ago. He set the bowl down on the bare wood.

When he went out after them, the letter was in the inside pocket of the coat, against his ribs, the corner of it digging in when he walked.

In the hall the house bell rang.

Teo turned his head.

Jon walked on across the yard without turning round. Eli walked after him, faster. Behind them the steward's feet crossed the hall, and his voice came out through the open kitchen, flat.

"Ninth, the house."

They went past the long house and the barracks. The little ones were out on the barracks step with a bit of bread between them, and they stopped chewing and watched the three of them go by. The smaller one stood up. Neither of them said anything.

The slot was a gap in the white wall above the culvert, one orc wide, with the soil sacks stacked on the far side of it. There was no arch and no glass, only the disc set in the stone at the height of an orc's chest. Jon went in first. He filled most of it, and he went through sideways, with the pear held up clear of the wall, and the disc didn't light for him.

"Mind your head," he said.

Eli went in after him. The walls of the slot were close and smooth and white, and on the left-hand side, a little above his own head, there was a mark on the white, a dark smear gone brown, with grit dried into it. He looked at it going past.

The disc in the wall stayed dark. It didn't look at him. There was no light, and no chime, and no voice to tell him what time of day it was. He came out the other side onto the head of the culvert steps and stopped on them, and waited a step, and it didn't come.

He looked back. The disc stayed dark for Teo too, and Teo stepped out of the slot with his bad hand held against his chest.

Below them the culvert ran down between its high walls toward the Spur, wet, with the morning in the top of it and the dark still in the bottom. Jon went down ahead of them, eating the pear.
