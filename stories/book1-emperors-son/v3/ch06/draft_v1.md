## SIX · The Healer

Teo sat up in the night and talked to the lamp.

"—the step," he said. "She'll have it swept. He's to— Tell him he's to go."

"Lie down," Eli said. "Teo. Lie down, you're—"

Seffa had him already. She put a hand flat on his chest and laid him back on the sacks the way you'd set a chair straight, and he went, and his eyes stayed open on the lamp and went on talking to it, too low now to hear. The coat had come off his shoulder. She put it back.

"He's hot," Eli said.

"He's hot, love." She tucked the collar in under his chin. "Hot's alive."

Under the yellow lamp the pink had gone the colour of a bruise coming out. It ran up out of the collar and along under the jaw to the hinge of it, just below the ear, and the skin there was tight and shone. When Teo's head rolled over to the side Eli could see the vein going in it, quick and hard, a long way too quick.

The barge hummed. It had hummed all day and all the first night, a sound you stopped hearing and then heard again all at once, in your back teeth. The lid stayed down. The lamp swung on its hook whenever the barge leaned and threw the shadows of the sacks up the hull and down again. Somewhere forward, past a bulkhead with a door in it that no one ever came through, men were playing at a game with a cup and a lot of noise, and every so often a cheer came through the iron, flat and far off, and then not another for an hour.

Eli counted the horns. Four since Ithe, each a long one and a short. He had no idea what a horn meant on a barge, whether it was a watch or a mile or a man wanting his dinner, but he counted them, and he'd got to four.

"By the next one," he said. "Or the one after. And I'll do the talking, Teo, you'll just lie there. You won't have to say a word. I'll tell him all of it, the arm and the pills and the tape, and he'll have you up in no time. He'll be marvellous. Seffa says he's marvellous."

"I said he was good to her," Seffa said.

"There you are, then."

She had the cup out. She'd been at it since the ramp. Water from the can by the steps, a finger's depth in the bottom of the cup, then Teo's head lifted in the crook of her arm and the rim put to his lip, and a little, and wait, and a little. Most of it went in. Some went down his neck into the collar, and she wiped that out with her thumb. Then she laid him down and said a number under her breath, and when she got to wherever the number went, she did it again.

"Let me," Eli said.

"Hold that." She unhooked the lamp and gave it to him. "Not like that. Like it's a baby."

He held it up in both hands with the bad one underneath, and the light came down over Teo's face, and Seffa looked at the jaw in it and said nothing about the jaw. The lamp was warm on his palms. It was the only warm thing he'd had hold of since Ithe. He kept it up until his arms shook, and then for a while past that.

---

Near the end of the night Teo's eyes came back.

Eli saw it happen. They'd been going past him, at the hull and the lamp and the dark up under the lid, and then they stopped going and came to his face, and they were Teo's, sore at the rims and very tired.

"It's at my jaw," Teo said.

"It's not. It's only—" Eli brought the lamp down. "A bit. It's a bit up. We're nearly there."

Teo pushed the coat off his chest with his good hand, the whole coat, down to his waist. Seffa reached over and he took the cup out of her fingers before she could get her arm under him. He got himself up on the elbow and drank, all of it, with his throat going, and held the cup out at the end of his arm without looking at either of them until Seffa took it. Then he lay down. He lay with his face to the hull.

Seffa pulled the coat back up.

Eli hung the lamp on its hook. He sat on the sacks at Teo's feet in his shirt, and the cold came in at him off the hull, and he tried the hand. Partway. He held it there and pushed and it shook and went no further, and he put it between his knees.

The barge leaned, and came back.

"He'd know what to do," Eli said. "Jon. Wherever he's got to."

Seffa had her head back against the iron and her eyes shut. One of them opened, and looked at him, and shut.

"Is there any of the bread?"

She found him the heel of it in her bag without opening her eyes.

---

The fit came out of nothing, an hour on, with Teo asleep.

His heels went first. They drummed on the sacks, both together, a quick dull beat, and then his back came up off the sacks in an arch with his head driven back into the hull, and his jaw was shut so hard Eli heard his teeth, and the noise coming out past them wasn't a word or anything near one.

"Legs," Seffa said. "Get his legs. Eli."

Eli got them. He threw himself across Teo's shins and got his arms round, and the good hand took hold of a boot, and the bad hand took hold of nothing. He felt the fingers go on, and shut partway, and slide. Teo's other leg tore out of his arms and the boot hit him in the face, on the swollen side, and the lamp went over and round in a white smear.

When he could see again Seffa was lying on Teo.

All of her. She'd got her chest across his chest and one arm down over his hips and pinning both legs, and her cheek against his cheek, and her whole weight on him, and she was holding him down into the sacks while he went. He went for a long time. His good hand beat on her back and she let it. She said something into his ear, over and over, too low for Eli, the same three or four words. The arch went out of him by degrees. The heels slowed. Then he was only shaking, and then he was lying there under her, breathing hard, with his eyes open.

Seffa stayed where she was. She stayed until the breathing evened, and longer.

Then she got up off him, onto her knees, and took the rag out of her sleeve and wiped his mouth. There was blood on it, from his tongue.

"Good," Eli said. "Yes. Good. That's it, that's what I'd have— That was exactly right. Well done. Thank you."

She looked at him. Then she sat back against the hull with her knees up and her hands hanging off them, and the hands were shaking, and she put them under her arms.

His cheek was opening up again on the inside. He could taste it. He picked the lamp up off the sacks and it was still lit, and he hung it back on its hook.

---

The lid came back in the morning with a shriek of hinges, and the light that came down into the hold was white.

White and cold, with salt in the wind. Eli went up the steps with his teeth going and stood on the deck in his shirt, and looked.

A cliff. It went straight up out of the water on one side of them, black, and somebody had cut it into steps, great square steps going up and back, each one the height of a house, with men on them. He could see them, small, going along the ledges with barrows, and the sound came down off the face in the wind, hammers, a tock and a tock, never together. Dust came off the top of it and blew out over the water in a long black smear and settled on everything. It was on the deck. It was on the snow that lay along the roofs of the town under the cliff, so that the snow was grey, and the town was black stone and grey roofs going up in a huddle from the quay with smoke coming off it sideways.

On the other side, the sea. Grey and heavy, the swell coming in slow under the barge and lifting it and going on to slap at the quay, and ice in the weed at the waterline. Past the end of the sea wall it went out flat to nothing. There was a sun, small and red, the same as Ithe's, higher up here and no warmer.

"Come on," Seffa said behind him. "Help me with him."

They got him up the steps between them. Teo walked some of it. His legs went at the top, and they took him across the deck with his boots dragging, and down the ramp, where the man on the ramp looked at Teo and looked at Seffa.

"Going back?" Seffa said.

"When she's full." The man turned his head and spat into the water between the barge and the quay. "Dark. Maybe."

"We'll be on her."

He didn't write anything down.

Seffa knew the way. She went along the quay with Teo's good arm over her shoulders and never looked up at the turnings, away from the town, along the top of the sea wall where the wind came straight off the water and through Eli's shirt. The wall went a long way round. Down on the rocks under it the sea went in and out, and up on top it was flagged with black stone, slick, with salt in white crusts in the joints.

At the end, where the wall curved, there was a long low house built of the same black stone, no windows on the sea side, with a roof of tarred canvas held down by ropes and the ropes weighted with rocks. Smoke came out of a pipe at one end. A curtain of sacking hung over the door.

There were people all along the wall in front of it. Sitting on the flags with their backs to the stone, and lying, and some on doors and boards that somebody had carried them there on, out of the wind as far as the house would put them, which wasn't far. They went back along the wall from the door to where Seffa had stopped.

She had stopped. She stood at the end of them with Teo hanging off her and looked at the sacking over the door, and Eli waited for her to go on, and she didn't, not straight away.

"No reader," she said. "He's not on anything." She shifted Teo higher. "Back of the line, love."

"But he's—"

"They're all dying. That's what the line is."

---

The man in front of them was a quarryman. Eli could tell by the black in the creases of his hands and down in the lines round his eyes, where washing never got it out. He sat with his knees up and coughed into a rag. It took him a long time each go, a deep tearing cough from right down, and when he took the rag away and looked at it, the rag was black, and then it was pink.

In front of him a woman sat with a bundle in a shawl that had a baby in it, and the baby was very quiet, and she kept moving the shawl back off its face and looking. In front of her a young man with his back to the wall and his eyes open. Behind his ear the hair was cut away in a square. There was a brown crust under one nostril, old, and a new line of red coming down out of it, and he left it.

Then a girl with a yellow face. Then an old man on a door, under a blanket, with two women standing over him who weren't talking to each other.

Two old women sat on the flags near the door with their shawls up over their heads like hoods.

"Forty year," one of them said. "Sat in there with that lot all day. Forty year. Never a cold."

"You never know who your neighbour is," said the other. "Might be two. Might be three."

"Give over, Mam."

The wind came along the wall and took the end of whatever the old one said next.

Eli stood it as long as he could. He could feel Teo's heat through the coat from where he was holding him, and Teo's head was down on Seffa's shoulder and his breath came short and wet, and every time the quarryman coughed Teo flinched at it.

"I'll only ask," Eli said. "It can't hurt to ask."

He went up the line.

"Good morning." His face hurt with the cold and the boot and he smiled anyway, at the front of the line, at the two old women and the old man on his door and the women standing over him. "I'm so sorry. I'm hoping— He's very bad, you see. My friend. It's gone up his neck, it's at his jaw, and we've come from Ithe, we've been on the boat all night, and I wonder whether—"

The old man's women looked at him.

"It's not that I think we're more— It's only that he's young. Honestly. Ask anyone."

"Ask him," said the one who'd said Give over, and tipped her head at the quarryman.

Eli got the sock out of his trouser pocket. The coins slid down into the toe and knocked. "I can pay. Whatever's usual, double, I'm happy to—"

"Put that away," said the old woman, "before someone has it off you, and you with it."

The sacking over the door went back.

The gnome who came out under it was small even for a gnome. The top of his head came about to Eli's ribs. He had a round face, red with the stove he'd come from, and a round chin with white stubble on it, and his ears were enormous: they stood out from his head like the handles on a jug and went up past the top of it to points, and they moved. They turned toward the quarryman's cough before the rest of him did. He had a long apron on that had been white once and his sleeves rolled up over brown forearms, and he was drying his hands on a rag, finger by finger.

"Doc," somebody said, down the wall.

He looked along the line. He didn't look at Eli.

He went down it. He went along the wall from the door with the rag over his shoulder, and stopped at each of them, and bent, and with his thumb he lifted an eyelid, and looked, and let it down. The old man on the door. The girl with the yellow face.

"There you are," he said to her. "Took your time."

The young man with the square behind his ear. Doc thumbed his eye open and looked a while longer at that one, and let it go, and said nothing to him at all.

The woman with the bundle caught his hand as he went by her and got it to her mouth.

"Don't," he said. He took it back. "I've had it in a goat."

Eli went after him. "Excuse me. I'm sorry. Good morning. I was just saying to these ladies, my friend's very bad, it's in his arm, from his hand, it's gone right up, we've come from Ithe on the—"

Doc stepped round him.

He came to the quarryman, and bent, and lifted the eyelid, and the quarryman coughed in his face and he kept his own face where it was. "Hello, you," he said. Then he came to Teo.

He stood in front of him for a bit, with his head about level with Teo's chest. Teo's head was on Seffa's shoulder. Doc reached up and thumbed open the near eye, and looked in it, and then he put one finger in Teo's collar and pulled it down, and looked at the jaw.

"Off-world, are you?" he said. "I can smell it." He sniffed. "No, I can't. It's the coat." He let the collar go. "That one."

"Yes," Eli said. "Thank you, yes, that's what I was—"

"You. Shirt. Pick him up."

"Since Thursday," somebody said along the wall, not loud. "Some of us. Since Thursday, sat here."

Eli got Teo's other arm over his shoulders. Seffa looked at no one. They went up the line with Teo between them, past the old man on his door, past the yellow girl, past the bundle and the young man with the blood coming down his lip, and the woman who'd spoken was the one standing over the old man on the door. She watched them all the way. Doc held the sacking back.

---

Inside it was hot, and dim, and it smelled.

It smelled of lye, and vinegar, and of the stove, which was burning something that wasn't wood, and of piss, and of under all of that a sweet thick smell that got into the back of Eli's throat and stayed there. It was one long room under the canvas. Lamps hung down the middle of it on a wire. Down both sides there were pallets on the floor, straw ticks, with people on them, and some of the people had someone sitting by them on the floor and some had no one. Somebody down the far end was moaning a word over and over. A woman went between the pallets with a bucket.

At the very end there was a curtain on a wire, a blanket, brown, that went from wall to wall, and behind it somebody was breathing. Eli could hear it from the door. A long dragging breath in, like a saw going through wet wood, and then nothing, and nothing, and then another. Under the hem of the blanket he could see a pair of boots, a man's, sitting, the heels together.

By the stove there was a door laid flat on two trestles.

"There," Doc said.

They put Teo on it. Teo's head went back against the wood and his eyes rolled up, and came back, and rolled up.

"Lamp." Doc unhooked one off the wire and put it in Eli's hands. "Up. Higher. Over his arm. There. Now don't move it."

Seffa stood at the end of the trestles, by Teo's feet. She was looking down the room at the blanket on its wire.

"She was behind there," she said.

"Who was?" Eli said.

"Mam."

"Oh." He held the lamp. "I'm sorry."

She took Teo's boots off, one and then the other, and stood them together under the trestles. She got the coat off him and folded it on the end of the door.

Doc had a pair of scissors out of his apron, small ones with curved blades, and he went in under the grey tape on Teo's palm and cut it, snip, snip, and peeled it back, and the smell came up out of it. Eli's eyes ran. Under the tape the palm was split open where the kit blade had cut it clean, and it wasn't clean now, the edges gone grey and soft and lifted, and yellow in the bottom of it, and the whole hand round it swollen up hard and shining to the wrist.

"Who taped this?" Doc said. "A blind cobbler?"

"I did," Eli said.

"Hm." Doc turned the hand over. Teo made a noise. "With your feet?"

"I didn't have—it was in a ship—"

Doc wasn't listening. He'd reached up past the lamp, and his fingers, small and dry, went round Eli's wrist, the bad side. They closed once. They turned the wrist a little to the left, and a little to the right, and Eli felt the tendons pull in the back of his hand all the way to the knuckles.

"Hm," Doc said, and let it go.

He took a brown bottle off the shelf by the stove and pulled the cork with his teeth.

"My brother had a goat," he said round the cork. "Did I say? No. Course I didn't, I've never met you." He spat the cork into his apron pocket. "Hold his arm, girl. Not there, at the elbow. Lean on it." Seffa leaned. "Ate my whole herb garden in a night, this goat. Feverfew. Sorrel. The lot. And the fence."

He poured.

Teo came up off the door. Seffa leaned and held him and Teo's good hand came round and got her by the hair and she let it. The noise he made went down the room and the moaning at the far end stopped to listen to it. Vinegar, and something under the vinegar that burned Eli's eyes from where he stood, and Doc poured it straight into the open hand and went in after it with a twist of rag on a stick.

"And the fence," Doc said. "Did I say the fence? Then it lay down in the hole where the sorrel had been and looked at me like I'd planted it there for him. A goat. Lift the lamp. Lift it. My brother says, well, it's a goat, what did you expect, and I said, I expected a *fence*—"

He went in with the rag again. Teo screamed through his teeth.

"—and he says, a fence is only a suggestion, to a goat. Hold still, you. You've a lovely jaw, I'd hate to lose it."

Eli laughed. It came out of him on its own, high, and he couldn't stop it, and the lamp shook.

"Lamp," said Doc.

Down the end of the room, behind the blanket, the breathing caught. It went in on its long saw and stuck halfway, and there was a sound of a stool going over, and a man's voice said something, sharp, just the one word.

Doc put the stick down across Teo's palm.

"Don't touch that," he said. "Hold the lamp. Talk to him."

"About what?"

"Anything. The goat." He wiped his hands on the rag over his shoulder and went down the room between the pallets, at no particular speed, with his ears up, and put the blanket aside, and let it fall behind him.

Eli stood with the lamp.

"So there was this goat," he said to Teo. Teo's eyes were open and on the canvas overhead. "It ate the fence. And his brother said—what did he say. He said it was only a fence. No. That the fence was the goat's idea. And then—"

Behind the blanket somebody was talking. Doc. Low and easy, no words Eli could make out, on and on, a voice for a horse. Then the other voice, the sharp one, a man's, and Doc again, and the boots under the hem had stood up and were standing very close together.

"—and then it lay down in the hole," Eli said, "where the herbs had been. And looked at him. As if he'd done it on purpose." He held the lamp higher. "It's a good story. Isn't it? I'll tell it better when I've heard the end."

Teo's lips moved. Eli bent down.

"Lamp," said Teo.

Eli put it back up.

The blanket went aside and Doc came out from behind it and let it fall. He came back up the room between the pallets the same as he'd gone down it, at no speed, wiping his hands one finger at a time on the rag. He stopped at the stove and held them out to it. He looked at the stove.

"Three," he said.

He said it to the stove, not loud. Then he came back to the trestles and picked the stick up off Teo's palm.

"Where was I," he said. "The fence."

He didn't go back in with the rag. He put the stick down on the shelf. He took Teo's forearm in both his hands, below the elbow, and his hands were too small to go round it, so that the fingers came round each side and stopped, and the two thumbs lay together on the inside of the arm where the line ran up under the skin.

"So I got another fence," he said. "Better one. Stakes as thick as your leg. Took me a week. And I'm standing there admiring it of an evening, and I look round, and where's the goat?"

His thumbs went up the arm. Slow. From the wrist toward the elbow, pressing hard enough to whiten the skin ahead of them. They got to the elbow and stopped and came back down and went up again.

"On the *roof*," Doc said. "Of the shed. Eating the thatch. How does a goat get up on a roof? You tell me."

"I don't know," Eli said.

"Nor did my brother. He thought it was funny. Lift the lamp."

Eli lifted it.

He watched the arm because it was what the lamp was on. The thumbs went up and came down. Teo's breathing went on short and fast, and then less fast. The hand at the end of the arm lay open on the door, split and swollen and wet with the vinegar, and the fingers past the split were fat and shining, and then, if Eli looked at them and looked away at Doc's ears and looked back, they weren't so fat. The knuckles were there again. He could see the knuckles.

He brought the lamp up over Teo's face.

The colour under the jaw had gone back from the hinge. It was down under the line of the jaw, and when he looked again it was down at the collar, and he pulled the collar open with the bad hand, which went far enough to do that, and the colour went down Teo's neck into his shirt as he watched it, pink, and paler, like a tide going out down a flat beach.

"It's going down," Eli said. "Teo. The red. It's going down."

Teo turned his head.

"I told you." Eli was grinning. He could feel it pull the swollen side. "Didn't I tell you? The best one there is. I said. Seffa, look at it. I said."

"Don't shout," said Doc. "I'm not deaf." He took one hand off the arm and flicked one of his ears with a fingernail, and it turned over and flicked back. "Look at the size of these."

Seffa was looking. Eli saw her. She'd let go of the elbow when Doc took the arm and gone down to the end of the trestles, and she stood there with her hands on Teo's ankles, and she looked at the arm, and then she looked at Doc's hands on it, and then at Doc. At his face. She was still looking at his face when Doc had gone back to the arm and the goat and Eli had looked away and looked back. Her own face did nothing at all.

"Some of them get up," Doc said, to the arm. "That's the whole trick. Some of them do."

Teo's eyes had come back. They were on the canvas and then they weren't, they'd gone down the room, past the lamps on their wire and the pallets and the woman with the bucket, to the far end. To the brown blanket on its wire. They stayed there.

The stove ticked. Somebody down the left side coughed, and coughed. Out past the canvas the sea went in under the wall and out again.

Doc let go of the arm.

"There," he said. "Now. Now then. Somebody give him a drink, I'm not a well."

---

He washed his hands in a basin on the shelf, in water that steamed, and dried them, and dropped the rag in a bucket.

Eli put the lamp back on its wire. He had to try twice; his arms had gone. Then he got the sock out of his pocket and held it out by the toe, with the coins down in it.

"What do we owe you? I'm sorry, I should have asked first, I've no idea what's usual here. Whatever it is. Honestly. There's an account as well, at home, so if it's more than this, I can—"

"Put that back in your trousers," said Doc.

"But you've—"

"I've what." Doc looked at the sock. "Is that a sock?"

"It was a friend's."

"Then give it him back." He dried between his fingers with the corner of his apron. He looked at Teo on the door, who had his eyes shut now and was breathing like a man asleep, slow and deep, with his mouth open. He looked at Seffa, who had gone to the stove and was pouring water from the kettle into a cup. Then he looked up at Eli, all the way up, with his head back.

"I've had a mind to travel," he said.

"Have you?"

"Fifty years I've had it. Longer. It keeps, a mind like that. Like a cheese." His ears went round toward the door, where the wind was getting in under the sacking, and came back. "Where's it you're going?"

"Ithe. Back to Ithe, there's a man there. A teacher. He's going to take me on. He's as good as said so." Eli felt himself start to smile and let it. "And then after that—well, I'll be set up. Properly. You'll see. And when I am, I'll send for you. I will. You'll come and you'll have—a whole wing, whatever you want, your own place, all the goats you like." He laughed. "Only not now. We're going back to a yard, there's no room, there's barely room for us, and Teo's going to need the—you can see. But I'll send. You'll come later, properly. I'll send a boat."

Doc looked at him a while.

"Suit yourself," he said.

He went off down the room to the woman on the third pallet, who had her hand up, and got down beside her on his knees.

Eli let his breath out. It had gone well. He'd said it well, and Doc had taken it the right way. He'd have to remember. A boat, and a wing. He'd have to remember to do it.

Seffa came back with the cup and put it in Teo's good hand, and Teo woke and drank. He held it himself. Then he got his elbow under him and sat up on the door, slowly, with his legs over the side, and sat there with his bare feet hanging and the cup in his lap. His hand lay open beside him on the wood, the split in it pink now and closed at the edges, and the fingers lying there, ordinary.

He looked at the hand.

The blanket at the end of the room went aside.

The man who came out from behind it was forty or so, with quarry black in the creases of his hands like the cougher outside, and a cap in them, which he was turning round and round by the brim. He let the blanket fall behind him. He came up the room between the pallets, and he wasn't looking at Doc, or at anyone. He was looking at Teo.

Teo, sitting up on the door with a cup.

He stopped at the end of the trestles. He looked at Teo's feet, bare, hanging, and at the boots under the trestles, and at the coat folded on the end of the door, the long one with the good cut and the yellow on its cuffs.

"You took him first," he said.

He didn't say it loud. He said it to the coat.

Doc had got up off his knees down the room. He stood by the third pallet with his hands at his sides.

"You took him first." Louder. The man turned round to the room. "Off the boat this morning. In a coat. And you took him first, in front of us. In front of my dad. Four days, we sat out there. Four days on that wall."

Down the room people were sitting up on their pallets. The sacking over the door had gone back, and the faces from the wall were in the doorway, the two old women, the quarryman with his rag, and in front of them the woman who'd watched them come in, the one from the old man's door.

"That's right," she said. "Since Thursday. That's right."

"Look at him," said the man. "Sat up. Drinking. Look at him."

"Excuse me," Eli said. He came round the trestles with his hands out. "I'm so sorry. I'm sorry about your father, truly, I— If there's been some sort of— I'm happy to pay for the—"

He had the sock in his hand still. He hadn't put it away. The man looked at it.

Somebody in the doorway laughed, short.

"He'll pay," the woman said. "Hear that? He'll pay."

The man let go of his cap. It went on the floor. He took one step and put the flat of both hands on Eli's chest and shoved, and Eli went back into the trestles, and the door slid on them and Teo grabbed the edge with his good hand and the cup went over, and the coins in the sock went across the floor under the pallets, rolling, every way. A child on the near pallet put her hand out flat on one and kept it there.

Doc came up the room.

He came between the pallets at no speed at all and stopped in front of the man, with his head about level with the man's belt, and the man looked down at him with his hands still up.

"I wasn't able to save him," Doc said.

That was all. He didn't say it kindly or any other way. He said it, and stood there.

"You took him first."

Doc said nothing to that.

"You took that one first and you went in to my dad after and you never—you were in there one minute—"

Doc stood. His ears had gone flat back against his head. His hands were at his sides and his face was round and red from the stove and it did nothing, and the man stood over him shouting with his own face working, and it went on, and the room listened to it, and Doc let it.

Seffa put the kettle down.

She came round the stove and took the man by the arm, above the elbow, the way she'd taken Teo's. He tried to shake her off. She didn't let go.

"What was his name?" she said.

The man looked at her.

"Your dad," she said. "What did they call him?"

"Garro."

"Come and sit with Garro," Seffa said. "Come on. I'll bring you a cup. Come and sit with him, he's on his own back there." She had him turned already. "Come on."

He went. He went down the room with her hand on his arm, with his face all to pieces, and halfway he stopped and turned his head and looked back at Teo on the door, and Seffa said "Come on" and he came on, and the blanket went aside, and fell behind them.

The cap was on the floor where he'd dropped it.

In the doorway nobody went anywhere. The old woman who'd said Give over had her arms folded. The quarryman coughed into his rag. On the near pallet the child had picked up her coin and was looking at it.

Doc looked round the room.

He took his time about it. The pallets, down both sides, and who was on them. The woman with the bucket, standing still. The faces in the doorway with the white light and the wind behind them and more faces past those, along the wall, out of sight. The cap. Then he went to the shelf by the stove and took down a bag, an old leather bag with a strap, the leather gone pale at the corners, and put it on the shelf and opened it, and took things off the shelf one by one and put them in. The brown bottle. The scissors. A tin. A roll of rag.

"Well," he said. "That's my mind made up."

He untied his apron and hung it on the nail where the bag had been.

"Doc?" said the woman on the third pallet.

He went to her, and bent, and put the brown bottle in her hand and closed her fingers on it. "Twice a day," he said. "On it. Not in you." Then he did up the bag.

In the doorway, the woman with the bundle in her shawl had come to the front. She watched him do up the bag. She moved the shawl off the baby's face and looked down at it, and up at Doc, and down.

Doc put the strap over his shoulder. He went past her and out under the sacking with the bag knocking at the back of his knees, and the faces in the door let him through, and nobody stopped him, and he didn't look round.

Eli stood by the trestles.

"Where's he—" he said. "Wait. Where's he going?"

Teo was on his feet. He had one hand on the door and he was standing on the floor in his bare feet, on his own, looking at the sacking where it was still swinging.

"Boots," Teo said.

---

They had to go back through the doorway. The faces moved out of the way for them and that was all they did. Eli kept his eyes on the flags. Teo walked. He walked the whole length of the sea wall with the coat on and his boots on and the laces trailing, slowly, with his good hand on the stone of the wall, and he stopped once and went on.

Seffa came after them a while later, when they were nearly at the quay, at a walk, with her bag over her shoulder. She didn't say anything about Garro or his son and Eli didn't ask.

At the foot of the ramp the man was standing where he'd stood that morning, with his arms folded.

"He's gone aboard," he said, before Eli could ask anything. "Little feller. Paid."

"Paid?"

"Out of a tin." The man held his hand up, the fingers bunched, and opened them one at a time. "Counted them out into my hand. One at a time. Took him a while."

Down in the hold the lamp was on. Doc was sitting on his bag at the foot of the steps, against the hull, out of the way of the sacks, with his hands on his knees.

"Oh," Eli said, on the steps. "I— Look. I did say. There isn't the room, there's no bunk, there's barely—"

"I'm not in your bunk," said Doc. "I'm on my bag."

---

The lid came over at dark. It went over slowly, a long way up, and the white went out of the hold, and the lamp was all there was.

Teo ate.

He sat on the sacks with his back to the hull and the coat round him and ate with the good hand, the other one lying in his lap, open, and he ate everything Seffa gave him. Bread. A heel of hard cheese with the rind on, rind and all. The cold end of a sausage she'd had wrapped in a cloth at the bottom of her bag since Ithe. He ate it steadily and without talking, with his eyes on the food.

Seffa broke the last of the bread in three. She gave a piece to Eli and a piece to Teo, and the third she held out across the hold to Doc without asking him, at the end of her arm, until he leaned forward off his bag and took it.

"Ta," said Doc.

She sat back against the hull and looked at the three of them, eating.

"Somebody's got to feed you," she said, to the hull.

Nobody took that up.

Doc ate his bread in small bites, round the edge first, the crust, and then the middle. "Did I tell you about the second goat?" he said. "No? Worse than the first. The first one had *principles*. You knew where you were with the first one. The second one—" He took another bite. "My brother got it off a man at a fair. For a hat. A good hat. That should have told him." He chewed. "It never ate a thing it was given. Not once. It'd starve sooner. But anything you hadn't given it—"

The hum came up in the floor. The barge moved off the quay, and leaned, and came back. Doc's chin had gone down on his chest. The last of the bread was in his hand on his knee, and his ears had gone slowly down to either side, and his mouth was a little open.

"He's off," Eli said. He was grinning at it. "Look at him. Mid-goat."

He leaned his head back on the iron. He felt good. He felt better than he had since the pad on Ithe, since before that, since the culvert, he couldn't think since when.

"Bring him back breathing, he said," Eli said. "Well. He's breathing. He's *eating*. Look at him. Teo. You're eating."

Teo ate.

"He'll have to say yes now. He'll have to. He said it, and here you are, and more than he asked, frankly, you're better than when we got there. He'll see that." He looked across at Doc asleep on his bag. "And the old man'll like you, Doc. I know he will. Everyone likes you."

Doc slept.

Eli put his hand inside his shirt. The letter had dried against him on the sea wall in the wind, stiff, in waves, and the corner that had gone to pulp had dried hard, and broke a little under his thumb when he touched it. He took his hand out.

Teo had stopped eating. He was looking across the hold at Doc. He looked at him for a long time, at the round face gone slack and the hands on the knees and the ears, steadily, as he'd looked at the square behind the big loader's ear on the pad at Ithe.

Then he leaned out from the hull and put the end of his sausage on the sacks by Doc's boot, where Doc would find it.

"Right," he said.

He sat back. Eli didn't know what he'd said it to.

Seffa had her eyes shut. Her hands were under her arms again, in the cold, and she sat with her back to the iron and her bag on her knees, and the lamp swung, and the shadows of the sacks went up the hull and came down.

On his bag, asleep, Doc's ears came up. Both together, slowly, turning toward the bulkhead where the men were playing at their game with the cup. Then the rest of his face went after them, a little, with its eyes still shut, and he said something in his sleep that might have been a number, and settled.
