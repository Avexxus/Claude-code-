# The Emperor's Son

## ONE · The Gift

The ground in the Scald stayed warm all night, and nobody down there had ever found out why.

Vosk walked up out of it with the dwarf's price in his head. Eleven hundred, she'd said, in marks, not imperial. She'd said it to the wall over his shoulder while she wiped her hands, and then she'd put two fingers on the back of his neck to show him where. Tomorrow night, she'd said, or it went to someone else. He had forty-one marks in a sock under his bunk. It had taken him nine years.

He had come off the evening shift and gone straight down to her, straight past the bell for the night count, and now he was heading back with the night gone, past the shut stalls and the sleepers on their mats, feet tucked into each other's coats for the company, under pipes that sweated a slick with a skin on it. The heat rose through the soles of his boots the whole way and into his shins, and his shins ached with it as they ached after a double shift, which was what he had ahead of him.

Nearer the Tower the air didn't sit right. It had a shine in it at head height. Bits of light drifted in the cross-streets, slow, none bigger than a seed, and they cast no shadow on anything they passed. A girl of about eight stood in the mouth of an alley with a net of fine wire on a pole, waiting for one to float close enough. Her hands were wrapped to the elbow in rag. She kept her eyes on the light. You didn't touch drift with skin. Everybody knew someone who had.

There were humans down here at this hour too, which was the joke of the place. Clean coats, soft shoes, standing with their foreheads to the old walls and their eyes shut, palms flat on the stone, waiting. Three of them in a row outside the bathhouse that had been dry since before Vosk was born. They paid orc children to mind their shoes. One of the children had already sold one of the shoes.

People who lived on the old ground got sick in ways the clinics up top had no words for. He passed a woman whose jaw had kept growing down one side until it had shut her eye. She was selling batteries off a blanket and she said good morning to him, though it wasn't morning yet, and he said it back. Two small boys sat on a step sharing a cake, and the nearer one held it in a hand where the fingers had grown together into one flat piece, and broke it on his knee, and gave the bigger half away.

He turned the corner into Pole Street and stopped, because everyone else had.

There were maybe forty orcs in the street, all of them on their knees with their faces turned to the wall, men and women, a few children, and not a sound out of any of them. The heat was worse here, and it blew in gusts, out of a doorway across the street where a curtain of plastic strips hung down.

He got down. He put his face to the wall like the rest and showed his hands.

Someone was walking up the line. He heard the boots stop and go on, stop and go on. Young men's boots, light, quick, and when they stopped beside him he saw from the corner of his eye a pair of shins in yard trousers, and a hand hanging loose with a cutter in it, the kind you used for strapping. The hand had wire wound round the knuckles.

"You're up late, Terrace," the young man said.

Vosk kept still. The young man smelled his collar, actually leaned down and smelled it, and the back of his neck came past the corner of Vosk's eye. Where the chip scar should have been, a pale ridge like anyone's, the skin had been opened and stitched shut again with the same wire, tight, in neat crosses, and the stitches wept. He laughed through his nose and moved on.

Behind the plastic strips, something big shifted its weight, and the floor told him about it.

They stayed on their knees until the boots had gone back inside, and a long while after. When people did start getting up, they did it one at a time and without looking at each other, and split off in different directions, and the woman next to Vosk, who was old and had burns up both hands to the wrist, stayed down longer, rocking. When she got up she put her thumb to the back of his neck, on his scar, and pressed. He did it back. Her scar was hot.

"Eating," she said. "He's eating tonight."

"I didn't see."

"No," she agreed.

She walked with him as far as the foot of the Long Stair, because it was on her way, and as they went she sang, not loud, the one about Hurrak leaving the yard. Vosk had heard it all his life and never the whole of it, from anyone. Hurrak walked out of the yard and the dogs didn't bark, and he walked past the gate and the gate didn't bite, and he walked out of the city and kept walking. In the version his mother had sung there was a part about him carrying his own name in his mouth like a stone so he wouldn't lose it, and the old woman left that part out, and he didn't ask her why.

At the bottom step she said, "Walk well," and headed off into the Scald with her burnt hands in her armpits.

---

The Long Stair was four thousand and some steps, and there was a reader at every landing. A disc of dark glass set in the wall at eye height for a human, at the chest for him. When you passed one, it looked. For a human, Vosk had been told, it was only a light, a name maybe, a soft chime. With an orc the chip in the neck woke up and answered it, and the answer went cold down both your arms into your hands, every hundred steps, to the top. You got used to that. The readers could also tell the chip to hurt you, and you didn't get used to that.

Halfway up, the stair left the old ground and passed through the sumps, where the city's water ran back down to be cleaned. After that the foundations, and then the underside of the mid-levels, where it was suddenly cooler and there were lights that worked, and grey walls with the Emperor's seal stamped on them at every landing, an oval with nothing in it.

On a landing in the foundations he stopped to get his breath and went over the price again. It came out the same as it had at the bottom. He stood there with it and thought about a window, and he'd stood too long. The reader on the wall had gone on looking. The cold came down his arms and at the wrists it turned round and started back up, and he tasted foil at the back of his jaw, faint. Two men in grey came down past him with their eyes on their slates and stepped round him. He got his feet going. On the next landing the reader looked and let him by, like all the rest.

The mid-levels were never fully asleep. The traffic lanes ran overhead in strings of light, three deep, the lowest close enough that you could hear the hulls tick as they cooled, and the feeds ran on every wall with the sound off. PLAGUE HOLD EXTENDED, THREE SYSTEMS. LEGION RECRUITMENT AT RECORD. TERRACE PROPERTY VALUES.

The ground up here was wrong too, only quieter about it. The floors had been laid level and leaned anyway, all of them in one direction, so that a drink spilled on the Spur ran off toward the Tower whichever way the street went. Plants in window boxes grew sideways. Dogs didn't last. Everybody had the headache. It sat behind the eyes and in the ears, all day, every day, and there was a stall on every landing selling a blue pill for it that never worked, and people bought the pill and complained to each other about it instead of saying good morning. Sometimes in the gutter water there was a thin smear of light that slid away from a straight stare, and the cleaners hosed it down the drains with everything else.

The flesh market at Ninth Spur was opening. Vosk cut through it, because it was quicker and the stalls were full of men too busy to see him. They were hosing down the slabs. Steam rose off the water. The cold-cases were going up on their trestles, and every case had a stamp on the end the size of a hand, black on white, CLONE STOCK — NO SOUL, with the grade under it. A woman in an apron was laying out tongues in rows on ice, and a man at the next stall was sorting a basket of eyes by colour, and a dwarf further down sold hands in pairs, matched, with a paper tag on each thumb. Vosk had worked a season here when he was small, carrying ice. He knew what an orc arm sold for, which was less than a goat's.

At the end of the row a man was shouting, "Real! Real, not clone, real!" and a young woman with a shopping bag stopped and asked him, quite politely, how she was meant to tell.

"Costs more, doesn't it," the man said.

She had a pound. He cut it from something under the counter, not from the cases, and wrapped it in a page of feed sheet, and she put it in her bag with the bread.

The lift at the Spur's head had a core, a grey lump the size of a loaf in a glass box, wet, with threads going out of it into the works, and when the lift was coming down the lump twitched, slightly, the way cut meat twitches on a block. Vosk went round it to the culvert.

From the culvert's mouth you could see the Tower, all of it, if you leaned back. You couldn't anywhere else in the mid-levels. It rose out of the middle of everything, out of the oldest ground, pale and seamless and so big that the clouds broke round it, and the lanes bent away from it in a wide circle, and every building in the city kept a mile back from its foot. The rich said it was warm on your face. They said it the way they said a good wine was warm. The Terrace was built on the first rise of land that faced it, and the houses there had their best windows turned to it, and people paid for those windows more than Vosk could count.

He leaned back and stared up at it, because it was there. A tall pale thing. He didn't feel anything.

---

The service gate for Ninth Terrace was a slot in a wall at the top of the culvert, one orc wide. Vosk was fourteen minutes past the count.

He knew it before he got to the slot, because the slot knew, and the chip knew, and it started before he could get his shoulder through. It always started in the jaw. It felt like biting on foil, and the foil spread into the bones of his face and down his neck, and from there it went into the long muscles of his back and pulled them all at once in the same direction. He hit the wall of the slot with his head. It never lasted long. When it let go he was on his knees in the slot with his mouth full of spit and his hands open and held out, as they taught you to hold them, and somewhere far off the gate said, in a pleasant voice, "Late. Logged."

He got up. He went through.

The Terrace was the other side of the slot, all at once, every time. Water ran down channels cut in the white stone. There were trees, real ones, with real dirt round their feet that orcs changed every month. The air was cool and smelled of cut grass. In the first-floor window of the third house, the Ilders', a boy of about nine was eating breakfast in a white shirt, and down on the wet lawn the family's yard orc, a grown man, was hopping. Every time a foot touched the grass it jerked up again as if the grass were a stove, and the other one came down and jerked, and he went on like that, faster, with his arms clamped to his sides and his face screwed shut. The boy had his spoon in one hand and his thumb on a fob in the other. He wasn't laughing. He was interested.

Vosk kept walking. He had his thumb on his own scar.

In the barracks under the long house, the others were already up and dressing. Somebody had kept him a bit of bread. He ate it sitting on his bunk.

He went out into the yard and stood under the third window from the end on the second floor, which had been the same window since he was twelve, found a bit of gravel at the edge of the path, and threw it.

It took three. The third rang off the glass, and the window came up, and Eli put his head and his bare shoulders out into the cold, with a crease down one cheek from the pillow and the hair flat on that side.

"It's the middle of the night."

"It's after the count."

"That's what I said." Eli folded his arms on the sill and put his chin on them. "Go on."

Vosk had had it ready since the foundations. He'd said it over on every landing after. Now it came out short.

"I want a loan."

"A what?"

"Eleven hundred. Marks."

Eli lifted his chin off his arms. He studied Vosk, the work canvas, the grit to the knees, and then he laughed.

"What for?"

Vosk put two fingers on the back of his own neck, where the dwarf had put hers.

Eli watched the fingers. "Does it hurt?"

"No."

"Then what's wrong with it?"

Vosk took the fingers down.

"There's a dwarf," he said.

"There's always a dwarf. Where?"

"Down."

"Down where?"

"The Scald."

Eli's face changed, up there in the window. "Huh," he said.

"Forty-one now," said Vosk. "Then a mark a month."

Eli's lips moved. He'd been bad at sums with the tutors and he was bad at them still, and he did this one on his fingers along the sill. "That's ninety years."

"Eighty-eight."

Eli put his face down on his arms and laughed into them until the sill shook. When he came up his eyes were wet. "You'll be dead."

Vosk waited.

"Don't be stupid. Keep your forty-one."

"A loan," Vosk said.

"Fine. A loan. When?"

"Tonight. After."

"After what?"

"Double."

"Why are you on a double?" Eli wasn't looking at him. He was looking over the barracks roof, at the culvert wall and whatever he could see past it from up there. "The Scald," he said. "Tonight."

"Yes."

"Leave it with me." The window came down, and came up again an inch. "Leave it with me," Eli said through the inch, and shut it.

Vosk stood in the yard. The cut on his forehead had opened again in the cold, and a line of it had run down into his eyebrow. He wiped it off with his wrist.

---

The steward was out on the barracks step with his slate, doing the yard by numbers. He didn't lift his head for it. He read a number and a job, and the job went into your neck as he said it, a warmth that turned you the right way, and you went. Vosk's number came near the end.

"Double," the steward said to the slate.

The warmth came, and stayed on past the word.

The trees were his. He'd had them nine years. Every house on Ninth had four, two at the front and two at the back, and the dirt round their feet went sour in a month and had to come out. The new came up the culvert in sacks on the backs of the Spur crews and was left stacked at the top of the slot, and Vosk dug the old out and put the new in, house by house, down one side of the Terrace and back up the other. The old came out grey, in clumps that broke like ash. The new came out of the sacks dark and wet and smelling of something he had no word for, and when he'd firmed it down round the trunk he kept his hands in it after the job was done, both of them, to the wrist.

That morning his neck turned him to the Ilders'.

He went in by the side and kept to the paving as far as the first tree, and after that there was nothing for it but the lawn. The grass was still wet. At the far end, on the path, the Ilders' man was raking, and when Vosk's boots went onto the grass he stopped raking and stood with the rake upright in both hands.

Vosk dug.

The boy came out onto the front step in his white shirt and a school coat, with a slate under his arm, to wait for whatever was coming down out of the lanes for him. He saw Vosk. He studied the boots on his lawn. He took the fob out of his coat pocket.

Vosk kept digging.

The boy pointed the fob at him the way you'd point at a dog you wanted to come, and pressed it. Nothing happened in Vosk's feet. At the far end of the lawn the Ilders' man went up off the path with both heels, came down, and went up again, the rake going one way and his arms the other.

The boy frowned at the fob. He pointed it at Vosk again, harder, with his arm out straight, and at the end of the lawn the Ilders' man went up and came down on his knees on the path and stayed down.

A flyer came out of the lanes and settled at the kerb. The boy put the fob away and got in.

The Ilders' man got up. He came along the paving with the rake and stopped by Vosk's tree, his boots an inch off the grass, and spoke in the orc talk without looking at him.

"You've been down."

"Pole Street."

"Eating?"

Vosk nodded.

"That's three nights." The man looked at his own boots, and the inch. "Their young one's getting the hang of it," he said. "Kid's fob. Does their own. His dad's does anybody's." He went back up the paving with the rake.

Vosk did the tree by the front step last, and quickly, and kept his eyes on the dirt.

---

The noon bell went for bread. In the barracks yard the two little ones from the end bunks were playing Hurrak. The smaller one was Hurrak, and the bigger one was everything else, the dogs first, barking, and then the gate. When Hurrak walked past, the gate bit him on the forearm and hung on, and Hurrak screamed and beat the gate about the head with both fists.

Vosk pulled the gate off by the collar.

"Gates don't bite that hard."

"This one does," said the gate.

Hurrak had stopped crying to look at the marks on his arm. He counted them. "Then what?"

"Then he walks out of the city."

"Then what?"

"Keeps walking."

"Where to?"

Vosk tore his bread and gave them half each.

"My mam says they caught him," said the gate, with its mouth full.

"Your mam wasn't there," said Vosk.

Inside, on his bunk, he got the sock out and tipped it onto the blanket. He counted it in fives. Forty-one. He put it back in the sock a mark at a time, knotted the sock, and sat with it in his lap.

Leave it with me.

He put it back under the bunk, and pushed it to the back against the wall with two fingers, and went out to the trees.

By the late afternoon he was on the far side, at the houses by the gate, with the barrow half full of grey. The gate said good afternoon to somebody. Eli came through it laughing, with a young man in a coat the colour of plums, and the young man saw Vosk on his knees at the tree and stopped and said something, and Eli laughed again and called across the channel.

"Tonight!"

He held up nine fingers and waggled them. Vosk's double ran to ten. The young man in the plum coat studied Vosk the whole time with his head on one side, the way people on the Spur studied a stall.

Vosk lifted a hand off the dirt.

Eli went off up the white path toward the culvert end, walking backwards for a few steps to wave. The young man went the other way. Vosk put his hand back in the dirt and found the root he'd been working round, and worked round it.

---

The steward woke Eli at a quarter past eleven that morning by standing at the foot of the bed until he woke.

"There's a woman at the door, sir."

Eli turned over. "So let her in."

"She won't come in."

"Then let her out."

The steward didn't smile. He held his slate flat against his chest with both hands. "She has something for you. She won't give it to me. She has been on the step since eight. She said not to wake you."

Eli came up on one elbow. "Since eight?"

"I offered her a chair."

He went down in his shirt and bare feet. She was standing on the step outside the open door, not leaning on anything, a woman of about fifty in a grey coat buttoned to the throat, with her hair cut short as a man's. The shoulders of the coat were wet. It hadn't rained on the Terrace. She had an envelope in one gloved hand, and when she saw him on the stairs she held it out, and went on holding it out while he crossed the hall.

"Who's it from?"

She waited until his fingers had closed on it. Then she let go, and turned, and went down the white path toward the gate.

"Thank you," Eli said to her back.

The envelope was heavy and pale and the front was blank. On the back, pressed into a disc of grey wax, was the seal off the stair landings. He had seen that oval ten thousand times, on landings and crates and the backs of buses.

"Will there be an answer, sir?" said the steward.

"She didn't say there was a question."

He carried it into the kitchen. The cook was at the hot-plate. He put the letter on the table, and stood the fruit bowl on it, and the cook watched him do it and turned back to her pan.

"Eggs," Eli said.

"It's gone eleven."

"Eggs," he said, and smiled at her, and she did eggs.

The steward came in after him and stood by the table while he ate. Eli talked with his mouth full.

"I need some money."

"Sir."

"Eleven hundred."

The steward's thumb was already moving on the slate. "Imperial."

"Marks."

The thumb stopped. Then it went on, and the steward turned the slate face in against his shirt to wait, and before he could lower it the slate chimed against his chest. He turned it out and read it.

"Your father's people," he said. He said it whenever the account paid, in the same voice, and Eli had stopped asking which people years ago.

"Tell them thank you."

"Sir."

In the hall the house bell rang. The steward went out to it, and left the door a hand's width open, and Eli, buttering, heard him.

"Ninth, the house." A pause. "Yes." A pause. "Eleven hundred." A longer one. "Marks, madam." Longer. "Eleven. One, one, and two noughts." A silence Eli got most of a slice buttered in. "Yes, madam."

Eli laughed into his toast. "One, one and two noughts," he told the cook. The cook didn't laugh.

The steward came back as far as the door. He stood there with the slate held flat in front of him, as though somebody had set a thing on it to be carried, and then he went up the stairs without a word.

---

Dace was waiting at the gate with his hands in his pockets, rocking heel to toe, in a coat the colour of plums. The gate looked at him and said, "Good afternoon, Dace," and let him out onto the ramp. It looked at Eli and said, "Good afternoon," and let him out after.

"Still?" said Dace.

"It's shy."

Dace asked every time.

The Vennicks were up on the Seventh, where the houses stood higher and further apart and the Tower filled the whole end of every street. Their roof was a garden. Long chairs were set out on it in a row, all turned the one way, and people lay in them with their faces up and their eyes shut, getting the warm.

Lisane got up out of hers when she saw him.

"You came."

"I always come."

"You do." She kissed his cheek and kept hold of his arm. "We've had an offer on the house."

"Don't take it. Where would we lie?" He was looking past her down the row. "Is that Ilder? Ilder!"

Ilder lifted a hand without opening his eyes.

Lisane let go of his arm.

Eli took the chair beside Ilder's and turned his face up like the rest. There it was, past the edge of the roof, pale, so close from up here that he could see nothing else. He shut his eyes. Dace said it was warm today, and somebody said it was always warm, and somebody else said not like today. Eli kept his face up and said it was very warm.

One of the Vennicks' men came along the row with a tray. He was in white to the gloves, and he bent low at each chair and held the tray down, so you didn't have to sit up. Eli sat up anyway. He took two of the little pastries and ate one and held the other out.

"Here."

The man looked at the pastry.

"Go on. They're good."

The man took it in his gloved fingers and held it.

"Eat it, then."

"They're not to eat on the roof," said Lisane, from her chair.

"I won't tell." Eli patted the man's arm, twice, above the glove. "Good man."

The man went on down the row with the tray in one hand and the pastry in the other.

"You'll spoil him," said Dace.

"They like it."

"How's your big one, anyway?"

"He was at my window this morning," Eli said. "Middle of the night. Throwing stones." He swung his legs round to face them. "Wants eleven hundred marks."

That got them. Dace took his feet down.

"For what?"

"A chip. There's a dwarf in the Scald who does them."

"Does what with them?" said Lisane.

"Better ones." Eli spread his hands. "A better chip."

"Better at what?"

"He didn't say." Eli grinned round at all of them. "He's going to pay me back. A mark a month."

Dace was quicker at sums than Eli. "That's—"

"Eighty-eight years," said Eli, flat and low, in Vosk's voice, and they laughed, all of them, Dace with his head right back. Eli laughed with them, and said it again, Eighty-eight, and they laughed harder.

Ilder laughed less. He had his glass standing on his chest, and he was rubbing his thumb over the heel of his other hand, where there was an old white mark, a ragged crescent. He'd had it as long as Eli had known him.

"Vosk," Ilder said. "I remember Vosk. He could dance."

"Vosk?" said Eli. "He's got feet like barrows."

Ilder smiled into his glass.

"Down the Scald," said Dace, "with eleven hundred on him. They'll have him on a slab by morning. Graded."

"They'll rob him first," said the girl at the end.

"They'll all rob you in the end," said Dace. "Give them a reason. A better chip—"

"Not Vosk."

"Why not Vosk?"

"Because it's Vosk." Eli took a glass off the next tray that came by. "And they won't have him on any slab. I'll be with him."

There was a pause down the row. Somebody opened their eyes.

"You," said Ilder.

"Me."

"You've not been below Ancel Row in your life."

"So I'll go below Ancel Row."

"You won't."

"Tonight," said Eli.

Ilder lifted his glass off his chest, and the others lifted theirs, the girl at the end, Dace, a boy Eli didn't know, all down the row. Lisane didn't have a glass. Eli lay back in the warm with his eyes shut and his own glass on his stomach.

"Show them, then," said Dace. "If you're going down there. Do the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing. Lift something."

They were all watching now, even the ones who hadn't been listening. Eli looked round for something to lift. There was an empty chair at the end of the row. He put his hand out at it.

It was easy. It was always easy. He thought about the chair, its four feet on the tiles, and waited for the give.

The chair sat there.

Somebody giggled. Dace said, "Ooh."

"I've had wine," said Eli, and lay back down, and they laughed at that too. The Vennicks' man was coming back up the row with the tray, and Eli flicked two fingers at it as it passed, without looking, the way he'd flick a crumb. The tray jumped in the man's hand. Glasses danced. He caught it to his chest with both arms, elbows clamped in, eyes screwed shut, and stood there in his white gloves holding on to it while it rattled, and the whole row laughed, louder than for anything yet.

"Sorry!" said Eli, laughing. "Sorry. Good man."

The talk went on to something else.

When he left, Lisane walked him down. On the stair she stopped him with her hand flat on his chest.

"Eli. It's the last one."

"The last what?"

"Of these." She pointed up, at the roof. "We go down at the end of the month."

"Down?"

"Down," she said.

"Then come tonight!" He took the hand off his chest and held it. "Come with us. See what it's like. I'll look after you."

She looked at him a while. Then she took her hand back, and said have a good time, and went up.

The Vennicks' man was at the foot of the stair with the tray under his arm. He still had the pastry, flat now, in a smear of cream on the white glove. He opened the door for Eli and stood back from it.

---

Teo's house was the narrow one at the culvert end of Ninth, three floors and a bare roof, and every window in it looked at the culvert wall, which was why it was cheap. Teo's mother kept it on what the big houses paid her to teach their children to read. She'd taught Eli to read in it, at the kitchen table, with Teo kicking him underneath.

She opened the door with chalk on her hands.

"He's upstairs," she said. "He's had a day."

"Haven't we all," said Eli, and took the stairs two at a time.

The trunk was in the middle of Teo's floor with its lid back. It had been packed in the spring. Teo was sitting beside it in his socks, taking things out.

He took out a book, and got up, and put the book on the shelf, in the clean place in the dust where it had stood till the spring, and sat down again and took out the next thing. A cup with a chip off the handle. There was a clean ring on the windowsill, and he stood the cup in the ring.

The slate lay on the bed. Eli picked it up. PLAGUE HOLD EXTENDED, THREE SYSTEMS, and a list of the three under it, and one line, further down, that Teo had marked.

"Oh, Teo."

"Mm."

"That's rotten. That's really rotten." Eli sat down on the bed, on a pile of folded shirts. "They'll lift it."

"They said that in the spring."

"They'll lift it by winter. And you'll go, and it'll be awful here without you, and you'll write and tell me about the food."

Teo took a small grey stone out of the trunk, turned it over, and put it on the sill beside the cup, in a clean place the size of a stone.

"They'd given me a room," he said. "There was a picture of it. The window was on the water, and you could—"

"Listen. Tonight."

Teo turned the stone round so it sat the way it had sat before.

"Come to mine at nine," Eli said. "We're going down."

Teo looked up.

"The Scald. You, me and Vosk."

"Why?"

"I'll tell you at mine."

"Why not now?"

"It's a surprise."

"For who?"

Eli laughed and got up off the shirts, and some of them slid to the floor. "Nine," he said. "Wear boots."

On the stairs he heard Teo get up behind him, and pick the shirts up, and fold them again.

---

When he got home the cook had moved the fruit bowl to wipe the table, and the letter lay out on the wet wood by itself, the grey wax up. She saw him see it.

"It'll mark," she said.

He put the bowl back on it.

The steward was in the hall when he came out, with a purse on the hall table in front of him and his hand flat beside it, not on it.

"A man brought it at four, sir. He waited."

"They all wait today." Eli picked it up. The clasp bit him, and he laughed and sucked the finger. "Vosk's lates. Take them off."

"Sir."

"Both of them. He was with me."

The steward had the slate up. He didn't write anything on it. "They're logged, sir. Lates go up."

"Up where?"

The steward lifted his eyes from the slate to Eli's face, and then a little past it, to the stair, and the house above the stair, and whatever was above the house.

"Up," he said.

"Then bring them down."

The steward wrote. He held the slate a moment, and it chimed, the same small chime as the morning, and he turned it face in against his shirt.

"Off, sir."

"There," said Eli. "Was that hard?" He went up two stairs and turned round on the third. "Where's my good coat? The long one."

"Pressed, sir. In your room." The steward looked at the purse in Eli's hand, and at Eli. "For the Spur, sir?"

"Further."

The steward said nothing to that. When Eli came down again in the coat with the collar up and the purse in his hand, the steward was still in the hall, and he looked at the coat from its hem to its collar, the whole length of it, as he looked over a bill before it went up.

"Very good, sir," he said.

---

The purse was on the kitchen table next to the fruit bowl, where he could see it while he talked.

"Eleven hundred," he said. "In marks."

Teo was sitting on the counter by the cold-box with his heels against the doors and his slate on his knee, eating an apple from the bottom up, which was how he ate them. With his other thumbnail he was working at the skin down the side of his thumb. He did that when he read, and the thumb was always half healed.

"From who?"

"The account." Eli turned the purse over. It was heavier than he'd expected money to be. Money was a number on a slate. "Asked at breakfast. It was back before the steward had his slate down. Somebody rang the house to check. A woman." Eli laughed. "No one ever rings him. He's been in a mood all day."

"What did she want?"

"To check. I just said."

Teo gave the purse a long look and went back to the slate.

The account had come with the house, and the house had come with Eli, before he could remember. It had paid for both his flyers and for the window he'd put a chair through at fifteen, and it never asked what anything was for.

"He asked you for money," Teo said.

"A loan."

"A loan." Teo turned the apple in his fingers. "He didn't ask you to come."

"He didn't have to." Eli got up, crossed to the window and came back. "On his own? At night, down there, with that on him?"

Teo didn't say anything.

"They'd have the boots off him before the second landing. He's never had eleven hundred of anything. He'd carry it in his hand." Eli sat down again. "He would. In his hand."

Teo finished the apple and glanced round for somewhere to put the core. Eli flicked two fingers at the yard door without looking round. The core left Teo's hand and floated across the kitchen, not fast, to the open yard door, and dropped on the step inside it, where somebody would sweep it.

Teo considered his empty hand. He put the thumb in his mouth. He had none of it himself. The tutors had sat him in front of a feather on a plate when he was nine, and given up by lunch, and he'd gone home pleased.

On the wall behind him the feed was running with the sound off. Grey men at a grey table. The ribbon underneath said ELVEN DELEGATION RECALLED, then THE QUEEN DECLINES TO COMMENT, and a line about rates.

The letter was under the fruit bowl.

"Are you going to open that," said Teo.

"Tomorrow." Eli slid the purse into the inside pocket of his coat, and patted it. "Whoever they are, they've had twenty-four years. They can—"

There was a knock on the yard door, two short, which was Vosk's knock, and Eli was across the kitchen before the second one had finished.

Vosk filled the door. He ducked coming through it, though the top of his head cleared the frame by an inch. He still wore his work clothes, the grey canvas, with dirt dried to the knees, and he had a cut on his forehead that Eli hadn't noticed that morning, scabbed, with grit in it.

"What happened to your head?"

"Wall."

"Sit down. You haven't eaten. There's the end of the bird. Teo, move."

"I'm all right," Vosk said.

"Sit down."

Vosk sat on the step inside the yard door, which was where he sat. The apple core was by his boot, and he moved the boot. Teo slid down off the counter. Eli got the end of the bird out of the cold-box and put the plate down on the step beside Vosk, and the fork on the plate, and a cloth after, because he was always forgetting the cloth. He crouched down facing him with his forearms on his knees.

"Your lates are off," he said. "Both of them. I told the steward you were with me."

Vosk put two fingers up to the cut on his forehead and took them down again.

"And I've got it." Eli patted the coat. "I'll carry it."

Vosk's eyes went to him, and past him, to Teo.

"It's quicker on my own," Vosk said.

"So we'll be quick."

"I know the way. I go down there."

"I know you go down there. That's how you got your head opened." Eli reached out and put his hand on the back of Vosk's neck, over the scar, and gave it a squeeze and a little shake. The scar was warm under his palm, a raised ridge. "I'm not handing eleven hundred marks to a dwarf I've never met. Would you? And nobody's putting a knife in anybody with me stood there." He let go and stood up. "They know what a Terrace coat is."

Vosk had picked up the fork. He turned it over. He put it down on the plate again, very square, next to the bird.

"Eli," he said. "Not in the coat."

"Eat your bird."

Vosk ate the bird. He ate it slowly, and near the end of it he stopped chewing, and his head went back against the door frame, and he was asleep, with the fork still in his hand and his mouth open.

Eli took the fork out of his hand. He fetched his second-best coat from the hall and put it over him, and it covered about half of him.

Teo checked the clock on his slate and glanced at Eli.

"So she waits," Eli said. He sat down on the kitchen floor with his back to the cold-box, where he could see Vosk's face. "And I'm not walking four thousand steps. Find us a way down that isn't the stair."

Teo turned the slate round. He'd had it up since the purse came out: the sump crews' rota, two weeks of it, and a crews' shaft off the Spur with a cage in it, a change at half past eleven, and nobody due in the shaft after that until two.

Eli didn't look at it. "Good," he said.

---

They left the house just after eleven.

## TWO · The Scald

Going down the white path they passed the Ilders'. The Ilders' man was out in the dark, sweeping round the front step, and he was standing on the paving to do it, right at the edge, with his boots an inch off the grass. Eli nodded to him. The man didn't nod back, which was the Ilders all over; they didn't train them. The youngest had been given a fob in the spring, and most mornings you could hear him at it from the kitchen, slap, slap on the wet lawn.

At the arch the glass looked at Eli and said, "Good evening," and let him out onto the public ramp. It said, "Good evening, Teo," to Teo.

Vosk wasn't with them. The front gate didn't read orcs. It bit them.

"That's stupid," Eli had said in the yard. "Come through with us. I'll tell it." Vosk had said, "It bites," and gone off down the side of the house toward the culvert. He'd folded the second-best coat in four and left it on the step.

They found him at the bottom of the public ramp a few minutes later, standing still in the shadow of a pillar, with spit drying on his chin and his hands where people could see them.

"See," said Eli. "No trouble." He clapped him on the arm.

Vosk wiped his chin on his wrist. The slot didn't like orcs out after the count.

The mid-levels at night were louder than by day. Noise poured down from the lanes overhead and up from the streets underneath, and there were so many feeds you could have read by them. Every human face that passed under a reader got its light. Eli had stopped seeing that at about sixteen. He walked down the middle of the walkway with his coat open and the purse knocking on his ribs, and people stepped round him, and he let them.

On the corner of Ancel Row the man on the door of the club with the red lamps saw him coming and unhooked the rope.

"Mr Eli."

"Not tonight." Eli didn't slow down. "We're going down."

The doorman looked at Teo, and at Vosk behind Teo, and at the coat. He hooked the rope back on its post.

"Down, sir."

"Down," said Eli, and laughed, and walked on. Behind him he heard the doorman say it again to somebody inside, and the somebody laugh.

Past Ancel Row the Spur got narrower and darker, and the walkway leaned. The flesh market was shut, the cold-cases chained under tarps, and the gutters still ran pink from the evening hosing. At the Spur's head the lift's core sat in its glass box and twitched when a car went down. Vosk went round it. Eli bent to the glass to look at it, and Teo said, "Eli," and he came away.

Teo took them past the lift to a pump house with its door propped open on a brick. Inside was a square hole in the floor with a rail round it, and a cable going down into the hole, black with oil, humming.

Vosk stood just inside the door with his back to the wall, where he could see the street. Eli walked once round the rail and came back to him.

"You've got your face on," he said. "Like you're waiting for the count."

Vosk didn't answer. Out in the street a flyer went over low, and the light off it came in through the door and went across the floor and up the wall and was gone.

At half past the cable stopped humming. Something came up the hole, slowly, ticking, and the top of a cage rose out of it, and then the cage, with five orcs in it in waders wet to the chest and a human with a slate on a cord round his neck. The human saw Eli. He saw the coat. He stood in the cage with the gate half open and looked at Teo, and at Teo's slate, and at the coat again, and then at nothing in particular.

"Gentlemen," he said, and came out round them, and went out of the door.

The orcs came after him one at a time. They smelled of the sumps. The last of them was old, with grey in the stubble on his head, and he stopped in front of Vosk with the water running out of the tops of his waders onto the floor and said something low in the orc talk. Vosk said something back, one word. The old one looked at Eli a while, from the boots up. Then he went out.

"What did he say?"

"Shift's over," said Vosk.

Teo got into the cage first, without a word.

It was a long way. The cage went down grinding, and every yard of it was warmer than the last, and after a while Eli stopped trying to guess how far. Partway down, the shaft opened on one side onto the sumps: black water sliding past in a channel as wide as a street, with lamps on the far wall laying long smears on it, and a smell of shut cellars.

There the cage stopped.

It swung a little and was still. The cable ticked overhead. The water went past.

"Teo."

"It does that." Teo had the slate up. "It says here it does that."

"For how long?"

Teo turned the slate toward the lamps and read on.

Eli laughed. It went out over the water and nothing came back. He turned round and put his back to the mesh. Vosk was at the far side of the cage with one hand on the rail, and the light off the water moved on his face.

"Give it here," Vosk said.

"Give what?"

"The purse. Before we're down."

"I've got it."

"I'll carry it."

"You'll carry it in your hand." Eli patted the coat over it. "Anyway it's in here. Nobody's going near this."

Vosk kept the hand out. It had the tree dirt still in the creases of the knuckles. After a while he put it back on the rail.

"Forty-one's under my bunk," he said. "At the back. Against the wall."

"I don't want your forty-one."

"It's the first of it."

"Eighty-eight years," said Eli, flat and low, in Vosk's own voice, and grinned at him.

Teo looked up from the slate.

Vosk looked at Eli. Then he turned his head and watched the water go by, and the cage jerked and dropped a foot and went on down.

It stopped at last at a grille where the cable ended. The last of it was a ladder. The rungs were slick and warm. Eli's forearms had started to burn. Below him Teo's breathing came up the shaft, slow and even. Above him Vosk made no sound at all.

---

At the bottom of the shaft the floor was the wrong size.

It was a street. It had a gutter, and a curb, and on the other side another curb. But the curb on this side came up to Eli's chest, and the far one was a long way off, so far that the lamps strung between them sagged in the middle, and the street between had been filled in with poured stuff to make a floor at a sensible height, and people had built shacks on it, and a ramp went up over the curb made of pallets and somebody's door.

Eli stood with his hands on the curb. The stone was smooth where people had held it getting up the ramp. Teo was already at the top.

A boy was sitting on the curb beside the ramp with his knees up, eating something out of a paper. He watched Vosk come out of the shaft, and Teo, and Eli. He looked at Eli's coat the longest. Then he folded the paper over what was left and put it inside his shirt, and got up and went off along the top of the curb, not hurrying, and dropped down the far side into the street.

"Hello," Eli said, after him.

The Scald was hot. Eli had known it would be. He hadn't known the heat would have a direction. It came from ahead and above, out of the dark past the shack roofs, and it pushed on his face and his chest, and the further they went the harder it pushed. He took his coat off and carried it, and put it back on because of the purse. Sweat ran into the small of his back. The air tasted of metal and burning fat.

There were a lot of people, orcs mostly. They sat in the shack doors and cooked on drums in the street and lay on mats along the foot of the curb, and they moved round Eli without touching him, a gap opening half a step before he got there and closing up behind. A woman in a doorway saw him and let her curtain down. Somewhere ahead of them, toward the heat, somebody whistled two notes, and further off somebody else whistled them back.

Eli whistled them back too. Nobody laughed.

"Polite, aren't they," he said to Teo. Teo didn't answer.

Vosk walked ahead. He walked differently down here. Looser. His hands hung at his sides. A big orc with a split ear stepped into his way and the two of them put their thumbs to each other's necks and spoke low in the orc talk, and the other one laughed and eyed Eli's coat past Vosk's shoulder, and Eli, and added a few words, and Vosk said, "No," sharply.

"What did he say?"

"Nothing."

"He said something."

"He asked was you mine."

Eli laughed. "What did you say?"

Vosk walked faster.

The street ran out at a cut in the ground. It ran away on both sides, forty feet down, maybe more, and the bottom of it wasn't dark but lit from somewhere under it, a dull white that moved, and drift rose out of it in ones and twos. Along the edge children stood with nets on long poles, wrapped to the elbow in rag.

One of them, a girl, was working a light that wouldn't come. It hung a yard past the rim of her net, turning, and every time she reached the pole out after it the air off the cut pushed it back, and she leaned out further, with her rag feet on the lip of the drop.

Eli flicked two fingers at it on his way past.

The light slid sideways into her net and lay there.

She didn't pull the net in. She looked at it, and back along the pole to her own wrapped hands, and then at Eli's fingers, and at his coat. She came off the edge a step at a time with the pole held out level between them. Then she turned and ran along the rim toward the bridge, with the net up over her head and the light in it.

"Don't do that," said Vosk. "Not down here."

"She'd have been there all night."

Vosk watched her go. She was over the bridge already, a small dark shape and a light bobbing on a pole, and down into whatever was on the far side of it.

The bridge was one long curved girder with a deck laid on top, ribbed like a spine, dark grey going brown, and it spanned the cut in one piece. Eli put his hand on it as he stepped up, stopped, and put his hand back.

It was warm. Warm like a horse.

"Don't touch it," said Vosk.

---

Hookline was the market the bridge ran down into, and it was called that for the hooks: the whole low ceiling hung with them on chains, thousands, and goods on half of them. Coats. Lamps. Pots. A whole side of something red and white, sawn down the middle, turning slowly in the heat rising off the cut, with a paper tag on the shank, blank, not even a grade. Across the floor from the bridge end stood an old stone arch with plastic strips hung across it, and the heat poured out of there harder than anywhere. Music played from three places at once. A dwarf woman sold soup out of a hot-box, with a ladle as long as her arm, and the soup smelled good, and Eli slowed for it.

Teo stepped in close beside him.

"Take the coat off," he said. "Carry it inside out."

"It's got the purse in it."

"I know what's in it." Teo kept his head still. "Every stall, Eli. Nobody's looking at him. They're looking at the coat."

Eli turned to see. A man at a stall of pans studied his pans. Two orc women by a hook of lamps looked at each other. Up on a crate by the arch, the net girl sat with her pole across her knees.

"It's a good coat," Eli said, and put his arm round Teo's shoulders and gave him a shake, and let him go, and went to the soup.

"Three," he said.

The dwarf woman looked at him. She looked past him at Vosk. She ladled out three cups, slowly, without spilling, and set them on the edge of the hot-box in a row. Eli went through his trouser pockets and found a note, imperial, folded small, and put it down beside the cups.

"Keep it."

She picked the note up and held it. She didn't put it away. Her eyes went past him across the floor, and Eli looked where she was looking, and the girl had got up off the crate by the arch and was slipping in through the plastic strips, not hurrying.

He gave Teo a cup. Teo held it. He held one out to Vosk, and Vosk looked at it and didn't take it, and Eli drank his own standing there, and it burnt his tongue, and it was good.

"Vosk," he said, loud, over the music. "Which way?"

Vosk didn't look round. He turned off into an alley halfway along the market's side wall, between two stalls. Eli put the third cup down on the corner of a stall of lamps, and they followed him.

---

The dwarf was called Velk, or that was what it said on the shutter of the shop she worked out of the back of, which had sold water once and still had the tanks. She was short even for a dwarf and nearly as broad as she was tall, and she had a scar that ran from her hairline down through one eyebrow and on down her cheek, and the eye under it was a pale grey and stayed still when the other one moved.

She looked at Eli. At Teo. At Vosk.

"You said you'd come on your own," she said.

"He's paying," said Vosk.

"He's paying." She ran her eye over Eli again, his coat, his hands, his boots. "Course he is. You're late." She jerked her head at the shutter. "It's hot. I told you it was hot."

"It's boiling." Eli flapped his coat.

"Come in. Don't touch anything."

The back of the shop was a long room with a low ceiling and shelves on every wall, and on the shelves were things Eli had no names for. A grey cube that sat at a tilt and, while he watched, tilted the other way. A coin standing on its edge on a saucer.

Eli bent to the coin. He laid it down flat on the saucer without touching it, a little push with nothing at all, and stood it back up on its edge, and looked round at Teo to see if he'd seen.

"Don't," said Velk. She was at a basin in the corner with her back to him, washing her hands, fast, with a brush, up to the elbows, and her live eye was on him in the dark side of the tank.

"I didn't touch it."

"Don't."

"Where's all this from?"

"Out." She shook the water off. "Depot takes a third now. A third. Then his cut. And whatever he sends up to whoever." She dried her arms.

"Whose cut?" said Teo.

Velk pulled a heavy chair into the light, high-backed, with straps on the arms, and nodded at it, and Vosk sat down in it and put his own arms in the straps.

Eli took out the purse.

She counted it on the table in stacks of fifty, wetting her thumb, so fast that Eli lost the count at the second stack, and swept most of it into a tin with a mark scratched in the lid and the rest into a drawer, and held her hand out again.

"And two," she said.

"It was eleven."

"It was eleven last night." She kept the hand out. "Tonight you're late, and it's hot, and there's three of you."

"I haven't got two."

Her live eye went to his wrist.

Eli unbuckled the watch and put it in her hand. A birthday present. It kept the time on four worlds he'd never been to and it had a little moon on it that went round. She put it in her apron without a glance.

In the far corner, behind the tanks, a thin dwarf boy of maybe ten sat on a stool with a cup. Eli hadn't seen him. The boy's eyes went to Eli's bare wrist. To the pocket of Eli's coat where the purse had been, and Eli's boots, and Eli. He put down the cup and slipped out through a curtain at the back.

"Where's he going?" said Teo.

Velk answered without turning. "Tax." She pulled the lamp down on its chain. "Hold his head."

Eli stood there.

"His head. Forward. Chin down. Unless you'd rather do the knife."

Eli held his head.

Up close, Vosk's hair smelled of the barracks soap, and the back of his neck was hot, and the scar was there under Eli's fingers where it had always been, a pale ridge as long as a thumb. He'd touched it a thousand times. Velk wiped it with a cold pad and laid her tools out on a cloth, small bright things, picked one, put the point of it in the old scar at the top end and drew it down.

Vosk's whole body went tight in the chair.

There was more blood than Eli had expected, and it was darker. It ran down into the collar of Vosk's work shirt and Velk let it run. She opened the cut with two small hooks and held it, and Eli could see down into it, past the red, to a grey, shining thing with threads running out from it into the meat like roots. She took hold of it with narrow pliers and began to pull, slowly, turning, and the threads came out one at a time with a sound like wet paper, and Vosk said three words through his teeth in the orc talk, the same three over and over, and his hands in the straps opened and shut.

"Talk to him," said Velk. "If you're going to stand there."

"It's all right," said Eli. "It's all right. You're doing well. You're doing so well."

The thing came free, the size of a fingernail, with its threads trailing, and she dropped it in a dish, and in the dish it moved. Once, a small curl of the threads.

Teo had gone to stand by the door, watching the shutter. Out in the market, one of the three musics had stopped.

The new one came out of a sealed packet. Bigger than the old one, darker, ridged on one side, and someone had taken a file to the mark on it, not well enough. Eli could make out part of a word. LEGION.

"Soldier's," said Velk. "Leash is out."

She pressed it home with her thumb, and took the thumb away fast and shook it, and it didn't take. The threads on it moved, and stopped, and lay down flat in the wet. She said a short word in dwarven and opened the cut wider with the hooks, and Vosk's back came up off the chair, and Eli held his head down with both hands and all his weight on them. She pushed it in again and held it, with her lips pulled back off her teeth. This time the threads went, feeling their way down into him, and under Eli's fingers the skin round the cut went hot, and hotter, so that he nearly let go.

She glanced at Eli with her live eye. "You know what you've—"

"Yes," said Eli.

She watched him, and closed the cut with glue and a strip that pulled tight as it dried, and undid the straps, all of it fast. There was a blister coming up white on the ball of her thumb. Vosk stayed in the chair. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his head hanging, breathing hard, and blood dripped off the end of his nose onto the floor between his boots.

He lifted his head.

His eyes passed over all of them. He turned his face, slowly, to the far wall of the shop, and stared at it.

"What's that," he said.

His voice was strange. Eli crouched down to him. "What's what?"

"That." Vosk had his eyes wide open. "It's. Up. It's right there."

"Tower," said Velk. She was rolling her tools up in their cloth. "Everybody, first time. Sun in the next room."

Eli turned to the wall. Shelves.

Vosk put his hand up toward the wall with the palm out and the fingers spread, and held it there. His head came round, slowly, and down, toward the floor and the front of the shop and the market beyond it.

"And them," he said. "All of them." He shut his eyes. "It's loud."

"It's lit," said Velk. She tied the roll. "It'll settle." She put the roll under her arm. "Or it won't."

"Is it hot?" Eli said. "Vosk. Does it hurt?"

Vosk opened his eyes and found him. He studied Eli's face. He looked down at his hand, and he closed the hand.

"No," he said.

Eli let his breath go. He put his hand on Vosk's knee.

Teo crossed the room. He took hold of Eli's arm above the elbow, not hard. His face was very calm.

"Get him up," he said. "Now."

Vosk's hands went to the arms of the chair.

"He's just had his neck opened." Eli shook Teo's hand off, not unkindly, and kept his own where it was. "Give him a minute. Sit, Vosk. Sit."

Vosk sat.

Teo held Eli's eye, and went back to the door, and stood there with his arms folded, and said no more. Out in the market a second music stopped.

Velk had the watch out of her apron. She checked it once, and put it back. She went to the curtain at the back and put her face to the gap at its edge, and listened, and let it fall.

"Out the front," she said. "The back's mine."

She left by the back, with the tin under her arm. Through the curtain, already going, she said, "And I didn't fit it."

Vosk was standing. Eli hadn't seen him get up. He stood straight with his head tilted, and his face was turned toward the front of the shop, and the blood was drying on his lip, and he was listening.

"It's gone quiet," Vosk said.

---

It had. Eli hadn't noticed the market's noise until there wasn't any. No music. No one selling soup. The heat was still there, pushing through the shutter at the front, and a smell had come into it, hot metal and a sweetness behind the metal that turned his stomach.

They went out the front.

The alley was empty. The market at the end of it was not. Everybody was still there, the stallholders, the pan man, the two orc women, the soup woman; they were all on their knees, every one, facing the nearest wall. Every head stayed down. On the hooks overhead the meat turned in the heat. The cup Eli had left was still on the corner of the lamp stall, with a skin on it.

The alley let out halfway along the market's side wall. The bridge end was to their left, thirty steps along the wall, back where they'd come in. The arch was off to the right and across the floor, past the soup stall and the hanging meat, with its plastic strips.

"Bridge," said Teo, very low. "Walk. Don't run."

Eli walked. Teo had him by the sleeve. Vosk was a step behind. They moved along the wall past all the bowed heads toward the bridge end, and out of the arch, through the plastic strips, young men began to come.

Six. Eight. Orcs, young, light on their feet, in yard trousers, with wire wound round their knuckles and their necks opened and stitched with wire. They took their time. They crossed the market floor between the kneeling people, spread out between Eli and the bridge, and stopped. The dwarf boy from the shop was with them, at the back. He pointed at Eli, once, and one of the young men paid him, and he ran off between the stalls. The girl with the net was there too, by the arch, with her pole on her shoulder and the light gone out of it.

One of them, not the biggest, smiled at Vosk and touched his own stitched neck and pointed at Vosk's, where the new strip was.

"Lit you up like a lamp, Terrace," he said. His eyes went to Eli's coat, and back. "And I hear the coat does tricks."

"It's paid for," said Eli. His voice came out clear and reasonable, the voice he used with the house steward. "If there's some sort of fee, I'm happy to—"

The smiling one glanced at him and turned back to Vosk.

The plastic strips moved.

The heat came first. Eli felt it on his face and the backs of his hands, a hard dry push that dried the wet out of his eyes, and every bowed head in the market went lower, and on the hooks overhead the fat on the sawn carcass started to run. The strips parted, and something came through the arch that had to turn its shoulders to fit.

He was the shape of an orc, the heavy shoulders and the long arms, but half again as tall as Vosk and twice as broad, and the light rising off the cut lit him from underneath. The chest and arms and the slabs of the shoulders were made of pieces, sewn edge to edge with wire, and the pieces didn't match. A hand's width of thick grey hide ran into something finer and paler, and that ran into hide again. One patch on his upper arm had an old burn on it that had healed on somebody else. The seams stood up in dark ridges, and some of them wept. There were plates of dull metal let into him, one over the left side of the ribs and one down the length of a forearm, riveted at the rims, and the meat had grown up round the rivets. Dace was under the middle lantern in the plum coat with three people Eli half knew, and he saw Eli and put his arms out wide.

"He lives!"

People turned round.

Dace had him by both shoulders. He turned Eli's face to the lantern with a finger under the chin, the swelling gone dark down one side of it. "Oh, look at you. You went. Ilder! He went!"

"We went," Eli said.

"And?"

"We had soup."

Dace let go of him to tell the others. "Soup."

Through the open doors, on the wall of the Ilders' front room, a feed was running with the sound off. Grey men at a grey table again, or the same ones. The ribbon underneath said UNREST IN THE SCALD. It said TERRORIST CELL SUSPECTED. It said LEGION, and a line of figures, and went round again.

Dace read it out. "'Terrorist cell.'" He laughed into his glass. "Was it you, Eli? Was it your big one?" He turned to the people with him. "Eli's orc. He's huge. He wanted a better chip, and Eli's paid for it, and he's going to pay him back"—Dace put a hand flat on Eli's chest and held him there, for the line—"a mark a month."

A girl Eli didn't know did the sum on her fingers, and got lost, and looked up.

"Eighty-eight years," said Dace, flat and low, in the voice Eli had done on the roof.

They laughed. The girl laughed with her hand over her mouth. Dace waited for Eli to laugh, and when he didn't Dace laughed harder, at that.

Lisane wasn't there. Nobody said where the Vennicks were.

Ilder had come out of the house behind them with a glass in one hand and the other hand in his pocket. He hung back from the group, and he didn't laugh this time either. His eyes were on Eli's face, on the dark side of it.

"Did he get it?" Ilder said. "His chip."

"He got it."

"Good."

Ilder took the hand out of his pocket to change his glass over, and there was the mark on the heel of it, white in the lantern light, the ragged crescent, and he rubbed his thumb across it.

"Ilder," Eli said.

"Mm."

"When we were small." He stopped. The piano went up and came down. "Your yard."

Ilder waited, rubbing the mark.

"Do the voice," Dace said, and got hold of Eli's sleeve. "Eli. Do it for them, you do it better than me."

Eli pulled the sleeve out of his hand. Dace's elbow went, and his drink went down his own front, and somebody said oh.

Eli bent and put his glass down on the grass, carefully, upright, and walked off the lawn.

The Ilders' man stood where he had stood all night, with the tray, and his eyes were on the glass, out on the grass by itself in the middle of the lawn, three steps off the stone, with the lantern light in it.

Ilder caught him up where the hedge ended, on the white path, with his own glass still in his hand.

"My yard," Ilder said.

Eli stopped.

Ilder held up the other hand with the heel of it turned out, so the crescent caught what light came over the hedge. "You did that." He said it as he said everything, without any heat. "I'd had my father's fob out of his desk. Your one was on the grass, and I had him going a long time."

"Vosk."

"Till he wet himself. Everybody laughing. Me too. And you came off the step and got my hand in your mouth, and you wouldn't let go. My father had to put his thumb in your jaw."

"I don't remember."

"You were smaller." Ilder looked at the mark. "Every time I pick up a glass."

"He remembered," Eli said.

"Did he." Ilder rubbed it. "How is he? You came back with a face."

"He's dead."

Ilder stood there with the glass. Up on the lawn somebody shrieked and somebody else said no, no, in the voice for a good story. Ilder looked down into the glass, and drank what was in it, all of it.

"Come back in," he said. "Dace'll stop."

Eli walked on down the path. Behind him the piano started the fast piece again from the beginning.

---

In the morning the count had one more in it.

He heard it from the bed. The bell, and the steward's voice, and a number, and somebody answered, and a number, and somebody answered. Near the end there was a number, and a voice answered it that Eli didn't know, young, too loud, saying the whole of the number back, every figure of it.

"Trees," said the steward.

Eli went to the window. The new one was crossing the yard toward the slot, fast, nearly running, with his head down. He was small for an orc, narrow through the back, and at the nape of his neck there was a square of white paper stuck over the scar, and it had come up at one corner.

Later he was at Eli's front tree with the barrow. Eli stood on the path and watched him dig. The old dirt came out grey and broke when it hit the barrow. The new one felt Eli there and stopped, and stood with the spade upright in both hands, and kept his eyes on the hole.

"Go on," Eli said.

The new one went on. He dug too fast and hit a root, and stood with it, and looked round for somebody to tell him.

Eli walked on down the white path.

The Ilders' man was sweeping the paving along the Ilders' front hedge, with his boots on the stone and an inch off the grass. Last night's lanterns were down and coiled on the step. The glass was gone off the lawn. He didn't look up when Eli came along the path, and Eli would have gone by him, and the man spoke to his boots, in the common talk, low.

"He's asking."

Eli stopped.

He went on sweeping. He swept up to where the hedge met the low wall of the water channel, and he put a hand in his pocket, and took it out, and set something down on the coping of the wall, flat, and took his hand away.

"Came up this morning," he said. "From Hookline. Dwarf paid her cut in it."

"Who's asking?"

He swept round the corner of the wall and along the far side of it, away. Halfway along he said two more words, not turning round, and the broom went on, and Eli didn't catch it until the man was nearly at the Ilders' side door. It had been "Walk well."

The watch was on the coping. The strap was done up, buckled, as though somebody had taken it off a wrist, and it was going. The four faces were all going, four times on four worlds he'd never been to, and the little moon was up in its window. Grey had got into the links. He picked at it with a nail, and it came out as dust and went on his fingers.

He put it on. He did the buckle up on the hole it had always been on.

All the rest of the day he checked it. He'd lift his wrist for the time and it would be there, and he'd look at it, and put his arm down, and in a while he'd lift it again.

---

In the evening he was in the barracks yard when the new one came back through the slot from the stack outside with a sack on each shoulder, and the bell for the night count had already gone. Eli heard the slot before he saw it. Something hit the inside of the white wall, hard, and a sound came from the gap that wasn't a word, and after it the pleasant voice said, "Late. Logged."

The new one came through the slot on his knees. The sacks had gone down either side of him and one had split, and the dark new dirt was running out of it across the white stone. He had his hands out in front of him, open, held up, and spit was coming out of the side of his mouth and he couldn't stop it. The paper had come off his neck. Under it the scar was new and pink, closed with a strip, and the strip had lifted at one end.

He saw Eli. He tried to get up, and his legs wouldn't do it.

Eli got down by him in the mouth of the slot. The disc was set in the stone over them, at the height of the new one's chest, and there was a light in it, low and steady, on the new one. Eli's face was a hand from the glass. It didn't come on for him. It stayed on the new one's neck until the new one got his hands down, and then it went out.

"It's all right," Eli said. "It's all right. Stay there."

The new one stayed there.

Eli went over to the barracks step, where the steward stood with the slate.

"Take it off. His late."

The steward's thumb stayed still on the slate. "Sir."

"Take it off. He's new. He wasn't to know."

He wrote, and held the slate, and it chimed, and he turned it face in against his shirt. "Off, sir."

Eli went back to the slot. The new one was on his feet now, scooping the spilled dirt back into the split sack with both hands, fast, getting all of it, and when most of it was in he got down and picked the last out of the cracks between the stones with his fingers.

"Leave it," Eli said. "Somebody'll sweep it."

The new one went on picking it out of the cracks.

In the kitchen the cook had her coat on to go. She stopped at the table on her way to the door and took a plum out of the bowl, a dark one with the bloom still on it, and turned it over, and put it back with the others.

"I never got plums," she said, to the bowl, and went.

Eli lifted his wrist and looked at the time.

---

He didn't sleep. He lay with his wrist up in the dark over his face and watched the little moon go over in its window, so slow you could only see it had moved if you looked away and back, and he looked away and back.

At the bell he came down with his boots in his hand.

The kitchen light was off.

There was grey in the window from the yard, enough to see the table by, and a man sitting at it, in Eli's chair, the one that faced the yard door, with his coat off and hung over the back of it and his sleeves pushed up, eating a plum. He was fifty or so, big through the chest and the shoulders, with grey hair cut close to his skull and a short grey beard, and the plum was nearly done. Three stones lay on the table already, in a row, and he set the fourth down beside them and took another plum from the bowl. The bowl sat where it always sat. The letter was under it, with the pale corner showing.

He glanced up at Eli in the doorway and smiled.

"Morning," he said.

Eli's boots came up against his chest, both of them, and he held them there. The yard door was behind the man, and past it, across the yard, the steward read a number off the slate at a dark window, and somebody answered. Eli's eyes went to the door. He got his breath in.

"Sorry about Vosk," the man said.

He said it round the plum, and bit the plum.

The breath went out of Eli again. He stood in the doorway holding the boots.

"Look at the face on ya," the man said. "Sit down. It's your kitchen."

Eli put the boots down. He stayed standing.

"You knew him," he said.

"Knew of him." He turned the plum and bit it. "Did the trees here."

"Nine years."

The man nodded, chewing. "Then he wants a chip."

"I paid for it," Eli said. "Eleven hundred. It was my money. He was going to pay it back, a mark a month, he had it all worked out, he was going to—" Eli stopped. Outside, the steward said a number, and somebody answered. "I paid it."

"I know you did, son."

"There was one with a hook," Eli said. "At the end. He knelt on him."

"They'll have had the chip out." He found the last of the plum with his teeth and ate it. "Rest of him goes by weight, down there."

Eli's hand closed partway, as it had at the window, and stopped. He pushed it. It shook, and went no further, and he held it like that at his side.

Across the table the man wiped his fingers on the cloth the cook left folded by the bowl, and sat back.

"He's asking after you," he said. "You'll have heard."

"Who?"

"Big lad. Under the arch." He held his hands apart over the table, wide, and then wider. "Eats on Pole Street."

"The King," Eli said.

"He likes that."

"He's asking."

"He is."

"The Ilders' man told me." Eli came to the table with the hand still crooked at his side, and put his other arm down flat on the wood by the row of stones, and turned the wrist over so the faces showed. "He sent this up."

The man was looking at Eli.

"He'll go on asking," he said.

Eli sat down. The man took a plum and held it out to him across the table. "Go on. You've not eaten."

Eli took it. He held it in his hand.

Out in the yard the count came near the end, and the steward read the number, and the new voice answered it, too loud, all of it, and the man tipped his head at the window, and turned back.

"I'm putting a crew together," he said. "There's a job."

Eli waited.

"Ends at the top of the Tower."

Out past the window, past the long house roof, the Tower stood up out of the grey with the first of the light on it, pale, up and up into the cloud, where you couldn't see the end of it. Eli turned and looked at it. He'd looked at it every day of his life from this kitchen, from his bed, from the Vennicks' roof with his eyes shut and his face up.

"He looked at it," Eli said. "At the end. He was on the stones and he—" He stopped.

The man waited.

"What's up there?"

"Never been." The man looked at the plum in his hand and turned it to the light. "Better fruit than this, I'd hope."

Eli laughed. It came out of him before he knew it was there, one bark, and it hurt the side of his face, and the man laughed with him.

"I'd love ya to come." He reached into the bowl and took a plum, and turned it over, and put it back, and took a different one. "I would."

"What would you want me for?"

The man's eyes went down to Eli's hand on the table, the one with the plum in it, and stayed there, and came back up.

"I can't do it," Eli said. "Not when people are looking. I put my hand up and it just—" He took the hand off the table, plum and all. "Everything sits there."

"There's a teacher," the man said. He bit the plum. "You'll not like it much."

"All right," Eli said. "All right. What do you need? Is it money? I can ring the account, it doesn't ask, it'll be back before—"

"Keep your money, son." He chewed. "I'll tell ya some of what I want."

"Go on."

"You come today."

"Yes."

"You don't come back here after."

"Yes."

"And when I say do a thing, you do it. Then. Not after you've had a think." He looked at the plum and turned it to the next bite.

"Yes," Eli said. "Yes."

The man ate the plum.

"Teo comes," Eli said. "My friend. He was there. He was there for all of it, he got me out, he—Teo comes."

"If he wants."

"He'll want."

"Go and ask him, then." He set the stone down at the end of the row and squared it with a fingertip against the others. "I'll be here."

Eli got up and put the plum down on the table, whole, at the end of the row of stones. At the door he turned round, and the man was reaching for the bowl again. The letter's corner showed under it, pale, with the grey wax on the far side where you couldn't see it. Eli looked at it, and the man looked at it too, once, the corner, and took an apple from the bowl, and sat back with it.

Eli went out by the front with his boots in his hand, and put them on on the path. The watch said ten past six.

---

Teo's mother opened the door in her apron with a wooden spoon in her hand, and porridge on the spoon. She considered Eli's boots, laced wrong, and his face.

"He's up," she said. "He's to be on the Spur at nine. There's a man there doesn't write things down." She held the door. "He's to go."

"I'll tell him."

Upstairs the trunk was empty, with its lid down, pushed under the window. Everything that had been in it was in its place. The books were on the shelf, the cup was in its ring on the sill, the stone was beside the cup, the shirts were gone into the drawer, and the room looked like a room somebody lived in. Teo was sitting on the bed in his trousers with the slate on his knee and the bad hand turned up on his thigh. The dressing had gone yellow in the middle. The skin round it was shiny and pink as far as the wrist, and there was a smell in the room, faint and sweet, that hadn't been there before.

Eli shut the door behind him and stood against it.

"There's a man in my kitchen," he said.

He told it. He told it in the wrong order and went back. The plums. The King, and where the King ate. The Tower. The teacher. He held his wrist out and turned it so the four faces caught the window, and the little moon, and Teo looked at the watch, and lifted his eyes to Eli's face, and went back to the watch, and his mouth stayed shut.

"He knew Vosk," Eli said. "He said his name. First thing, before anything. Sorry about Vosk."

"He knew his name," Teo said.

"That's what I'm telling you."

Teo watched him. He put the bad hand down flat on the bed and thumbed at the slate with the other, slow, and read, and thumbed, and read. He turned it round.

It was the gate's page again, the one he'd had on the counter, run on to now. The Ilders' people out late, and their flyers up from the kerb. The cook's girl with the bread, in at five, out at ten past. After the bread there was nothing, not at the arch and not at the kerb, down to the bottom of the page, and the bottom of the page was now.

"Nobody came in, Eli," Teo said.

"So he came another way."

Teo kept the slate turned to him.

"So there's another way," Eli said. "There's the slot. There's the culvert, there's—it doesn't matter how he got in. Listen." He came off the door. His voice went clear and reasonable. "It's perfectly simple. He's asking. He sent my watch up, Teo. To the Ilders' man. Three doors from my house. He knows which house. You heard what they said about the coat. He'll come up." He held up a finger, and another. "And it's today. We go today, we don't come back here after, and when he says do a thing we do it. I've said yes. I've said it."

"You don't know what it's for," Teo said.

"No."

Teo put the slate down on the bed, face down.

"It was eleven hundred marks," Eli said. "And a chip off a dwarf. And my watch, and a soup stall. That's what it was. And I came home and had a bath." He stopped. "She sweeps round his coat. The cook. It's on the step and she sweeps round it."

Teo looked at the floor between his feet.

"Teo. Listen. Listen to me."

Teo sat and listened.

"You're not doing anything," Eli said. "You're sitting here. They've put you back to the spring, and in the spring they'll put you back to the winter, and you'll pack and unpack and put your cup back in its ring. You've nowhere to be."

Teo sat still.

"So come," Eli said. "That's all it is. Come with me. He said you could. Teo."

Downstairs the spoon went round against the side of a pot.

"Say something."

Teo sat.

"Say something, then! You've had three days. You said plenty in the Scald, you said plenty with your hand—" He stopped. He stood in the middle of the room and breathed. "Teo."

Teo's hair was wet at the front, though the window was open and the room was cold. He put his thumb on the skin by the edge of the dressing and pressed, and took it away, and the place stayed white. He watched it. It came back slowly, pink, then more than pink. On the bed his mother's slate was lit with one line, CLINIC, SEVENTH, NOON, and her mark beside it. He turned it face down.

Teo got up. He went round the trunk to the window, and stood at the sill, and picked up the stone. He turned it over in his good hand. He put it in his trouser pocket, and left the cup in its ring. He sat down on the lid of the trunk and put his boots on. He laced the left one. He tried the right one with the bad hand, and the hand wouldn't close on the lace, and he did it with the good hand and his teeth, slow, and stood up.

He took the slate off the bed and put it inside his shirt.

Eli had been talking. He heard himself stop.

Teo opened the door and went down. At the foot of the stairs his mother came out of the kitchen with the spoon.

"Your hand," she said.

"Right," said Teo, and went past her, and out.

Eli came down after him. She was still standing there. She held the spoon, and a drop of porridge went off the end of it onto the boards, and she watched Eli to the door.

"Nine," she said. "He's to be there at nine."

"I'll tell him," Eli said, and went after Teo.

---

The man was where Eli had left him. There were seven stones on the table now, in a row, and Eli's plum was gone, and past the last stone an apple core was eaten down to the pips, and the bowl had two pears in it and nothing else. The light had come up. The yard was empty, the count long done. The watch said twenty to seven.

He stood up when they came in.

"Morning," he said to Teo.

Teo stopped inside the door and said nothing.

"Jon," the man said, and put his hand out.

Teo's right hand was the one in the dressing. He kept it at his side. Jon looked down at it, and up at Teo, and took his own hand back and put it in his trouser pocket, and with the other he picked his coat up off the chair and shrugged it on.

"This way," he said.

He took a pear out of the bowl as he went by it, and went to the yard door. On the step inside it the second-best coat lay folded in four where Vosk had left it, and he stepped over it and out.

Eli bent and picked it up. He shook it out. The folds stood up in it in a cross, sharp. He put it on. It hung heavy on the left side, low, where the sock was down at the bottom of the pocket. His good coat was in the hall on its hook, pressed. He left it there.

Jon was out on the step in the light with his back to them, eating the pear, his face turned to the long house. Eli stood by the table with his eyes on Jon's back. He lifted the bowl. The last pear rolled in it. The letter was there, face down, the grey wax up, and he took it out from under, and it was as heavy as it had been on the first morning. Twenty-four years, he'd said. They could have tonight. That had been four nights ago. He set the bowl down on the bare wood.

When he went out after them, the letter was in the inside pocket of the coat, against his ribs, the corner of it digging in when he walked.

In the hall the house bell rang.

Teo turned his head.

Jon walked on across the yard without turning round. Eli walked after him, faster. Behind them the steward's feet crossed the hall, and his voice came out through the open kitchen, flat.

"Ninth, the house."

They went past the long house and the barracks. The little ones were out on the barracks step with a bit of bread between them, and they stopped chewing and watched the three of them go by. The smaller one stood up. Neither of them said anything.

The slot was a gap in the white wall above the culvert, one orc wide, with the soil sacks stacked on the far side of it. There was no arch and no glass, only the disc set in the stone at the height of an orc's chest. Jon went in first. He filled most of it, and he went through sideways, with the pear held up clear of the wall, and the disc didn't light for him.

"Mind your head," he said.

Eli went in after him. The walls of the slot were close and smooth and white, and on the left-hand side, a little above his own head, there was a mark on the white, a dark smear gone brown, with grit dried into it. He looked at it going past.

The disc in the wall stayed dark. It didn't look at him. There was no light, and no chime, and no voice to tell him what time of day it was. He came out the other side onto the head of the culvert steps and stopped on them, and waited a step, and it didn't come.

He looked back. The disc stayed dark for Teo too, and Teo stepped out of the slot with his bad hand held against his chest.

Below them the culvert ran down between its high walls toward the Spur, wet, with the morning in the top of it and the dark still in the bottom. Jon went down ahead of them, eating the pear.

## FOUR · The Beater

The culvert let out under the Spur's head, behind the lift, where the wall ran wet and the morning hadn't got down to yet. Jon went up the last of the steps and out into the street without looking to see if they were behind him. He'd finished the pear. He dropped the core in the gutter, and the gutter water took it and turned it round once and carried it off along the kerb toward the Tower.

Eli came up into the noise after him. The lanes overhead were going over three deep with the first of the day's traffic, and the feeds were coming on along the walls one after another as the light reached them, and somewhere a hose was running on stone. He'd been on the Spur a hundred times. He'd been here four nights ago with the purse knocking on his ribs. He stood on the last step and put the heel of his hand to his eye and pressed.

It was the headache. It sat in there behind the eye, and in both ears, and in the hinge of his jaw where he'd been clenching it all the way down. Everybody down here had it. He'd had it every time he came down, and gone home up the stair and not had it, and never put the two together. There was a stall on the corner with blue pills in paper twists laid out on a tray, and a woman was buying two and telling the man the last two had done nothing. Eli took his hand down. The headache stayed where it was. He could feel the shape of it now, the edges, and now he'd found the edges he couldn't stop finding them.

"Teo," he said. "Keep up."

Teo came up the steps one at a time with his good hand flat on the wall. The bad one was inside his shirt, against his chest, in with the slate, and he'd done the buttons up over both. He had no coat. His face was grey under the brown and wet along the hairline, and the morning was cold enough that Eli's breath showed. He got to the street and stood there and breathed through his nose.

"I'm fine," he said.

"I never asked."

Jon was going away down the middle of the street at the same pace he'd crossed the yard. Eli went after him. Teo came after Eli.

"This is it, though," Eli said, dropping back. "Isn't it? This is the thing." He had Teo's good elbow and was steering him round a puddle Teo had already stepped round. "A year that trunk's been packed. Every page there is about off-world, at my table, with your tea going cold. And now you're—Teo. Today." He squeezed the elbow. "And the hand, the minute we're there. Somebody proper. I'll find out who's best. That's the first thing I'll do."

"I need to stop a minute," Teo said.

"We'll stop when we're there. You'll see. It's going to be good."

The street leaned. It had always leaned. The Spur ran out from the Tower toward the drop and the floor under it tipped back the other way, so that walking out along it you were climbing, a little, every step, and your ankles knew it before you did. Eli had never once thought about it. Teo's feet kept going wrong. Twice they went from under him, and both times he went back, not forward, a step back down the street toward the Tower, and the second time Eli caught him by the good arm and held on.

"Got you."

Teo took the arm back.

In the window boxes the plants grew out sideways over the street, every one of them pointing the same way, back over Eli's shoulder. He turned to see what they were pointing at. The Tower filled the street behind him, pale, going up out of the roofs and out of sight.

"Teo," he said. "The plants."

Teo didn't look at the plants.

At the second landing, where a side stair came up from the levels below, a young orc was sitting on the top step with his elbows on his knees. Yard trousers. No shirt. Wire wound round the knuckles of both hands, bright, and round his neck a line of stitches done close in the same wire. He was eating something out of a paper. He watched Jon go by, and Teo, and then he watched the coat.

He folded the paper up small. He stood, and went down the side stair two at a time, and the stair took him.

"Jon," Eli said.

Jon walked on.

Eli looked at Teo. Teo had seen. He had his eyes on the empty step, and then on Eli, and then on the road.

"It's half orcs, the Spur," Eli said. "They sit on steps. It's what steps are for." He laughed. "It's a step."

Teo walked.

---

The flesh market was open. They were hosing the slabs down, and the water ran off the trestles into the gutter, pink. A woman in an apron sorted kidneys into two trays, the good and the less good, quick, without looking at her hands, talking to the next stall about her sister.

Eli walked through it beside Teo, pointing.

"Eyes," he said. "He does them by colour. And the real ones are under the counter. You have to ask."

The stall he was pointing at was doing feet. He'd seen the eyes in a feed. He'd been through here four nights ago with the tarps down and seen nothing at all.

At the far end of the cases a man had a burner going under an iron plate, and strips of something on the plate, spitting, and a stack of cones twisted out of old feed sheet. Jon stopped there. First thing he'd stopped for since the kitchen.

"Two," he said.

The man turned the strips with his fingers. "Clone or the other?"

"Whatever's hot."

The man shovelled a cone full off the hot end of the plate. Jon paid him coin out of his trouser pocket and walked on eating, and left the change on the plate. A little way on he held the cone back over his shoulder without turning his head, and Eli, who was nearest, took a strip out of it.

Hot, and salt, and good. He ate it.

"That's good," he said. "What is that?"

"Get it down ya," Jon said, and ate.

Eli held the cone out to Teo. Teo looked at it, and back at the plate with the man scraping it, and shook his head once, and kept walking with his hand in his shirt. Eli shrugged and gave the cone back over Jon's shoulder, and Jon's hand came back and took it.

---

The feeds ran with the sound off. ELVEN DELEGATION · TALKS, and tall people coming down a white ramp in the rain, too far off to have faces. A minister smiling, then gone. TERRACE PROPERTY VALUES, and a line under it going down.

"Elves," Eli said. "Dace's father had one to dinner once. He said it stood the whole night. Wouldn't take a chair. They had to eat round it."

Jon finished the cone and folded the paper in four and put it in his pocket.

At the landings the walls went from paint to grey stone, and on every one of them there was the seal, stamped, an oval with nothing in it. Eli had passed under a thousand of them. He looked at one now going by. Plain. A raised rim, and inside the rim smooth, and a little worn where people had touched it, or leaned on it, or put their backs to it waiting for something, so that the worn part was cleaner than the wall round it. His coat swung as he walked and the corner of the letter dug in under his ribs. His hand went flat on the coat over it and stayed there till the next landing.

It was a long way out. They stopped for Teo three times. The first time he only stood. The second time Eli had the coat off one shoulder to put round him before he thought about the pockets, the sock in the one and the letter in the other.

"Vosk would've carried you," he said. "Over one shoulder, and not a word. You remember the tree. By my window. I wanted it moved by Thursday and he had it across the yard on his back, pot and all. He'd have had you there and back."

Teo looked at the coat and said "No," and Eli shrugged it back on. The third time Teo sat, on a bollard, with his head down between his arms, and Jon stood a little way off with his back to them and his hands in his pockets, and waited, and when Teo got up Jon walked on.

Out along the Spur the buildings got lower and further apart and the street got wider and fuller: men in grey, a gang of orcs on a scaffold scraping a wall white, a woman selling slates off a blanket with their screens all lit and all showing the same face. The air had a new smell in it, hot metal and something burnt, and under the noise of the lanes there was a deeper one, a hum that came up through the boots. The berths. Ahead the street ran in under a long low roof with lights in it, and across the mouth of it there was a barrier, and a booth with a window, and beside the booth an arch with a reader set in it. A queue went under the arch one face at a time, and each face got its light and its soft chime.

Jon went down the side of the queue, past all of it, to the booth window. Eli and Teo went with him.

The watch said nine.

Eli saw it on his wrist, and he saw Teo see it too, and look away, back up the Spur, the way they'd come. Teo's mother on the step with her spoon. He's to be there at nine. Eli turned the wrist over and back. The little moon was low in its window.

"What," he said.

Teo didn't say what.

On the side of the booth somebody had pasted a notice. Paper, which you hardly saw any more, gone soft with the wet off the street and lifting at one corner. NOTICE, it said at the head, in plain black, and under that TERRORIST CELL, and under that UNREST IN THE SCALD · ANY INFORMATION · REWARD, and under that a row of grey boxes the size of a face. In each box, small, in the middle, it said TO FOLLOW.

Teo read it. He read the whole of it, down to the line along the bottom, which was very small and which Eli let go.

"No faces," Teo said.

"They never have faces." Eli rubbed his eye. "Dace'll have one in a frame by the weekend."

The man in the booth was human, heavy, his collar open, with a cup of something steaming on the ledge by his elbow. He had a slate by the cup and he was watching the arch, and the faces going under it, and nothing much. Jon put his forearms on the ledge outside the window and said something through the gap, low. The guard's eyes came up to Jon, and went past Jon, and stopped.

Teo was standing behind Jon's shoulder. He'd gone very still, with his weight back on his heels.

The guard looked at the wet on Teo's face. He looked at the shirt done up to the throat in the warm under the roof, and the arm held across under it, and where the arm pushed, the shirt had pulled open between two buttons and the dressing showed in the gap, and the yellow on the dressing. His eyes stayed on that longest. Then he got up off his stool and stepped back in the booth, away from the window, with his cup.

"Not him," he said. "Not through here."

Eli stepped forward.

"He's fine," he said. "It's a cut. It's healing. He did it on a—it's a cut." His voice had gone clear and easy. He heard it go. "If there's some sort of form, I'm happy to—"

Jon came off the ledge and stood across him, between him and the window, not quickly.

"Wait there," Jon said.

Eli stopped with his mouth open on the next word.

*When I say do a thing, you do it. Then.* In the kitchen, round a plum, with the stones in a row on the table.

He shut his mouth. He stood. Behind him Teo had sat down. He hadn't meant to, from the look of him; his legs had just stopped, and he'd gone down onto the kerb with his back against the booth, under the notice, and his head down between his knees. Eli went down beside him and got an arm round his shoulders. Teo was hot through the shirt. He was shaking, small and fast, all through.

"I'm fine," Teo said, to his knees.

"I know. Stay there. I know."

Over his head Jon was talking to the window, low. Eli caught none of it except the tail, which was "—off a winch. Docks," and then the guard saying something back, and somebody in the queue saying "Come on," and the guard telling him to shut it. Then a laugh, short. He couldn't tell whose. He held on to Teo. Teo's breath went in and out against his arm.

When he looked up the guard was on his stool again with his cup, and the barrier was still down, and the arch was busy with the next face in the queue.

"Round the back, then," the guard said, to the window, to nobody. "Not my line."

"This way," said Jon.

Eli got Teo up. Teo came up with his good hand on the booth and stood, and on his feet he lifted his head and looked at Jon's back going away along the barrier, and then at Eli. He held it there. Then he looked at the ground and went after Jon.

---

Round the back was a gap between two stacks of crates as high as a house, and then a lane with a gutter down the middle and a white wall along one side, and in the white wall, a long way along, a gate with no arch and no glass, only a disc set in the stone at the height of an orc's chest. A line of orcs waited at it. Thirty or forty, loaders, most of them in canvas with straps over the shoulders, some with hooks, standing along the wall in the order they'd come, one behind the other, close, not talking.

Jon walked down the side of the line the way he'd walked down the queue.

The line watched the gate. When the front orc stepped up, the disc lit for him, low and steady, and he stood in it while it read him, and his hands opened and shut once at his sides, and he went through. Then the next.

The next was old, grey in the stubble. He stepped up into the light and stood, and his eyes closed. Eli saw them close. He stood there with his face in the light and his shoulders down and his eyes shut, and the disc had read him and he hadn't moved on, one breath, two, and the light in the disc came up brighter. The old one's head jerked back. His mouth came open and his knees went, both at once, straight down onto the stone with a noise like a dropped bag, and he stayed there, kneeling at the gate, with his chin going and spit running out of the side of his mouth and his hands shaking open on his thighs.

The light went down again.

The orc behind him stepped round him into it and stood and was read and went through. The one after that did the same. Nobody bent down.

Jon came to him and stepped over his legs, one long step, not breaking stride, and went through the gate.

Eli went round. He had Teo by the arm and he went round wide, by the gutter, and his boot went in the gutter to the ankle and came out wet. He looked down at the old one going past. The old one was looking at the stone between his knees.

"Is he—" Eli said.

The line watched the gate.

The disc stayed dark for Eli, a hand from his face. It gave him nothing. He came through into the hot metal smell and the long roof with Teo against his side, and the disc stayed dark for Teo too, and behind them the next orc stepped round the old one into the light.

---

The berths ran along the city's lip under the long roof, one after another, cradles of black iron set out over the drop, and in each cradle a ship. The roof stopped where the floor stopped, and past that there was nothing, only air going down into haze, and the light coming in white and flat from the cloud overhead. Eli had never been to the public berths. His flyers had come to the kerb.

They walked the length of it. A big grey hauler with a company's stripes down her flank, lit up, her hatches open on long clean holds. A passenger boat with a queue at the ramp and a girl at its head in a cap taking slates. Two cradles empty, with men hosing them out. A white private thing with a crest on her nose that Eli half knew, gold on blue, from a house on the Seventh, and a man in house colours on her ramp eating his breakfast off a plate, who saw the coat and stood up. A long old one at the far end of a side spur on her own, grey and lean and ribbed down her length so you could count the ribs, with nobody loading her and her ramp shut. Teo turned his head and looked at her going by. Eli was looking at the crest.

The last berth before the roof gave out had the beater in it.

She was long and flat-bottomed and wide in the beam, and she'd been four colours once and was now about nine, patch on patch, plates of grey on plates of a different grey, with a sheet riveted over her nose by somebody who'd done it by hand, the rivet heads standing up all round it in a ragged row. Her landing legs were three. Two were the same length. Under the third somebody had put a block of wood. She sat in her cradle with a lean on her, nose down to the left, and from twenty yards off you could smell her: fish, and wet grain, and engine, and something sweet and old under all of it that had soaked in and wasn't coming out.

Eli laughed out loud.

"Look at her," he said. "Teo. Look at her."

Teo was looking.

"She's got a lean on her. Like the Spur." He was walking faster. "She's on a block of wood. She's standing on a block."

The ramp was down at her stern, and six orcs were loading her off a stack at the rim of the berth, crates on trolleys up the ramp and into the dark of her and back down empty. Every crate on the stack had the same stencil on its end, BONE MEAL and a grade, and under that, smaller, ITHE.

"Bone meal," Teo said.

"Ithe," Eli read. "What's an Ithe?"

Nobody told him.

At the foot of the ramp stood a woman with a board.

She was human, forty or so, in a flight jacket that had had a name tape over the pocket once and had only the stitch holes now. Her hair was scraped back and tied with a bit of wire. She had a tin cup in the hand without the board, and her eyes were red at the rims. Each time a crate went past her up the ramp she made a mark on the board, and between crates she shut her eyes where she stood, with the board against her chest, and opened them when the next wheels came onto the ramp. She had them shut when the three of them came up. She opened them on Jon. Then past him.

"You said one," she said.

Jon was wiping his fingers on his trousers. "Two."

Her eyes went to Teo and stayed a while, on his face, and on the arm held under the shirt, and she drank out of the cup. Then she looked at Eli, and at his coat, and her eyes went over it. The collar. The cuffs. The cloth across the shoulders where it hung right. The buttons.

"That's Terrace," she said.

"It's my second-best," Eli said, and smiled at her. "You should see the other one."

"I could run her three months on that coat. Fuel and berths." A crate went by and she marked it. "Keep that thing out of sight."

"Absolutely," said Eli. "Not a soul. I'm—"

"Board's on." She marked the next crate. "It's got you as meal. Two over the count, wet. Say a name on my ramp and it writes the name, and then I'm carrying people, and they take the boat for that."

He opened his mouth and shut it again and laughed, and looked at Teo to see if Teo was laughing, and Teo wasn't. Teo was looking at the nearest crate on the stack. He went to it and sat down on it, slowly, and then all at once, and put his good hand on his knee.

Jon stood at the foot of the ramp with the pilot. He said something to her that Eli lost, low, with his head turned to the ship and not to her. She answered with her head turned the same way, and drank out of the cup.

A crate went up the ramp. The orc pushing it was big through the back and had his eyes on the trolley's front wheel. Eli stepped out of his way and the orc went past.

"Good man," Eli said.

The orc went on up into the dark with the trolley.

Jon turned round. He looked at Eli, and took his hands out of his pockets.

"Got elves to see," he said.

Eli laughed. It came up out of him and it hurt his face. "Elves," he said. "Of course. Elves. Give them my love."

Jon waited till he'd finished.

"Ithe," he said. "There's a man there'll find you."

"Ithe. That's—" Eli looked at the stack, at the stencils. "Where's that? Is that far? Is that the teacher? Is that—you said a crew. You never said—" He stopped. "When will you—"

Jon's eyes had gone to Teo, on the crate, and the hand inside the shirt. They stayed there a little. They came back.

"That'll want looking at," he said.

"I know it will. I know. When will you—"

"Do as she says, son."

He went. He went back the way they'd come along the berths, not hurrying, with his coat open and his hands loose at his sides. Eli went after him one step, two, out of the ramp's shadow onto the bright floor, and stopped there.

Jon went past the ribbed ship and the white one with the crest. A cart came across the walk in front of him, piled with sacks, and he went round the back of it and into the queue at the passenger boat, and through the queue and out among the men in grey on the far side, a big grey head going away, and then a lot of heads, and then none of them his.

Eli stood and looked at the place. The queue shuffled up a step and closed it.

"When will you," he said, to the walk.

Behind him the pilot made a mark on her board.

"Ithe," she said, and breathed out once through her nose. It might have been a laugh.

He turned round. "You know it? Where is it? How long does it take?"

"Hold's back there," she said. "Don't touch the straps."

---

The hold was long and low, dark aft, and lit forward by two strips in the ceiling, one of which went off and on. Crates were stacked to the ribs down both sides and strapped, with a gangway between them a man wide, and halfway down, against the starboard wall, a bench that folded out on chains. The smell was worse inside. It had a sound too, a tick in the hull somewhere, and a hum that went up through the floor into his teeth and sat there, next to the headache.

Eli helped Teo down the gangway. Teo shook the arm off, and then let it come back. He sat on the first crate past the bench, not on the bench, and put his back to the stack behind him and shut his eyes.

"Not there. Here." Eli had him up again by the good arm. "The bench. There's a wall to it. You can lie down on it, look, it's practically a bed." He got Teo onto the bench and turned him so his back was square to the wall, and lifted the bad hand and laid it on Teo's chest for him, and put Teo's good hand over it, and stood back. "There. Isn't that better."

"The crate's fine," Teo said.

"The bench is better. Trust me." Eli turned round. "Look at this." He stood in the gangway and turned round, taking it in. The ribs. The patched plates, inside as well as out, and a seam over the bench with a strip of grey tape run along it, and a second strip over that where the first had lifted. He put his thumb on it. It gave. "It's tape. She's held together with tape."

Teo looked at the tape. Then past it, at a plate riveted by the hatch, small and brass and gone green round the rim, with letters stamped in it, and a number, and a little mark like a fish.

"Old hull," Teo said. "They stopped making these."

"How do you know that?"

Teo let it go by, and Eli was already moving. He went back up the gangway to the open ramp and stood at the head of it, in the hatch, out of the way, with the bright floor of the berths below and the orcs coming up and down.

They were nearly done. The stack was down to its last row. The six of them worked in two threes, one three bringing the loaded trolleys up, the other running the empties down and loading them, and they kept off the common talk, but now and then one said a word in the orc talk and the one it was said to answered with a grunt, or nothing, or a word. One of them had a strap on his harness that had gone and been mended. It was stitched back on to the buckle in a different colour, brown thread on the grey, big stitches, done by somebody who was no good at it and had done it anyway. He was young. He said something to the one beside him going down the ramp, quick and low, and the other snorted and shoved him with his shoulder, and the young one shoved back, and the pilot at the bottom said "You," without lifting her head from her board, and they both went quiet and went on down.

Eli watched them come and go. They went round him in the hatch. They looked at the coat, once, going past, or they looked at nothing.

The last loaded trolley came up with the big one behind it pushing. Halfway up, the strap on the top crate let go. It went with a crack and the crate slid, slow, then not slow, off the side of the trolley and over the ramp rail, down toward the berth floor, and on the floor under it the young orc with the mended strap was bending to the stack with his back to the ramp.

The big one had his head down behind the trolley. The rest were bent to the stack. At the foot of the ramp the pilot stood between crates with her eyes shut and the board flat to her chest.

Eli's hand was up. The crate stopped.

It stopped in the air on its corner, a man's height over the young one's back, and hung there. Heavy. He had all of it, every bit of how heavy it was, in his arm and in his teeth and up the back of his head, and he held it, and brought it back. It came up, slow, and back over the rail, and settled onto the trolley with a knock, a hand's width from where it had been, crooked.

The big orc lifted his head at the knock. He looked at the crate, and at the strap hanging off it, and at the crate again, and round, down the ramp. The young one had straightened up from the stack and was looking up too, at the knock, with a crate in his arms.

Eli had his hand down at his side by then. He put it in his pocket. Under his fingers was the sock, low and heavy, the knot in it gone hard.

The big one set his shoulder to the trolley and pushed it on up past Eli into the hold.

"Strap," the pilot said from below. She'd seen the end of it, the strap. She marked her board. "That's off your day."

The big one went on into the dark without answering.

Something warm ran out of Eli's nose onto his lip. He wiped it with the back of his other wrist, and the wrist came away red. He turned to the open side of the cradle and the air past it, and wiped again, and again, and swallowed. His stomach came up into his throat. He swallowed that too. The headache had gone up a notch behind the eye and was beating now, quick, and in the pocket the bad hand had shut on the sock, wet, all the way round the knot, the way it had not shut in four days, and when he went to open it, it stayed. With his back to all of them he looked out at the haze, white, a long way down, and a flyer going across it very small, until the bleeding stopped.

He wiped the wrist on the lining of the coat. The hand came open in the pocket a finger at a time. He took it out and looked at it then. The knot was printed in the palm, red, going white.

The young orc came up last with the empty trolley for the hold and the loading canvas over his shoulder, a big square of it, filthy, that they'd had over the stack. In the hold he ran the trolley in against the bulkhead and set its brake with his foot. Then he took the canvas off his shoulder and shook it out once. He folded it in half, and in half again, the long way and then across, corners to corners, quick and square, and put it on the crate by the hatch, and pressed it flat with the side of his hand, and went past Eli and down the ramp.

"Good—" Eli said.

It stopped there. He stood with it in his mouth. The orc went on, and the rest of it stayed where it was, and at the foot of the ramp the orc fell in with the others and they went off along the berth together, the six of them, toward the white wall.

The pilot came up the ramp with her board under her arm and stopped beside him in the hatch.

"In or off," she said.

Eli looked back along the walk. The passenger boat. The queue, with no big grey head in it. The white ship with the crest. The man in house colours had put his plate down on the rail and was standing at the foot of his own ramp with a slate in his hand, looking along the berths at the beater, at the hatch, at the coat in it.

Twenty minutes to the Seventh, in that. Less.

He looked down the hold. Teo had gone back to his crate, and sat on it with his eyes shut and his head back against the stack and his hand in his shirt.

Out on the white ramp the man lifted the slate.

Eli stepped back out of the light, into the hold.

The pilot went past him, and past Teo, and counted the crates down the starboard side with her finger, and down the port side, and hit a plate on the bulkhead with the side of her fist. Behind Eli the ramp began to come up.

"Mind your feet," she said.

---

She flew from a chair at the front of the hold, in a box the width of a cupboard, through a hatch in the bulkhead with a door on it hanging open on one hinge. The other hinge was in a tin on the floor by her foot. Ahead of her was glass, a long low curve of it, scratched, with a crack across one corner that somebody had filled with clear stuff and the clear stuff had gone yellow, and below the glass her board, lit, and two sticks, and a holder with her cup in it.

"Bench," she said. "Belts are on the bench. Not the straps."

Eli got Teo onto the bench. Teo went where he was put. Eli got the belt across him, canvas with a hook on it that went into a ring in the wall, and got his own across, and his bad hand closed partway on the hook and stopped with the fingers curled, and he did it with the other, and it took him three goes to find the ring. From the bench, if he leaned forward, he could see past her shoulder through the hanging door: a slice of the glass and the white beyond it.

The ship shuddered. The tick in the hull went quicker, and quicker, and stopped being a tick. Something under the floor began to roar, a long way off and then not far off at all, and the floor came up under them, and from outside, under the stern, there was a crack and a clatter, and Eli thought: the block of wood.

They lifted.

Through the door he saw the roof go by, and the light change, and then the berths fall away under them, sideways, the whole long row of them with their ships, the grey hauler and the white one, small already, and the ribbed one out on her spur. The beater turned. She turned slowly, with the roar going up and down, and swung her nose round, and through the scratched glass the city came across it.

He'd seen it before. He'd had his flyers. He'd seen it from the lanes every day of his life, from the Vennicks' roof, from his own window. He'd never seen it falling away. The mid-levels went by under them as a plate of roofs, grey on grey, and the lanes went by in strings, and the beater climbed up through the lanes and the lanes went under, the lowest one, the next, the highest, the traffic in them going on along them small and bright and not looking up. Then there was nothing over them but the beater's own noise. Below, on the first rise of land, a line of white houses, very small, with all their windows turned one way.

All their windows turned to the Tower, and the Tower was in the glass.

It stood in the middle of it, pale, up out of the oldest ground, and the city kept back from it in a ring, and the beater climbed, and it went on up out of the glass. She climbed and climbed and the roofs went to a grey map and the map went soft round its rim, and the Tower got no shorter. The glass went on cutting it off. Then the cloud came round them, white, then grey, then white, and the whole hull shook and something in the stern went bang and went on banging, and they came out on top.

The cloud lay out flat in every direction, bright, all the way to the sky. Out of the middle of it the Tower came up, pale, smooth, in one piece, and went on up out of the cloud and out of the glass, and Eli bent forward against his belt to see more of it, and there was no more. There was the frame. He bent further. The belt stopped him.

"Teo," he said. "Teo. Look."

The pilot said something to her board, flat and quick, in the voice people used for numbers, and the board answered her in the same voice, numbers and a name and numbers. With the banging, Eli caught none of it. On the board a line came up green.

Teo had his head back against the wall and his eyes half shut.

"Earth," Teo said.

Eli had his face to the glass. "What?"

Teo didn't say it again. On the board the line went away and another came up under it.

The beater was turning, swinging her nose off, and the Tower slid along the glass and off its side, and then there was only the cloud, and the sky going dark above it, and then the sky going black.

Eli put his palm on the wall of the hold beside him. The cold came through the plate into his hand, colder than anything all day.

"Teo," he said. "It's black."

Teo's chest went up and down.

---

Teo was sick before the hour was out.

He did it neatly, leaning out over the belt, between his own boots, and wiped his mouth on his sleeve and sat back. His face was the colour of the tape. When Eli put the back of his hand to Teo's forehead the heat came off him into it, and Eli took the hand away and looked at it.

"Let me see your hand."

"No."

"Teo. Let me see it."

Teo looked at the bulkhead opposite. He was breathing short through his mouth. Eli unhooked his own belt and got down off the bench onto his knees, and undid Teo's shirt, the bottom button, the next, and the slate slid down against Teo's hip, and Teo let him, and the arm came out. Teo held it out a little way, turned up, and looked at the bulkhead.

The dressing was his mother's knot. The linen had gone yellow right through and brown round the rim, and it had stuck. Eli picked at the knot with the nails of his good hand. It held. He got his teeth to it, and it went, and he unwound it, round and round, and the last of it was stuck to the palm and he pulled that and Teo made a noise in his throat, and it came.

The smell came with it.

The cut went across the palm from the root of the first finger nearly to the wrist. It had closed at the ends. In the middle it hadn't, or it had and come open, and the lips of it stood up, swollen, white and wet, soft-looking, and down in the bottom of it there was yellow, and the yellow moved when Teo's fingers moved. All round it the hand was tight and shiny and red, and the red went on over the heel of the hand and up the inside of the wrist, and up from the wrist there was a line in the skin, thin and red, going up the arm under the sleeve.

Eli pushed the sleeve back. The line went up past the elbow.

He sat back on his heels.

"Yes," he said. "Good. Yes." His eyes went round the hold, and to the pilot's back through the door. "I'll ring. I'll ring the account, they'll send something out, there'll be somewhere we can—"

"They log it," Teo said.

"I don't care if they log it."

"Nothing rings out of here," said the pilot, with her back to him.

"Then where's—have you got—there must be something on board, a kit, a—"

She reached under her chair without looking and brought out a tin box with a cross on it, and threw it back through the door over her shoulder. It hit the floor of the hold and came open and slid, and things came out of it.

"It's what there is," she said.

Eli gathered it up off the floor. A roll of tape, grey, the same as the tape on the seam. A tube of something with a split in its side, gone hard. A packet of pads with one pad in it. A blade in a paper sleeve. A card of pills with every pill on it punched out, all twelve, the foil standing up in little torn crowns.

He held the card up and looked at it.

There was a laugh in his chest. He could feel it sitting there. He put the card down on the bench.

"This isn't anything," he said. "This is nothing. This is a—" He got up. He went to the door with the card in his hand and stood in it, holding the frame. Past her the glass was black now, with a few hard points of light in it, and the board was lit green and amber, and her cup was in its holder, and she had one hand on a stick and the other on her knee. "He's got a line up his arm."

She didn't look round.

"Name it," Eli said. "Whatever it is. Whatever you've got. Name it."

She sat a bit with her hand on her knee. Then she took the other hand off the stick, and the ship flew on without it, and she reached inside the flight jacket and brought out a flat tin, the kind sweets came in, old, the paint worn off the lid, and held it on her knee unopened.

"Kit's a joke," she said.

"Yes. What is it? I'll pay. I can pay." His hand had gone into his coat pocket already, down to the bottom, to the sock. He felt the knot, and the coins under it through the wool, shifting.

The pilot turned her head and looked at him for the first time since the ramp. Then her eyes went down to his wrist on the doorframe.

"Not marks," she said. "That."

The four faces. The little moon in its window, low.

"It's only a watch," he said. He said it quick.

Behind him Teo said, "Don't."

He said it once, flat, from the bench, with his hand held out turned up where Eli had left it. Eli didn't turn round. His hand came out of the pocket and let go of the sock, and the sock settled at the bottom, and he undid the watch. The buckle was stiff with the dirt in it, and he worked it with the thumb of the bad hand until it gave. He held it out to her, and the strap hung down off his fingers, and she took it.

She paid no mind to the faces. She weighed it in her hand once, and turned it over, and looked at the back, and put it inside her flight jacket where the tin had been. Nothing went on her board. Then she opened the tin on her knee.

There were two kinds in it. White ones, flat and small. And three yellow ones, longer, in a twist of paper of their own. She counted white ones out into his palm, six, and then took the twist with the yellow ones and put that in his palm too, on top.

"Two white now," she said. "Two tonight. Two in the morning. Yellow if he goes off his head."

"Off his—"

"You'll know." She shut the tin. "Clean it out first. With the blade. All the yellow, down to where it bleeds. Then tape it shut. He'll shout." She put her hand back on the stick. "It'll hold him a few days. Then he wants a proper one."

"A proper what?"

She was watching her board.

---

He did it on the floor of the hold, on his knees, with Teo on the bench above him. He'd given him the two white ones first, and Teo had taken them dry, one, two, his throat working, and then put the back of his head to the wall and his good hand flat on the bench and shut his eyes.

"Ready," Eli said.

Teo said nothing.

Eli took the blade out of its paper. Very small. He laid Teo's arm across his own thigh, palm up, and put the bad hand round the wrist to hold it, and the hand closed partway and stopped, the fingers curled, and the wrist lay loose in it and slid.

"It won't," he said. "My hand. It won't—"

"Kneel on it," Teo said, with his eyes shut.

Eli looked at him. Then he laid Teo's forearm along the bench, palm up, and got his knee across the wrist, and leaned. Teo's breath went out of him.

He put the point in at the end of the cut, where it was white, and pushed, and the white opened and the yellow came, a lot of it, more than there could be room for, and ran out down the side of Teo's hand onto the bench and onto Eli's trousers, and the smell came up off it so that his eyes watered and he turned his head and breathed out through his mouth and turned back. Teo's whole arm went hard under his knee. He didn't shout. He made a sound with his mouth shut, high up in his nose, and kept making it, and his heels drummed twice on the floor and stopped.

"Nearly," Eli said. "Nearly. Nearly."

Along it, then. Down into it with the point, scraping, and he wiped the blade on his knee, and went in again. His good hand had stopped shaking. The point went where he put it. He scraped until it came red at the bottom instead of yellow and then went on a little past that in case, and then it was bleeding, properly, red, running into the cup of Teo's palm, and he pressed the one pad down into it hard with the heel of his hand and held it there.

Teo had stopped making the sound. He was breathing in short pulls through his nose. His eyes were shut and the tears were coming out from under the lids and down the sides of his face into his ears, and he left them.

"Done," Eli said. "That's it. That's all of it. Done."

He got his knee off. He taped it. He went round and round the hand with the grey tape until the pad was buried and the tape went over the knuckles and round the wrist, and he bit it off with his teeth, and it looked like nothing. He laid it on Teo's chest. Teo's good hand came up off the bench and found it and held it there, at the wrist, and stayed.

Eli sat back against the crate opposite. He had yellow on his trousers and on his cuffs, the second-best coat's cuffs, and on his hands, and he looked at it, and wiped his hands on the coat, and looked at them again.

"There," he said. He was out of breath. "There. That's done. And when we're down, first thing, the best man in the place. I'll get a call out, they'll fly someone. And a room with a window, Teo. A big one. You can lie and look out of it all day and I'll read you the boring parts."

"Is there water," Teo said.

"And a bath. Oh, a bath. You'll sleep a week." Eli laughed and put his head back against the crate. "Vosk said you could sleep anywhere. On a wall. He found you asleep on the yard wall once and stood under it the whole hour in case you—"

He stopped there.

Teo's tongue went over his lips, and stopped.

The hull ticked. In the stern the banging had gone back to a knock, every so often, uneven, something back there loose and swinging. The roar under the floor had dropped to a hum. Through the door the pilot sat with one hand on her knee, and her cup in its holder, and the black in the glass.

Eli lifted his wrist to see how long it had been. There was nothing on it. Only the mark where the strap had been, a band of paler skin with a line of grey in the crease of it, and the hairs flat. He turned it to the light, and put the arm down.

"Teo," he said, after a while.

Teo's eyes stayed shut.

"The board. When we came out on top. What did it say?"

Teo lay with his head back against the wall and his good hand on the taped one. His mouth was a little open.

"Clear of Earth," he said.

Eli waited for the rest. The hum went on in the floor.

"Ithe," he said. "Have you heard of it?"

Teo's good hand lay where it was, on the tape. He might have been asleep. Eli watched him to see.

The letter was against his ribs. He put his hand inside the coat and found it, the corner first and then the whole flat of it, warm from him, and the wax on the far side under his fingers, whole. He kept his hand there.

Aft, in the dark, the loose thing knocked, and was quiet, and knocked.
