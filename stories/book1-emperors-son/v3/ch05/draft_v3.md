## FIVE · The Teacher

The knock got worse coming down.

All through the dark it had been a knock, every so often, and Eli had slept against the crate in pieces and come awake to it each time. Once, when it felt like night, he put two white pills in Teo's mouth, and Teo took them dry without opening his eyes. Then the hum in the floor went up a note, and Eli got back up on the bench and found the ring for his hook, and the black in the glass past the pilot's shoulder went brown, and the knock came quicker, and then it was a bang, and then it was the whole stern going, bang and bang and a sound under it of metal working against metal and losing.

The pilot said something to her board. The board said something back in its numbers voice, and a light came up red in among the green, and she took her cup out of its holder and put it on the floor between her boots.

"Coupling," she said, to the glass. Then, over her shoulder: "Coupling. Off here."

"Off where?"

She let that go. The ship dropped, and caught, and dropped, and Teo's head came away from the wall and went back against it. Out in the glass there was ground now, flat and grey and running with water, with black heaps on it, and things standing up out of it on long legs that Eli took for trees until he saw they were cranes, dozens of them, hundreds, going away in rows into a haze the colour of weak tea. The light was wrong. It came in low and from one side, off a sun that was too small and too red and sat down near the rim of everything without warming any of it.

They hit belly first. She came down on something hard and flat and slid on it, and the bang in the stern went once more, very loud, and quit, and the hum died, and everything ticked.

The pilot was out of her chair before it had stopped ticking. She came back through the hold with a bar in one hand and her board under her arm, stepping over Eli's boots, and slapped the ramp release with the side of her fist as she went by, and the rain came in.

Cold rain, with grit in it. It smelled of hot iron and wet ash, and it put out the smell of the hold in one breath, and Eli sat with his face in it and breathed.

"Up," he said. "We're here."

Teo opened his eyes on the open ramp, and the rain on it, and the grey past it. He undid his own belt with his good hand and stood, and stood there, with the taped hand held against his shirt.

"I'm fine," he said.

Eli got his own belt off. The hook was stiff, and he took it in the bad hand, and the fingers shut on it most of the way round and held there, shaking a little, and the hook came off the ring. He opened the hand, and it opened.

From the stern there was a clang, and then another, and a panel came off and went down flat on the deck. The pilot had her arm in the hole up to the shoulder and her cheek against the hull, and the board pinned under the other arm against her ribs.

At the top of the ramp Eli turned with his hand out. "Te—"

Her board. Its little light, green, by her elbow.

"Come on," he said instead. "Mind the step."

Teo came past him and down, one hand on the rail. Eli looked back down the hold, at the canvas folded in four on the crate by the hatch, at the pilot's back. The jacket was zipped to the neck. The watch was in there somewhere, against her.

"Thank you," he said. "For the pills. And the—for all of it."

She brought her arm out of the hole with something in the fist, black and broken off at one end, and turned it over.

"Not my pad," she said, to the broken thing. "It's what there is."

He went down.

---

A pad for ore, long and black and slick, iron plate gone dull, with barges on it in a line, their bellies open, and a gang of men with a trolley already coming for the bone meal. Men. A dozen of them, in oilskins, with their hoods down and their faces red with the cold, and not one of them grey. Humans, doing it. He had never seen that.

They paid the coat no mind. They went up the ramp past him talking, about a shift, about somebody's sister, and came down again with the first crate on the trolley between them, and a big one at the front with his hood off was laughing. Laughing at nothing. He laughed a bit longer than there was anything to laugh at, with his mouth open and the rain going in it, and then stopped all at once and took his end.

"Behind his ear," Teo said.

Eli looked. Behind the big man's ear, where the hair had been shaved off in a patch the size of a thumb, there was a scar, pale and raised and puckered at the rim, and under it the skin stood up over something square.

"What about it?"

Teo had sat down on a bollard at the lip of the plate. He didn't say what about it.

The trolley went off across the pad toward a shed with its doors open and a fire in it.

Eli stood out on the plate, where it was flat and open and the rain came at him off the whole field of cranes. He stood with his back straight and the coat open, the way you stood at a ball when you'd said you'd be by the doors. Anyone coming off those barges would see a man in a long coat with a good cut, wet through, waiting, and know him.

Nobody came down off the barges. The men in the shed fed bone meal to the fire. Far off at the end of the rows a horn went, long and flat, and then another nearer, and the cranes on the nearest row all stopped together with their loads in the air, and hung, and started again.

"He'll come," Eli said. "He said a man."

Teo was sitting with his bad hand inside his shirt and the rain running off his chin.

"Nobody's coming, Eli," he said.

"He'll have been held up."

Behind them the beater's ramp came up, grinding, with a stop halfway, and shut, and the hum came on under the hull, rough. She lifted off the plate a little crooked and found herself and went off low over the cranes toward the far end of the field, and was a dot, and was gone into the haze. The knock wasn't in her now. Or it was too far to hear.

Eli watched the place she'd gone.

"Well," he said.

---

There was a stall at the head of the road where the pad met the heaps, a hut of sheet iron with an urn steaming in its open front and cups hanging on hooks, and a woman in it with her arms folded. Eli went to it. He put his hands on the counter and smiled, and felt the smile go wrong on the swollen side of his face, and kept it.

"Good morning," he said. It might not have been morning. "I'm hoping you can help. We're expected. There's a man meets the boats—someone who'd meet a ship coming in. From the city. He'd have been told."

The woman looked at the coat. She took her time on it.

"Pask met the boats," she said.

"Pask." He liked it at once. "That'll be him. Where would I—"

"Pask's dead."

The urn hissed. Rain ran off the roof in a line between them.

"When?"

"Before the rains."

"Oh. Then somebody of his. He'll have had people."

She unfolded her arms and took a cup off a hook and wiped it with her thumb and hung it back. "Kept a yard up the Cut. For boys. Him and the old one across."

"A teacher," Eli said.

She didn't say yes.

"Well, he'll be late, then." He laughed. She didn't. He stopped. "Sorry. I'm sorry. That's—who's got his yard now? Who's teaching his—where did they go?"

She looked past him at the pad, at the trolley gang coming back across it with their hoods down, at the big one at the front.

"Cranes," she said.

"And a doctor." He'd nearly forgotten. He turned and found Teo on the bollard, and turned back. "My friend's hurt. His hand. Who's the best? Not the cheapest. The best."

"Clinic's at the arch." She tipped her head at the road. "There's a reader on the door."

From the bollard, through the rain, Teo said, "They log it."

"Then somebody who doesn't."

The woman took a cup off its hook, and wiped it with her thumb, and hung it back.

The Cut was a lane between two walls of slag, going up. Black underfoot and black on both sides, and the rain had cut channels in the walls that ran down in fans, and the water in the bottom of the lane was ankle deep and orange. Teo walked in the middle of it where it was flattest and stopped twice and went on. The second time he put his good hand on the slag and it came away black, and he held it up in front of him like something he'd been handed.

There were yards off the Cut, behind fences of sheet. Through the gaps Eli saw stacks, rusted, and a dog on a chain that watched them and kept quiet, and a woman taking in washing that was wetter than when she'd put it out. The cranes stood over all of it and creaked, and the loads went across the sky on them in nets.

Pask's had a gate of bars with a chain on it, newer than the gate. There'd been a board nailed to the bars with something chalked on it, and the rain had had the chalk, all but a P. Through the bars, black cinder, with weeds coming up in a ring round where something had stood, and a rack against the wall with nothing on it, and a boy's shoe on its side by the drain with the sole come away.

Eli took the chain in his hand, and held it, and let it go. It swung and knocked on the bars.

"Something's happened to the man," he said. "That's all it is. Something's happened."

Teo had sat down where he was, with his back to Pask's wall and his legs out in the orange water.

"Teo."

"A minute."

"Not there. Not in that." Eli looked up the lane, and down it. The rain came straight down now, in rods, and his hands had gone stupid with it. Across the Cut and a little way up, a crane stood dead, its arm out over a yard and its hook rusted solid on a short chain. The yard under it had a gate of sheet iron, and the gate stood open a hand's width. Past it he could see packed earth, swept, with water standing on it in sheets, and a long shed with a roof.

Nobody in it. No light.

"There," he said. "Up you come. Look. A roof."

---

It was a roof over nothing they could use. The shed was open along the front and stacked full to the back, pallets of pig iron in bars, grey, each pallet as high as Eli's chest and every one wrapped round with wire, and the stacks went right up under the eaves so that the only dry was a strip a hand wide along the front, where the water came off the roof in a sheet.

Eli stood Teo against the gatepost, inside, in the dead crane's shadow, which was no use to anyone. He took in the yard. Swept earth, puddled. A water butt. A bench. A second, smaller shed at the far end with its door open on dark. The rain was going in his collar and down his back and he had stopped feeling it there. The front stack. Take the top off it, and the next, and there'd be floor under the eaves, and iron at the back to sit against.

Nobody looking.

He reached.

He did it the way he'd done the crate, without deciding, and it came the way the crate had, the first pallet off the top of the front stack, all of it, the wire and the bars and the wet wood under them, tearing up off the one below with a noise of iron unsticking from iron. It came too hard. The near corner hit the eave and the whole roof rang like a dropped pan, and he dragged it out from under, wide, swinging, the bars grinding in their wire and the water sheeting off the top of it, and slung it out over the yard toward the bench, more or less. Easy. So heavy it pulled at the backs of his eyes, and easy. His eyes were on Teo.

"There," he said. "See. It's all right. I've got it. I've got—"

Round the corner of the far shed came a pot.

A big black pot with steam coming off its lid in the rain, and a woman carrying it by both handles against her stomach with her elbows out, and behind her four boys with their shirts over their heads, running, and one of them laughing and one of them shouting something about his feet, and all of them coming across the yard straight under it.

They looked up.

Eli felt them look up. Every one of their faces turned up into the rain at the thing in the air, and the woman's face, and the wet whites of their eyes, and the thing in the air came down a foot, all at once, and stopped with a jerk that went through his teeth, and tipped. The near side dropped. The wire sang and one strand of it went with a crack, and the top bars slid, the whole layer of them sliding together toward the low side, and the pallet swung on nothing, out, away from him, toward the boys.

"Put it down," somebody said.

He couldn't. There was nothing to put. He had hold of it and he had nothing, both, and it was going, and the boys were under it, and the woman had the pot down on the ground and had stepped in front of the first boy with her arms out.

Then it stopped.

Dead. The bars that were sliding stopped sliding, half off, and hung there in the air past the end of the wood with nothing holding them. The pallet stopped in the middle of the swing, tipped, and stayed tipped. And the rain stopped.

All of it. Everywhere in the yard. The rain stopped in the air, every drop of it, a whole yard of grey rods standing still from the eaves to the ground, and the drops on the woman's face stopped running and the water off the roof stood out in a sheet, and there was no sound at all, not the rain, not the cranes, not his own breath. And his own reach, the thing he had, whatever it was, was not his. Something had closed on it. It had been shut in a hand, quite gently, the way you'd close your hand on a wasp against a pane to put it out, and held, and he could feel the size of the hand. There was no end to it.

One.

The rain came down.

All at once, everywhere, with a noise, and the roof sheet broke and splashed and the boys' shirts were wet again, and the pallet was turning in the air, level, slowly, with the loose bars riding back up onto it, and it went across the yard and down by the bench, and the wood touched the earth, and settled, without a sound. Set down like a cup.

Eli's knees went. He sat in the water.

The far shed. The door that was open on dark. There was a man sitting in it, on the floor just inside, out of the rain, with his legs crossed and his hands on his knees and his eyes shut. Old, and thin, all knuckle and neck, his hair cut close and gone white, and sitting the way he must have been sitting when Eli looked at that door and saw dark.

He hadn't stood up. He didn't now.

He put his thumb to his nose and took it away and looked at it, and wiped it down the leg of his trousers, slow, once. Then he put the heels of both hands into his eyes and rubbed. He went on rubbing.

Eli's nose was running. He put his hand to it, the good one, and it came away red, a lot of red, more than the crate, and the rain thinned it on his palm to pink. His stomach came up. He got onto his hands and was sick into the puddle, twice, nothing in it but water, and his head was beating behind both eyes and down into his jaw so that his teeth hurt, and his bad hand, under him in the mud, was shut.

Shut so hard the knuckles stood up white. He tried to open it, and it stayed.

The boys were looking at the far shed. Then the tall one looked at Eli.

"Did you see the size of that?"

"Inside," said the woman. "All of you. Inside. Go on." She had the boys behind her still, all four, without seeming to have done it. Her eyes weren't on the pallet, or on Eli in the mud, or the blood on him, or the coat. They were on the gatepost, where Teo was standing with his back flat to it and his good hand spread on the metal behind him.

"Is he yours?" she said. "He's gone grey."

---

The boys went as far as the long shed's eaves and stood in the strip of dry in a row, and watched him. The one who'd spoken was the tallest, a long thin boy of fifteen or sixteen, and he watched Eli the whole time.

The woman had picked her pot up again. She turned to the old man in the doorway, and he took his hands out of his eyes and looked at the pallet, and she took the pot in past him and was gone.

Eli got the bad hand open. He did it with the other one, kneeling, prising the fingers up off the palm one at a time, the last one hardest, and there was nothing in the palm, only mud, and the marks of his own nails gone in deep.

"Who sent you," the old man said.

It wasn't a question the way he said it. He said it to the yard. He had a dry, light voice that went no further than it had to.

Eli stood up. He did it in two goes. He wiped his nose on the back of his wrist and his wrist came away red and he put it behind him.

"I'm sorry. About the—I didn't think there was anyone. I'd never have, if I'd— I'm sorry. Is everyone all right?" He turned to the row of boys. "Are you all right?"

The row said nothing. The tall one grinned.

"There was meant to be a man. Who'd find us, he'd—" There wasn't a name to put to him. "And then they said Pask, and Pask's dead, and his yard's—" He pointed down the lane, through the gate. "So."

The old man waited. Nothing else came, and he let it go, and looked out of the gate at the rain.

"You lifted it blind," he said. "You opened your eyes and it fell."

Eli laughed. It hurt his face. "I didn't have my eyes shut."

The old man waited.

"But that's it," Eli said. "That's exactly what it does. Somebody looks and it just goes—and you—" He came a step across the yard. "The rain. What was that? I've never seen anyone— You're a teacher. You're the teacher. Aren't you? You're who he meant."

"No."

"No you're not, or—"

"No."

The old man got up. All at once, without his hands, from crossed legs to standing. Shorter standing than he'd looked sitting. He came to the doorway and stopped there, under the lintel, out of the rain, and looked past Eli's shoulder at the gate.

"Don't use more than you can control," he said.

"I know. I know, I—"

"You don't. Most masters barely teach. They stand you in a yard and they let you lift. I've buried the ones I taught."

He let a while go by.

"The ones who wanted more than they could hold went down to the cranes. Got the chip."

The rain came off the end of the roof in its sheet.

Eli heard it. He heard "barely teach", and how quietly it was said, and he went warm all through. That was what the good ones always said. Old Hesk at the riding school had said he was no sort of teacher, and he'd been the best Eli ever had.

"Then barely teach me," he said, and smiled. "I'm quick. Honestly. Anything I've ever been shown, I've had it in a week. Ask anyone."

Nothing.

"I can pay. I'll pay you. Whatever Pask's boys paid, double, three times. I haven't got it on me, there's an account, I'll have it rung through the minute I can find somewhere that—"

"No."

"I'll wait, then. I don't mind waiting. I'll wait as long as you like. You'll see."

The old man turned round and went back into the dark of the shed. He left the door as it was. He went in, and sat down, cross-legged, just inside, where he'd been, and put his hands on his knees.

From the gatepost, Teo said, "Eli."

Eli went to him. Teo's lips had gone pale at the corners and his eyes were wet and too bright, and the arm was out of the shirt, hanging, the tape grey and the fingers past the tape fat and shining.

"We'll go out," Eli said. "Just to the gate. Just outside it. He wants to see who stays, that's all this is." He got Teo's good arm over his shoulder. "And I'm terrible at going home. Ask anyone."

Behind them, under the eaves, the tall boy said something, and one of the others laughed.

---

They sat against the yard's front wall, outside, to the left of the gate, where the sheet iron came down to a lip of concrete a foot wide and the dead crane's arm went over above. It kept nothing off. Across the Cut, Pask's chain knocked on Pask's gate whenever the wind came down the lane. The horns went at the cranes, a long one and then a short one, and the light changed, red to brown to a kind of nothing that was neither, and the rain went on.

Teo sat with his knees up and the bad arm across them and his head back against the iron.

"How many have you got," he said.

Eli felt in the coat, the other pocket, and found the pills loose at the bottom, and the twist, damp, and put them in his lap. Two white. The three yellow in their twist.

"Two," he said. "And the yellow."

"Two left," Teo said, and shut his eyes.

The gate opened. It was the woman, with a bowl and a cup and a blanket over her shoulder with burn holes in it. She was big in the forearms, with her hair up in a net, and there was a scar across the back of one wrist, shiny and pink, the shape of a stove's rim. About thirty. She stood over them in the rain without a coat.

She gave the cup to Teo. She put it into his good hand and closed his fingers on it with hers.

"Drink that," she said. "Not fast."

Water. Teo looked at it. He drank, and stopped, and drank, and it ran out of the corner of his mouth into his collar and he let it, and she stood over him in the rain until he'd got to the bottom.

"There," she said, and took the cup back.

The bowl was for Eli. Broth, with things in it. She held it out to him and he took it in the good hand and said, "That's very kind, honestly, you shouldn't have, you've got all those boys to—"

"It's a pot, love," she said.

She put the blanket over Teo's knees, smelling of smoke and grease, and tucked it down the side where the wind came, once.

She looked down at Eli. "He said no to you, did he?"

"For now."

"He says no to the weather." She glanced up at the rain.

"So he doesn't mean it."

She looked at him a bit longer, not unkind, and then she went back in, and the gate shut, and opened again a hand's width the way it had been.

Inside, a boy shouted for Seffa, and she said, "I heard."

Eli ate the broth. Hot. He'd forgotten things could be hot. He ate all of it and held the bowl in both hands for the last of the warm.

"Seffa," he said, trying it. "She's marvellous. Isn't she? Did you see her, with the pot? Stepped right in front of them."

Teo was asleep, or doing it well.

---

The night didn't get darker than brown. The sky sat low over the cranes, lit from under by the fires in the sheds, and the rain came out of it orange where the fires were and grey where they weren't. The cranes went on all night, the creak and the chains, loads going over that nobody was watching.

The headache had gone. The old one. He found the place behind his eye where it had lived since the Spur, and there was nothing in it. Only his face, and his teeth, and the beat in his nose. It had stopped somewhere in the black between the two worlds and he hadn't felt it go.

Teo was shaking. It came up from his knees the way it had on the bench and went into his teeth, and he held the bad arm against himself under the blanket and shook, with his eyes shut, making no sound.

Eli took the coat off. He got the letter out of the inside pocket first and put it inside his shirt, against his skin, and it was cold there and then it wasn't. He got the sock out and put it in his trouser pocket and it pulled the trousers down on that side. Then he put the coat over Teo, over the blanket, and tucked the long skirt of it down under Teo's boots.

Teo didn't open his eyes.

"No," he said.

"It's done. It's on you now."

Teo's good hand came out and found the lapel and held it.

Eli sat back against the iron in his shirt. The rain came straight through it. He put his arms round his knees, and in a while he stopped being able to feel his hands.

There was a light in the yard. He leaned and put his eye to the gap in the gate. Across the swept earth, in the far shed, a lamp sat on the floor turned low, and the old man sat by it with a cup. The cup went up to his mouth and came down and went up again, and on the way it shook, and the lamplight shook on the water in it. He put the cup on the floor. He put that hand under the other one, on his knee, and sat like that. After a while the lamp went out.

Eli sat back.

He could see the rain if he shut his eyes. The whole yard of it, standing. That wasn't lifting. He'd lifted a chair and a crate and a loader and a pallet, and thought that was what it was, and how big. And that old man had put his hand round all the rain in a yard and the pallet and Eli, all at once, and put it down. And not stood up.

He'd teach him that. He'd teach him all of it. And then there'd be nothing, nothing anyone could do, and the next time somebody held a cutter at his ear—

He stopped that.

"He'll teach me," he said, to the dark. "You'll see. Everything."

Teo said nothing. Under the coat his teeth had stopped.

---

The second day was cold.

The rain had stopped before the light came, and the cold came down off the slag with the light, and the puddles in the Cut skinned over, and when Eli tried to stand he found he had to do it in three parts and the middle part hurt. His shirt had dried on him stiff. His fingers were white to the second knuckle.

Teo took the last two pills out of the coat pocket himself and held them in his palm, white on the grey of his skin, and put them in his mouth, and swallowed, and swallowed again. Eli was looking at the gate.

"Day two," Eli said. "That's nothing. That's barely anything. He's seeing if I mean it."

Inside the yard someone was hitting a stovepipe with a hammer, slow, a stroke and a wait and a stroke, and Seffa's voice was telling the stove what it was. "Ten years," it said. "Ten years I've fed you."

The gate opened and Seffa came out with two cups, steaming. She gave one to Teo, and the other she held out to Eli, and he put out the bad hand for it without thinking, and it came out of his lap white and stiff and the fingers stayed where they were. He tried, and the cup sat on his flat palm and tilted, and stopped tilting, and stood there, upright, a little off true. He hadn't done that. Seffa frowned at it. Then she put her own hand under it anyway.

"Other one," she said.

He took it in the other one. "It does that. It's fine. It was further yesterday. Further than this, I mean—it's the cold."

"Is it."

She crouched down by Teo. She waited until Teo had drunk some, and then she said, "Show me," and Teo moved the arm under the coat, back, away from her, against his side.

She waited.

Eli started to say something about the arm, about the pilot and the blade and what a good clean job it had been, and she lifted one finger off her knee without looking round, and he stopped.

Teo brought the arm out.

She didn't touch it. She studied the tape, and the fingers coming out of the tape, fat and tight and gone a colour, and she leaned in close and breathed in through her nose, and sat back on her heels.

"That's not a cut, love," she said. "That's a smell." She pushed his sleeve back with one finger, at the wrist, and Teo let her, and she went on pushing it, up, past the elbow, and the line went up past the elbow and on, and the sleeve wouldn't go any further. "How long's that been there?"

She was asking Teo. Her eyes were on him and she was asking him, and then she was waiting, with her hands on her knees, and Eli waited for Teo to say "I'm fine," and it never came.

"Three days," Teo said. "Four." He moved his shoulder under the shirt. "It's past the shoulder."

Eli hadn't known that. Under the collar on that side, against the neck, the skin was pink in a way the other side wasn't.

Seffa stood up. "There's a clinic at the arch."

"They log it," said Teo.

"So they do." She looked at Eli then, for the first time that morning, and kept on looking, and went back in.

The hammering inside stopped, and started again.

---

She came out at midday with a pot and went off down the Cut with it, and came back an hour after with the pot empty, and a man came up the lane behind her, fast and loose, a big young man in an oilskin with the hood down, younger than any of the pad gang, laughing at something before he'd got near enough for anyone to have said it, and Seffa walked ahead of him with the empty pot against her stomach and didn't turn round.

"Seff. Seff. Listen."

"Go back to your shift."

"I'm on my break. Listen." He saw Eli and Teo against the wall and his face went bright. His eyes went to the coat on Teo, and to Seffa, and off it. "Hello. Who's this? You're the one. You're him. Lifted half the old man's iron, they said, the whole pallet, I heard it down at four." He laughed. Behind his ear the hair was cut away in a square and the scar stood up white in the cold. "Was it heavy? I bet it wasn't heavy for you."

"Hollin," Seffa said.

There was a bar of pig iron in the lane, come off a barrow some other day and left, half sunk in the frozen orange mud. Hollin grinned at Eli, and the bar came up out of the mud with a suck, and hung in front of his face, turning, with the mud dropping off it in clods. No hand out. No eye on it. All of him was on Eli, to see Eli see.

His nose started to bleed. One side. It came down over his lip and he licked it and laughed.

"See? Nothing. Easy. I could do your whole pallet. I could do six." The bar turned. "I'll show him. Seff, tell him, I'll show the old man, you tell him I'll do the whole shed for him, for nothing, he can—"

"Put it down."

She hadn't raised her voice the first time. She raised it now. It went up the lane and off the slag.

"Put it down, you stupid great—look at your face. You're stood in the road with your nose going like a tap, showing off to a stranger, for what?" She jerked her head at the gate. "He told you. He told you, and you took a year's money down to Ferrel. Put it down. Go back to your shift. Go and lift something."

The bar dropped. It went into the mud on its end and stood there.

Hollin wiped his lip with the back of his hand and checked the red on it and laughed. He laughed a bit longer than there was anything to laugh at, and then he stopped, all at once.

"Bring the pie Thursday," he said. "The one with the onions."

He went off down the lane at the same loose fast pace he'd come up it.

Seffa stood with the empty pot. She stood quite still. Then she went in through the gate, and it shut.

Teo was watching the bar standing in the mud.

"Behind his ear," he said.

"I saw," Eli said. "I know. I saw." His eyes were on it too. "You could see it was nothing to him. Did you see? Nothing at all."

---

Toward the end of the day the tall boy came and leaned in the gap of the gate and looked at Eli.

He had an end of bread and he ate it, looking. He had a cold sore on his lip and very big hands.

"He's not going to," he said. "You know that."

"He might."

"He told me no. For a year he told me no. I've been here two years." The boy finished his bread. "He doesn't even let us lift. You know what we do all day? We sit." He laughed, a short one. "On the floor. Eyes shut. Listen to the cranes, he says. Tell me which one's carrying. That's it, that's all of it."

Eli laughed, and the boy grinned at him, pleased.

"And he can do that," Eli said. "Can't he. What he did. The rain."

The boy turned and looked back into the yard, at the far shed, over his shoulder. Then back at Eli.

"I never saw him do anything before," he said. "Not in two years. Not once."

He went back in. Eli sat with that.

In a while he said, "Teo."

"Mm."

"Two years. And never once." He was smiling. "And then I come up the lane, and the first thing—the very first day."

The light was going brown. The horns went at the cranes, the long one and the short. Seffa came out with the blanket from the day before, dried, warm, smelling worse of smoke, and swapped it for the wet one without a word and took the wet one in.

"How many have you got," Eli said, when she'd gone. He'd remembered all at once. "The pills."

Teo let a breath go.

"None," he said.

---

That night Eli talked.

Teo wasn't sleeping. Eli could hear him not sleeping, and the shaking had come back and gone and come back.

"There'll be a call out," he said. "As soon as he's said. There'll be a way to ring from here, there always is, and they'll fly someone. Not some arch. The best one there is. And they'll take you somewhere with a window, Teo. A proper one, all down one side. You'll lie there and look out of it and get fat, and I'll come every day after I've done here, and tell you everything he's shown me, and you'll be bored stiff."

Teo's breath went in and out.

"And Seffa," Eli said. "Did you hear her this morning, at the stove? It's never drawn right in ten years, she said. I'm going to have them send a stove. No, a kitchen. A whole kitchen, the new kind, the kind Dace's cook has. Can you imagine her face. She'd hit me with it."

Across the lane the wind got into Pask's chain.

"It's past the shoulder," Teo said.

"I know. I know it is. That's what I'm saying, that's why the call—that's why it has to be the best, not some—" He stopped. He'd lost what he'd been going to say. "When he's said yes."

Teo was quiet after that.

The gate opened behind them, in the dark, a little wider than its hand. Seffa stayed in the gap. She stood in the brown light from the fires with her arms folded and looked at the two of them against the wall, and Eli could see her breath.

"Who's minding him," she said, "while you're minding the gate?"

"I am. I'm right here."

She stayed there a while.

"There's a gnome," she said. "Over on Ammet. Next one over, where the ore boats go. He sits with the dying." She said it flat, the way she'd said there was a clinic. "I took my mother. Last winter."

"And she—"

"She died," Seffa said. "She died well. He was good to her." She unfolded her arms. "He's not a doctor. Some of them get up."

"Teo's not dying," Eli said.

She looked at him in the dark. Then at Teo, who had his eyes open now and was looking back at her over the coat's collar.

"No," she said. "I'd not leave it, though."

She went in.

---

The third day it rained again, fine and cold, hanging more than falling.

Teo got up to piss and fell down.

He got onto his knees and then onto his feet, with his good hand on the iron, and turned toward the lane, and his legs went out from under him sideways, both together, and he went down on his side in the mud at the foot of the wall with the coat under him and the bad arm thrown out. The good hand never went out to catch him. He just went.

"Teo."

He was lying in the orange water with his face in it. Eli got him over. His eyes were open and they went past Eli and didn't come back, and they were wrong, too bright, and moving, and he was hot, so hot that the rain on his face was steaming off it at the cheekbones. His lips moved. Eli bent to them.

"—the cup," Teo said. "Put the cup. She'll want her—"

"Teo. It's me. Teo."

"—the brush. On the step. Nine. He's to be there at nine."

The yellow pills. Eli got the twist out of the coat pocket, his hands useless, and the paper tore and one went in the mud and he left it, and he got the other two between Teo's teeth and held his jaw shut, and Teo's throat didn't move. Eli held his jaw. He put his face down by Teo's face and said, "Swallow, swallow, please, Teo, swallow it," and the throat went once.

The gate was a hand's width open.

He got up. Nobody decided it. He went to the gate and put the flat of his hand on it and it swung in, and he went into the yard, into the swept earth with the water standing on it in sheets, past the pallet where it had been set down, past the boys in their row under the eaves, sitting on the ground with their eyes shut, and the tall one opening his. Across to the far shed and the door that was open on dark.

The old man was sitting in it, just inside, with his legs crossed.

Eli stopped at the door, in the rain. His shirt was mud down one side and blood down the front from the first day, gone brown, and he had nothing in his hands.

"Not me," he said. "Him."

The old man looked at him. The first time. At Eli's face, not past it, and it was only a look, and then it went past him, out of the door, across the yard to the gate.

He got up from the floor without his hands.

He came out into the rain and across the yard at an old man's pace, past the boys, who had all opened their eyes, and out of the gate, and Eli came after him. In the lane he stood over Teo in the mud. He stayed standing. He took in the arm thrown out, the tape, the fingers, the shoulder of the shirt where Eli had pulled the collar open and the pink went up the neck now under the jaw. A long time, and he touched nothing.

Then he turned his head and looked across the lane at Pask's gate.

Behind him, in the gate, Seffa was already in her coat. She had a bag over her shoulder, a canvas one, done up, as if it had been done up since the night before, by the door.

"I'll take him," she said.

The old man brought his eyes back from Pask's.

"Seffa takes him," he said. "Now."

"I'm going with him," Eli said.

Nobody answered that. Seffa came out past the old man and crouched in the mud and got Teo's good arm across her shoulders, and Eli got the other side, and the bad arm hung, and between them they got him up onto his feet, his head down, his boots dragging and then finding the ground and dragging. Heavier than he looked. He'd always been heavier than he looked.

The old man stood in the rain and watched the gate.

"Bring him back breathing," he said. "Then we'll see."

Eli turned to him across Teo's bent head. He was grinning. He could feel it, with Teo's weight on him and Teo's breath going short and hot against his neck.

"That's a yes," he said. "Isn't it? Is that a yes? Because I waited, you saw I waited, I said I'd—"

"Waiting's not why."

The old man turned and went back in through his gate. He left it a hand's width open.

Teo's head came up. He turned it on Seffa's shoulder, slowly, and looked at Eli. His eyes had come back. They were wet and very tired and they were his own, and he looked at Eli from a foot away and held it there.

"Right," Teo said, and turned his face into Seffa's shoulder.

---

The ore boat went at the next horn but one. A barge with a lid, orange with rust, at the far end of the field, and Seffa knew the man on the ramp and the man on the ramp knew Seffa, and nobody wrote anything down. Eli got the sock out and stood holding it out on the ramp.

"He eats off my pot," Seffa said, and looked at the sock. "Keep that."

They put Teo down in the hold on a heap of empty sacks with the coat over him and the blanket over the coat. Seffa sat at his head with her back to the hull and her bag on her knees. She'd brought bread. She'd brought the cup.

"Four days," she said, to nobody, to the hull. "There, and him seen, and back. Four. They'll have to make their own dinner." She thought about that. "They'll eat the onions raw."

The barge shuddered. The lid came down overhead, a long way, slowly, and the light went brown and then went, and then a lamp came on, a yellow one, on a hook by the steps.

Eli sat on the sacks at Teo's feet in his shirt, in the cold. His teeth were going. He put his hand inside the shirt. The letter had gone soft and taken the curve of his ribs, and one corner had come to pulp where the wet got in. Teo's boots were against his leg.

"He said yes," Eli said. "Did you hear him? I told you he would."

Teo lay with his eyes shut.

Seffa looked at Eli across him, in the yellow light. She had a look on her he couldn't read. It went on a while. Then she reached over and pulled the coat up higher on Teo's shoulder, to the neck, over the pink, and tucked it, and took her hand back.

The bad hand was lying in Eli's lap. He closed it, and it went partway, and stopped, the fingers curled, and he left it like that and watched it, under the lamp, while the floor began to hum.
