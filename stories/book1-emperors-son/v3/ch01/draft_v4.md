## ONE · The Gift

The ground in the Scald stayed warm all night, and nobody down there had ever found out why.

Vosk came up through it on foot with the dwarf's price in his head. Eleven hundred, she'd said, in marks, not imperial, and she'd said it to the wall behind him while she wiped her hands. He had forty-one marks in a sock under his bunk and it had taken him nine years. He had come off the evening shift and gone straight down to her, and now he was coming back with the night gone, past the shut stalls and the sleepers on their mats, feet tucked into each other's coats for the company, under pipes that sweated something with a skin on it, and the heat came up through the soles of his boots the whole way and into his shins, and his shins ached with it the way they ached at the end of a double shift, which was what he had in front of him.

Nearer the Tower the air didn't sit right. It had a shine in it at head height. Bits of light drifted in the cross-streets, slow, none bigger than a seed, and they cast no shadow on anything they passed. A girl of about eight stood in the mouth of an alley with a net of fine wire on a pole, waiting for one to come close enough. Her hands were wrapped to the elbow in rag. She didn't look at him. You didn't touch drift with skin. Everybody knew someone who had.

There were humans down here at this hour too, which was the joke of the place. Clean coats, soft shoes, standing with their foreheads to the old walls and their eyes shut, palms flat on the stone, waiting for something. Three of them in a row outside the bathhouse that had been dry since before Vosk was born. They paid orc children to mind their shoes. One of the children had already sold one of the shoes and was minding the other with great seriousness.

People who lived on the old ground got sick in ways the clinics up top didn't have names for. He passed a woman whose jaw had kept growing down one side until it had shut her eye. She was selling batteries off a blanket and she said good morning to him, though it wasn't morning yet, and he said it back. Two small boys sat on a step sharing a cake, and the nearer one held it in a hand where the fingers had grown together into one flat piece, and broke it on his knee, and gave the bigger half away.

He came round the corner into Pole Street and stopped, because everyone else had.

There were maybe forty orcs in the street, and all of them were on their knees with their faces turned to the wall, men and women, a few children. Nobody was talking. The heat was worse here, and it came in gusts, out of a doorway on the far side where a curtain of plastic strips hung down.

He got down. He put his face to the wall like the rest and showed his hands.

Someone was walking up the line. He heard the boots stop and go on, stop and go on. Young men's boots, light, quick, and when they stopped beside him he saw from the corner of his eye a pair of shins in yard trousers, and a hand hanging loose with a cutter in it, the kind you used for strapping. The hand had wire wound round the knuckles. Up the arm, at the back of the young man's neck where the chip scar should have been flat and pale, the skin had been opened and stitched shut again with the same wire, tight, in neat crosses, and the stitches wept.

"You're up late, Terrace," the young man said.

Vosk said nothing. The young man smelled his collar, actually leaned down and smelled it, and laughed through his nose and moved on.

Behind the plastic strips, something big shifted its weight, and the floor told him about it.

They stayed on their knees until the boots had gone back inside. Then a long time after that. When people did start getting up, they did it one at a time and without looking at each other, and went off in different directions, and the woman next to Vosk, who was old and had burns up both hands to the wrist, stayed down a little longer, rocking. When she got up she put her thumb to the back of his neck, on his scar, and pressed. He did it back. Her scar was hot.

"Eating," she said. "He's eating tonight."

"I didn't see."

"No," she agreed.

She walked with him as far as the foot of the Long Stair, because it was on her way, and on the way she sang, not loud, the one about Hurrak who walked out of the yard. Vosk had heard it all his life and never all of it. Nobody sang all of it. Hurrak walked out of the yard and the dogs didn't bark, and he walked past the gate and the gate didn't bite, and he walked out of the city and kept walking. In the version his mother had sung there was a part about him carrying his own name in his mouth like a stone so he wouldn't lose it, and the old woman left that part out, and he didn't ask her why.

At the foot of the stair she said, "Walk well," and went off into the Scald with her burnt hands in her armpits.

---

The Long Stair was four thousand and some steps, and there was a reader at every landing. A ring of dark glass set in the wall at eye height for a human, at the chest for him. When you passed one, it looked. With a human it was nothing, Vosk had been told: a light, a name maybe, a little sound. With an orc the chip in the back of the neck woke up and answered it, and the answer went cold down both your arms into your hands, every hundred steps, all the way up. You got used to that.

The readers could also tell the chip to hurt you. That you didn't get used to.

Halfway up, the stair left the old ground and went through the sumps, where the city's water came back down to be cleaned. Then the foundations. Then the bottom of the mid-levels, where it was suddenly cooler and there were lights that worked, and grey walls with the Emperor's seal stamped on them at every landing, an oval with nothing in it.

The mid-levels were never all the way asleep. The traffic lanes ran overhead in strings of light, three deep, the lowest close enough that you could hear the hulls tick as they cooled, and the feeds ran on every wall with the sound off. PLAGUE HOLD EXTENDED, THREE SYSTEMS. LEGION RECRUITMENT AT RECORD. TERRACE PROPERTY VALUES. Somebody had thrown something at the ribbon and it had stuck there, brown, over the word VALUES.

The ground up here was wrong too, only quieter about it. Nobody built a floor to lean, and the floors leaned anyway, a little, all of them the same way, so that a drink spilled on the Spur ran off toward the Tower whichever way the street went. Everybody had the headache. It sat behind the eyes and in the ears, all day, every day, and there was a stall on every landing selling a blue pill for it that didn't work, and people bought the pill and complained to each other about it instead of saying good morning. In the gutter water now and then there was a thin smear of light that went when you looked at it straight, and the cleaners hosed it down the drains with everything else. Plants in window boxes grew sideways. Dogs didn't last.

The flesh market at Ninth Spur was opening. Vosk cut through it, because it was quicker and the stalls were full of men too busy to see him. They were hosing down the slabs. Steam came off the water. The cold-cases were going up on their trestles, and every case had a stamp on the end the size of a hand, black on white, CLONE STOCK — NO SOUL, with the grade under it. A woman in an apron was laying out tongues in rows on ice, and a man at the next stall was sorting a basket of eyes by colour, and a dwarf further down sold hands in pairs, matched, with a little paper tag on each thumb. Vosk had worked a season here when he was small, carrying ice. He knew what an orc arm sold for, which was less than a goat's.

At the end of the row a man was shouting, "Real! Real, not clone, real!" and a young woman with a shopping bag stopped and asked him, quite politely, how she was meant to tell.

"Costs more, doesn't it," the man said.

She seemed to think that was fair, and bought some.

The lift at the top of the Spur had a core, a grey lump the size of a loaf in a glass box, wet, with threads going out of it into the works, and when the lift was coming down the lump twitched, very slightly, the way cut meat twitches on a block. Orcs didn't ride the lift.

He took the culvert.

From the culvert's mouth you could see the Tower, all of it, if you leaned back. You couldn't anywhere else in the mid-levels. It came up out of the middle of everything, out of the oldest ground, pale and seamless and so big that the clouds broke round it, and the lanes bent away from it in a wide circle, and nothing anybody had built came within a mile of its foot. The rich said it was warm on your face. They said it the way they said a good wine was warm. The Terrace was built on the first rise of land that looked at it, and the houses there had their best windows facing it, and people paid for those windows more than Vosk could count.

He leaned back and looked at it, because it was there. A tall pale thing. He didn't feel anything.

---

The service gate for Ninth Terrace was a slot in a wall at the top of the culvert, one orc wide. Vosk was fourteen minutes past the count.

He knew it before he got to the slot, because the slot knew, and the chip knew, and it started before he could get his shoulder through. It always started in the jaw. It felt like biting on foil, and then the foil was in the bones of his face and down his neck, and then it went into the long muscles of his back and pulled them all at once in the same direction. He hit the wall of the slot with his head. It never lasted long. When it let go he was on his knees in the slot with his mouth full of spit and his hands open in front of him, the way they taught you to hold them, and somewhere far off the gate said, in a pleasant voice, "Late. Logged."

He got up. He went through.

The Terrace was the other side of the slot, all at once, every time. Water ran down channels cut in the white stone. There were trees, real ones, with real dirt round their feet that orcs changed every month. The air was cool and smelled of nothing much, which cost more than any smell. In the first-floor window of the third house, the Ilders', a boy of about nine was eating breakfast in a white shirt, and down on the wet lawn the family's yard orc, a grown man, was hopping. Every time a foot came down on the grass it jerked up again as if the grass were a stove, and the other one came down and jerked, and he went on like that, faster, with his arms clamped to his sides and his face screwed shut. The boy had his spoon in one hand and his thumb on a fob in the other. He wasn't laughing. He was interested.

Vosk kept walking.

In the barracks under the long house, the others were already up and going. Somebody had kept him a bit of bread. He ate it sitting on his bunk with his hand flat on the mattress, over the sock.

Then he went out into the yard and stood under the third window from the end on the second floor, which had been the same window since he was twelve, and found a bit of gravel at the edge of the path, and threw it.

---

The money came in a dwarven purse, the kind with a clasp that bit, and Eli had put it on the kitchen table next to the fruit bowl so he could see it while he talked.

"Eleven hundred," he said. "In marks."

Teo was sitting on the counter by the cold-box with his heels against the doors and his slate on his knee, eating an apple from the bottom up, which was how he'd eaten them since they were seven. With his other thumbnail he was working at the skin down the side of his thumb. He did that when he read. He'd done it through every exam they'd ever sat, and the thumb had never once been healed.

"From who?"

"The account." Eli turned the purse over. It was heavier than he'd expected money to be. Money was a number on a slate; he'd never held eleven hundred of anything. "I asked at breakfast and it came back before he'd put the slate down. Then somebody rang the house."

"Who?"

"To check the figure. A woman. She had him say it again, and he said it again, and she said thank you and that was it." Eli laughed. "Nobody ever rings him. He's been in a mood all day."

"What did she want?"

"The figure. I just said."

Teo looked at the purse for a while. Then he went back to the slate.

The account had come with the house, and the house had come with Eli, before he could remember. It paid the steward and the cook and the tutors, when there had been tutors, and the orcs in the barracks, and it had paid for his first flyer and his second, and for the window that had gone in after he put a chair through it at fifteen. It had never asked what anything was for. Your father's people, the steward called it, whenever it did something, and Eli had stopped asking which people a long time ago.

"He asked you for money," Teo said.

"A loan."

"A loan." Teo looked at the apple. "He didn't ask you to come."

"He didn't have to." Eli got up and went to the window and came back. "On his own? At night? With that on him, to go and sit in some dwarf's back room and let her into his neck?" He laughed. "They'd have the boots off him before the second landing. He's never had eleven hundred of anything. He'd carry it in his hand."

Teo finished the apple and looked round for somewhere to put the core. Eli didn't look up. The core came out of Teo's fingers and went across the kitchen, not fast, and out of the open yard door, and dropped on the step, where somebody would sweep it.

Teo looked at his empty hand. Then he put the thumb in his mouth.

On the wall behind him the feed was running with the sound off. Grey men at a grey table. The ribbon underneath said ELVEN DELEGATION RECALLED and then THE QUEEN DECLINES TO COMMENT and then something about rates.

The letter was under the fruit bowl.

It had come that morning, by hand. A courier in a grey coat, a woman, who had stood in the doorway and waited, saying nothing, until the steward had fetched Eli down himself, and then had held it out and waited again until it was in his fingers, and then gone. The envelope was heavy and pale and there was nothing written on it, and on the back, pressed into a disc of grey wax, was the seal off the stair landings. He had seen that oval ten thousand times on landings and crates and the backs of buses. He'd never had it handed to him.

He'd looked at it for a while in the hall. Then he'd brought it in here and put the bowl on it.

"Are you going to open that," said Teo.

"Tomorrow." Eli slid the purse into the inside pocket of his coat, and patted it. "If it's his people, they've had twenty-four years. They can have tonight."

There was a knock on the yard door, two short, which was Vosk's knock, and Eli was across the kitchen before the second one had finished.

Vosk filled the door. He always had, since he'd got his growth, and he always ducked coming through it, even though the top of his head cleared the frame by an inch. He was in his work clothes still, the grey canvas, and he had a cut on his forehead that Eli hadn't noticed that morning, scabbed, with grit in it.

"What happened to your head?"

"Wall."

"Sit down. You haven't eaten. There's the end of the bird. Teo, move."

"I'm all right," Vosk said.

"Sit down."

Vosk sat on the step inside the yard door, which was where he sat. The apple core was by his boot, and he moved the boot. Eli put the plate down on the step beside him, and the fork on the plate, and then a cloth, because he was always forgetting the cloth. Then he crouched in front of him with his forearms on his knees.

"Your late's off," he said. "I told him you were with me."

Vosk put two fingers up to the cut on his forehead and took them down again.

"And I've got it." Eli patted the coat. "I'll carry it."

Vosk looked at him. Then he looked past him at Teo.

"It's quicker on my own," Vosk said.

"So we'll be quick."

"I know the way. I go down there."

"I know you go down there. That's how you got your head opened." Eli reached out and put his hand on the back of Vosk's neck, over the scar, and gave it a squeeze and a little shake, as he'd been doing since they were boys. The scar was warm under his palm and a little raised. "I'm not handing eleven hundred marks to a dwarf I've never met. Would you? And nobody's putting a knife in anybody with me stood there." He let go and stood up. "They know what a Terrace coat is."

Vosk had picked up the fork. He turned it over. He put it down on the plate again, very square, next to the bird.

"Eli," he said.

"Eat your bird."

Vosk ate the bird. He ate it slowly, and somewhere near the end of it he stopped chewing, and his head went back against the door frame, and he was asleep, with the fork still in his hand and his mouth a little open.

Eli took the fork out of his hand. He fetched his second-best coat from the hall and put it over him, and it didn't cover much.

Teo looked at the clock on his slate, and then at Eli.

"So she waits," Eli said. He sat down on the kitchen floor with his back to the cold-box, where he could see Vosk's face. "Find us a way down that isn't the stair."

Teo turned the slate round. He'd had it up since the purse came out: the sump crews' rota, two weeks of it, and a shaft off the sumps with a crew change at half past eleven, when nobody would be on the ladder for twenty minutes.

Eli didn't look at it. "Good," he said.

---

They went out through the front a little after eleven.

On the way down the white path they passed the Ilders'. The Ilders' yard man was out in the dark sweeping round the front step, and he was standing on the paving to do it, right at the edge, with his boots an inch off the grass. Eli nodded to him. The man didn't nod back, which was the Ilders all over; they never trained them. The youngest had been given a fob in the spring and you could hear it from the kitchen most mornings, slap, slap on the wet lawn, and somebody ought to have a word with Ilder.

The gate of Ninth Terrace was a ring of dark glass in an arch of white stone, and Eli walked through it without slowing, the way he walked through every door. Something in the glass looked at him. A light came up green on the stone at his shoulder, and a soft voice said, "Good evening," without a name, and the arch let him out onto the public ramp. It did the same for Teo.

Vosk wasn't with them. The front gate didn't read orcs. It bit them.

"That's stupid," Eli had said in the yard, "come through with us, I'll tell them," and Vosk had said, "It bites," and gone down to the culvert, and they found him at the bottom of the public ramp twenty minutes later, standing very still against a pillar where the light didn't reach, with his hands where people could see them.

"See," said Eli, "no trouble," and clapped him on the arm.

The mid-levels at night were louder than by day. Eli had been coming down since he was fifteen, for food, for the clubs on Ancel Row, for no reason. Noise came down from the lanes overhead and up from the streets underneath, and there were so many feeds you could have read by them. Everyone down here had the headache; there was a stall of the blue pills on every landing, and he'd stopped seeing the stalls at about sixteen, and the headache not long after. Every human face that passed under a reader got a little light. Green, green, green. You stopped seeing that too.

They didn't take the Long Stair. Teo took them to a service shaft off the sumps, and stood at the top of it with his slate until the clock turned over, and then went down first without saying anything, and the ladder rungs were slick and warm, and the air got warmer every rung.

At the bottom of the shaft the floor was the wrong size.

It was a street. It had a gutter, and a curb, and on the other side another curb. But the curb on this side came up to Eli's chest, and the one on the far side was a long way off, so far that the lamps strung between them sagged in the middle, and the street between had been filled in with poured stuff to make a floor at a sensible height, and people had built shacks on it, and a ramp went up over the curb made of pallets and somebody's door.

Eli stood with his hands on the curb. The stone was smooth where people had held it getting up the ramp. Teo was already at the top.

The Scald was hot. Eli had known it would be. He hadn't known the heat would have a direction. It came from in front of them and a little up, as if there were a fire ahead on a high shelf, and it pushed on his face and his chest, and the further they went the harder it pushed. He took his coat off and carried it and then put it back on because of the purse. Sweat ran into the small of his back. The air tasted of metal and burning fat. There were a lot of people, orcs mostly, and they moved round Eli in the crowd without touching him, a little gap always opening half a step before he got there and closing behind.

He thought that was courtesy. He said so to Teo, who didn't answer.

Vosk walked in front. He walked differently down here. Looser. His hands weren't held out. A big orc with a split ear stepped into his way and the two of them put their thumbs to each other's necks and said something low in the orc talk, and the other one laughed and looked past Vosk at Eli's coat, and at Eli, and said something else, and Vosk said, "No," quite sharply.

"What did he say?"

"Nothing."

"He said something."

"He asked was you mine."

Eli laughed. "What did you say?"

Vosk walked faster.

The bridge went over a cut in the ground. The cut ran away on both sides, forty feet down, maybe more, and the bottom of it wasn't dark. It was lit from somewhere under it, a dull white that moved, and drift came up out of it in ones and twos. On the edges children stood with nets on long poles, wrapped in rag.

The bridge itself was one long curved girder with a deck laid on top, ribbed like a spine, dark grey going brown, and it went from one side of the cut to the other in one piece. Eli put his hand on it as he stepped up. Then he stopped and put his hand back.

It was warm. Warm like a horse.

"Don't," said Vosk. "Don't touch it."

Hookline was the market the bridge came down into, and it was called that for the hooks: the whole low ceiling hung with them on chains, thousands, and something on half of them. Coats. Lamps. Pots. A whole side of something red and white, sawn down the middle, turning slowly in the heat coming up off the cut, with a paper tag on the shank that said nothing at all, not even a grade. Across the floor from the bridge end, on the far side, there was an old stone arch with plastic strips hung across it, and the heat came out of there harder than anywhere. Music came from three places at once. A dwarf woman sold soup out of a hot-box, and the soup smelled good, and Eli slowed for it.

Teo came up very close beside him.

"Take the coat off," he said. "Carry it inside out."

"It's got the purse in it."

"I know what's in it." Teo didn't move his head. "Every stall, Eli. Nobody's looking at him. They're looking at the coat."

Eli looked round. A man at a stall of pans looked at his pans. Two orc women by a hook of lamps looked at each other. "It's a good coat," he said, and put his arm round Teo's shoulders and gave him a shake. "Look at you. You've gone grey. Vosk, look at him."

Vosk didn't look. He turned off into an alley halfway along the market's side wall, between two stalls, and they followed him.

---

The dwarf was called Velk, or that was what it said on the shutter of the shop she worked out of the back of, which had sold water once and still had the tanks. She was short even for a dwarf and very broad, and she had a scar that ran from her hairline down through one eyebrow and on down her cheek, and the eye under it was a pale grey and didn't move when the other one did.

She looked at Eli. She looked at Teo. She looked at Vosk.

"You said you'd come on your own," she said.

"He's paying," said Vosk.

"He's paying." She looked at Eli again, at his coat, his hands, his boots. "Course he is. You're late." She looked past them at the shutter. "It's hot. I told you it was hot."

"It's boiling," Eli agreed, and flapped his coat.

"Come in. Don't touch anything."

The back of the shop was a long room with a low ceiling and shelves on every wall, and on the shelves were things Eli didn't have names for. A grey cube that sat at a tilt and, while he watched, tilted the other way. A coin standing on its edge on a saucer.

"Where's all this from?"

"Out," said Velk. She was washing her hands at a basin in the corner, fast, with a brush, up to the elbows. "Depot takes a third now. A third. Then his cut. Then whatever he sends up to whoever." She shook the water off. "And they wonder."

"Whose cut?" said Teo.

Velk dried her arms and didn't answer. She pulled a heavy chair into the light, high-backed, with straps on the arms, and nodded at it, and Vosk sat down in it and put his own arms in the straps.

Eli took out the purse.

She counted it on the table in stacks of fifty, wetting her thumb, quicker than he'd ever seen anybody count anything, and swept it into a drawer, and held her hand out again.

"And two," she said.

"It was eleven."

"It was eleven last night." She didn't put the hand down. "Tonight you're late, and it's hot, and there's three of you."

"I haven't got two."

Her live eye went to his wrist.

Eli unbuckled the watch and put it in her hand. It had been a birthday. It kept the time on four worlds he'd never been to and it had a little moon on it that went round. She put it in her apron without looking at it.

At the back of the room a thin dwarf boy of maybe ten was sitting on a stool behind the tanks with a cup. Eli hadn't seen him. The boy looked at Eli's bare wrist. He looked at the pocket of Eli's coat where the purse had been, and at Eli's boots, and at Eli. Then he put down the cup and went out through a curtain at the back.

"Where's he going?" said Teo.

Velk didn't look round. "Tax." She pulled the lamp down on its chain. "Hold his head."

Eli stood there.

"His head. Forward. Chin down. Unless you'd rather do the knife."

Eli held his head.

Up close, Vosk's hair smelled of the barracks soap, and the back of his neck was very hot, and the scar was there under Eli's fingers where it had always been, a pale ridge as long as a thumb. He'd touched it a thousand times. Velk swabbed it with something cold and laid her tools out on a cloth, small bright things, and picked one, and put the point of it in the old scar at the top end and drew it down.

Vosk's whole body went tight in the chair.

There was more blood than Eli had expected, and it was darker. It ran down into the collar of Vosk's work shirt and Velk didn't bother to catch it. She opened the cut with two little hooks and held it, and Eli could see down into it, past the red, to something grey and shining with threads going out from it into the meat like roots. She took hold of it with narrow pliers and began to pull, slowly, turning, and the threads came out one at a time with a sound like wet paper, and Vosk said something through his teeth in the orc talk, the same three words over and over, and his hands in the straps opened and shut.

"Talk to him," said Velk. "If you're going to stand there."

"It's all right," said Eli. "It's all right. You're doing well. You're doing so well."

The thing came out. It was the size of a fingernail, with its threads trailing, and she dropped it in a dish, and in the dish it moved. Once, a little curl of the threads.

Teo had gone to stand by the door. He was watching the shutter.

The new one came out of a sealed packet. It was bigger than the old one, darker, ridged on one side, and someone had taken a file to the mark on it, not well enough. Eli could make out part of a word. LEGION.

"Soldier's," said Velk. "Leash is out."

She pressed it home with her thumb, and it didn't take. The threads on it moved, and stopped, and lay down flat in the wet. She said something short in dwarven and took it out again and opened the cut wider with the hooks, and Vosk's back came up off the chair, and Eli held his head down with both hands, harder than he'd ever held anything. She pushed it in again and held it. This time the threads went, feeling their way down into him, and under Eli's fingers the skin round the cut went hot, and then hotter, so that he nearly let go.

She glanced at Eli with her live eye. "You understand what you've bought him."

"Yes," said Eli.

She looked at him a little longer. Then she closed the cut with glue and a strip of something that pulled tight as it dried, and undid the straps, all of it fast. Vosk didn't get up. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his head hanging, breathing hard, and blood dripped off the end of his nose onto the floor between his boots.

Then he lifted his head.

He didn't look at any of them. He turned his face, slowly, to the far wall of the shop, and he looked at it as though there was a window there.

"What's that," he said.

His voice was strange. Eli crouched in front of him. "What's what?"

"That." Vosk had his eyes wide open. "It's. Up. It's right there."

"Tower," said Velk. She was rolling her tools up in their cloth. "Everybody, first time. You stop noticing. Sun in the next room."

Eli looked at the wall. Shelves, the cube, the coin. His ears had the faint fullness they'd always had, which was nothing, which was the headache, which everybody had.

Vosk put his hand up flat toward the wall and held it there, as if there were a fire. Then his head came round, slowly, and down, toward the floor and the front of the shop and the market beyond it.

"And them," he said. "All of them." He shut his eyes. "It's loud."

"It's lit," said Velk. She tied the roll. "It'll settle." She put the roll under her arm. "Or it won't."

"Is it hot?" Eli said. "Vosk. Does it hurt?"

Vosk opened his eyes and looked at him then. He looked at Eli's face for a long time. Then he looked down at his hand, and he closed the hand.

"No," he said.

Eli laughed out loud. He gripped Vosk's knee. "Look at you. Look at him, Teo."

Teo came across the room. He took hold of Eli's arm above the elbow, not hard. His face was very calm, the calm it went when an exam was going badly.

"Get him up," he said. "We go. Now."

"He's just had his neck opened." Eli shook the hand off, not unkindly. "Give him a minute. Sit, Vosk. Sit."

Teo looked at him. Then he went back to the door and stood there with his arms folded and didn't say anything else.

Velk had the watch out of her apron. She looked at it once, and put it back.

"Out the front," she said. "Not the back."

She went out the back. Through the curtain, already going, she said, "And I didn't fit it."

Vosk was standing. Eli hadn't seen him get up. He was standing very straight with his head a little tilted, and his face was turned toward the front of the shop, and the blood was drying on his lip, and he was listening.

"It's gone quiet," Vosk said.

---

It had. Eli hadn't noticed the market's noise until there wasn't any. No music. No one selling soup. The heat was still there, pushing through the shutter at the front, and a smell had come into it, hot metal and something sweet behind the metal that turned his stomach.

They went out the front.

The alley was empty. The market at the end of it was not. Everybody was still there, the stallholders, the pan man, the two orc women, the soup woman; they were all on their knees, every one, facing the nearest wall. Nobody looked up. On the hooks overhead the meat turned in the heat.

The alley let out halfway along the market's side wall. The bridge end was to their left, thirty steps along the wall, the way they'd come in. The arch was off to the right and across the floor, past the soup stall and the hanging meat, with its plastic strips.

"Bridge," said Teo, very low. "Walk. Don't run."

Eli walked. Teo had him by the sleeve. Vosk was a step behind. They went along the wall past all the bowed heads toward the bridge end, and out of the arch, through the plastic strips, young men began to come.

Six. Eight. Orcs, young, light on their feet, in yard trousers, with wire wound round their knuckles and their necks opened and stitched with wire. They didn't hurry. They came across the market floor between the kneeling people and spread out between Eli and the bridge end, and stopped. The dwarf boy from the shop was with them, at the back. He pointed at Eli, once, and one of the young men put something in his hand, and he went off between the stalls.

One of them, not the biggest, smiled at Vosk and touched his own stitched neck and then pointed at Vosk's, where the new strip was.

"Lit you up like a lamp, Terrace," he said. "Whole market felt it go in."

"It's paid for," said Eli. His voice came out clear and reasonable, the voice he used with the house steward. "If there's some sort of fee, I'm happy to—"

The young man looked at him. Then he looked at Vosk again, as if Eli had made a noise in another room.

The plastic strips moved.

The heat came out first. Eli felt it on his face and the backs of his hands, a hard dry push like opening an oven, and every bowed head in the market went lower, and on the hooks overhead the fat on the sawn carcass started to run. Then the strips parted, and something came through the arch that had to turn its shoulders to fit.

He was the shape of an orc, the heavy shoulders and the long arms, but half again as tall as Vosk and twice as broad, and the light coming up off the cut lit him from underneath. His skin was crusted all over the chest and arms and the slabs of the shoulders with something in rows, and the rows were chips. Orc chips, grey, with their threads, hundreds of them, sewn into him one by one through the skin with wire, the way you'd sew buttons on a coat, and every one of them was wet, and every one, faintly, was moving. He wore nothing on top. He had a yard apron on, black rubber, stiff with old use, like the men at the presses wore.

He didn't say anything. He looked at Vosk. He looked at the strip on Vosk's neck, and Vosk took a step toward him that Eli didn't think he'd meant to take, and stopped.

Then the King looked at Eli. Not long. Up, and down. Eli had seen the cook look at a bird like that.

"The King'll have the new one," said the young man with the smile. "And he'll have that."

"He's with me," Eli said. It wasn't the steward voice now. "We've paid. He's with—"

"Teo," Vosk said. "Take him up the stair."

"He's with me—"

"Teo."

Teo had both hands in Eli's coat. Eli could feel them shaking, and he could hear Teo's breathing, and it was slow, slow, in through the nose, out through the mouth, as Teo had breathed through the oral finals and the time the flyer lost a fan over the sumps.

The King lifted one hand, palm up, as if he were weighing something.

Every orc kneeling in the market sagged. All at once, the pan man, the two women, the children by the cut, as if something had gone out of them through the backs of their necks. And Vosk jerked. His head went back as if somebody had him by the hair, and his mouth came open, and steam came up out of his collar. The air in front of the King went thick and bright, and came across the market floor at them like a wall falling over.

Vosk put his hand up.

The air in front of his hand went white. Then the noise hit, and the floor came up under Eli's feet and dropped away again, and everything round Vosk went, and hardly any of it toward the King. A stall to his left came apart into its boards and its pots and a woman who had been kneeling behind it, and all of it went sideways through the air together and into the wall. Hooks on their chains swung out level and came back. The sawn carcass fell. A post by the soup stall broke in half. Something went past Eli's face close enough to lift his hair, and the wall behind him cracked. Two of the young men weren't standing any more; one was lying under the soup stall with his legs out, and the other wasn't anywhere Eli could see.

Vosk was standing where he'd stood. He looked at his hand. The palm had split across, three times, and it was steaming. He tried to close it, and it closed slowly, the fingers one at a time.

The King looked at him with something like interest.

Something took hold of the back of Eli's coat and yanked, and Teo's hands tore out of it, and Eli was pulled back against a chest that smelled of wire and sweat. One of them had come along the wall behind them while nobody was looking. A fist wound with wire had his collar. The other hand had a strapping cutter, and the cutter was under his ear, cold, the hooked end tucked in behind the lobe.

"Terrace," the young man shouted. He wasn't smiling; he was concentrating. "Terrace. Hand down. Put it down or he goes up the stair without his ears. King don't need his ears."

Vosk turned his head.

He only turned it. He looked across the floor at Eli with the cutter under his ear, and his hand stayed up, and he looked, and the King closed his fist, and Vosk's left arm broke.

Eli heard it. It was very loud, and it went on, because it broke in more than one place, above the elbow and below it and in the wrist, and the arm turned in a way it had no way of turning, and Vosk made a sound Eli had never heard him make and went down on one knee.

Eli put his hand up.

He could do this. He'd done it at fifteen, the flyer out of the lake, sick for a day after, so be sick. The wrist. The cutter. The throat, right there against his ear, wet, breathing. Take it. Take it off him.

Nothing came.

He pushed. Nothing. The cutter went in behind his ear and something warm ran down his neck. His hand was up and open in front of his face and it was a hand. That was all it was.

Teo hit the young man in the side of the head with a soup ladle.

It was a big iron ladle off the wrecked stall and he swung it with both hands and it made a flat sound, and the young man let go and sat down. Teo hit him again on the top of the head on the way down, carefully, as if driving a peg. Then he dropped the ladle and got Eli by the collar and pulled him along the wall toward the bridge end.

"Bridge," he said.

"Vosk—"

"Bridge."

Eli went backwards along the wall with Teo dragging him, and his hand was still up.

Vosk came up off his knee. He didn't put his good hand out this time. He turned, the whole of him, until he was facing the arch and the King standing in front of it, and stood there with his good arm and his broken arm both hanging, and Eli saw him shut his eyes.

Then it all came out of him at once.

There wasn't any aim in it. The floor under the King split down the middle and went up in two slabs, and so did the floor under the soup stall, and under the kneeling people along the far wall, who were up now and running, and some of them went up with it. The hooks tore out of the ceiling in rows and came down in their chains. A pipe burst in the wall a yard from Eli's head and blew steam and something black. Ahead of them at the bridge end one of the young men went backwards over the edge into the cut without a sound, and the one beside him lay down flat with his arms over his head, and Teo pulled Eli straight past him and he didn't look up.

Vosk was walking. He was walking at the arch, one step and then another, and every step something else in him gave. The skin on the backs of his knuckles split open over the bone, all four, and then the backs of both hands. Blood came out of his nose and his ears and the corners of his eyes. Steam came off his shoulders in sheets. The fingers of his good hand had come open, and he was trying to shut them, Eli could see him trying, and they bent back instead, all four, and stayed back. He kept walking. He wasn't pushing it anywhere. It went where it went, through the floor, through the stalls, into the ceiling, and he walked himself at the King and let it.

The King had one hand flat in the air and he was going back. One step. Another. His chest was smoking, and some of the chips sewn into it had burst and were running. His back hit the arch, and the arch cracked round him in long lines.

Eli had his hand up. It had never come down. It was pointed at Vosk now, at Vosk's back, at the broken arm hanging. Get him. Just get hold of him. The coat. Anything. Pull him back. Come on. Come on.

Nothing.

Vosk was nearly at the arch.

The arch came down.

It came down all at once, on the King and the plastic strips and two of the young men who'd been standing by it, a great sliding roar of old stone, and the dust came out across the market in a wave and over Eli and Teo where they stood at the bridge end, hot, and they couldn't see anything.

Teo found Eli's wrist in the dust and pulled the hand down and held on to it.

Then it was quiet. Things fell, here and there. Steam hissed.

Eli pulled his wrist out of Teo's hand and went back.

---

Vosk was on his back on the market floor with the dust settling on him.

He was steaming. Steam came off him everywhere, off his chest and his face and his good arm, and where it came off him the skin had gone dark and tight and shiny, and along the forearms it had split. Blood came out of his ears in two slow lines. His eyes were open. The whites of them were full of blood, both, to the edges. His broken arm was under him at an angle. The fingers of the other hand still stood back.

Eli knelt in the dust. He put a hand on Vosk's chest and took it off; it was hot as a stove.

"It's all right," he said. "It's all right. We'll get you up. Teo. Teo, help me get him up."

Teo had come back. He stood over them. He wasn't looking at Vosk. He was looking at the heap of stone where the arch had been.

"Vosk," Eli said. "Vosk, look at me."

Vosk's eyes moved and found him. His mouth was working. When it came there was blood in it, and a hiss on it, because something in his chest had gone.

"Did it."

"You did. You did it. Teo—"

"No." His hand came up off the floor, an inch, and fell back. "Listen."

"We'll carry you."

"Ilder."

Eli bent down.

"Ilder's lot." A breath. "Yard. You were." The hand came up again, not as high. "Small."

"Don't talk."

"His dad's. Fob." The breath stuck halfway and he waited for it. "Made me. Dance."

"Vosk—"

"Wet myself." His face did something. "Everybody. Laughing."

His eyes went past Eli, up, to one side, to the far wall of the market, and came back.

"You come off the step."

Eli was holding his hand. He didn't remember taking it. It was too hot to hold, and he held it.

"Bit him." Blood came out of the corner of his mouth. "Wouldn't let go. Screaming at them." A breath. "He's mine. Leave him."

"I don't remember," Eli said.

"I know." Something rattled. "That's all right."

Eli opened his mouth.

"Proud." It came out hard, all at once, the breath he'd been keeping for it. "Of him." Something rattled. "What he. Grew."

He stopped. Eli waited.

"I picked it."

His lips went on moving after that, and Eli bent close.

"Nobody spent me."

"Eli," said Teo.

The heap of stone where the arch had been moved. A slab near the top tilted and slid and went down the side of the heap with a long scraping noise, and dust ran after it, and from under the heap, from deep under it, came heat.

"Eli. Now."

"He's talking—"

"Go," Vosk said. He was looking past Eli again, at the wall, at whatever was behind the wall. His hand in Eli's hand had gone loose. "Oh," he said. "It's right there."

He didn't say anything else. His eyes stayed open on the wall. Steam kept coming off him, less and less.

Eli knelt there.

Teo took him by the back of the collar and pulled him up off the floor, the whole weight of him, and Eli's legs didn't work and he went sideways and Teo held him up against the wall of the nearest stall. Then Teo slapped him across the face, hard, open-handed, once.

"Walk," Teo said.

Eli walked.

They were nearly at the bridge end when the young men came back out of the dust round the foot of the heap, three of them, grey to the eyes. One of them saw the two humans and stopped. He looked at Eli. He took a step.

Teo didn't let go of Eli's wrist and didn't run. He lifted his chin at the heap, where the stone was shifting and the heat was coming out between the slabs.

"He's under there," Teo said. "You want to be up here with us when he comes out?"

The young man looked at the heap. He looked at Eli a moment more, as at a purse somebody else had dropped, and then he turned and went to the heap and got down on his knees with the others and began pulling stones off it with his hands.

The last one went to where Vosk lay and knelt on his chest with one knee, and turned his head to one side by the jaw, and took a long thin hook off his belt and put the point of it in the new strip on his neck.

Teo pulled Eli onto the bridge, and the bridge was warm under his feet all the way across.

---

They went up.

Not by the shaft. They went up the Long Stair with the rest of the Scald, which was all going up the stair that night, as many as could fit, orcs and dwarves and humans in clean coats with their shoes in their hands, and nobody talking. Every hundred steps the reader on the landing looked at them, and the orcs on either side of Eli flinched as it went cold down their arms, and he got a light. Green. His came up green with the other humans'. He had blood on his hands and on the knees of his trousers and down the side of his neck from behind his ear, and dust in his hair and his teeth, and the reader looked at him and went green.

Somewhere around the sumps his hands started. It went up his arms. Teo kept hold of his wrist and kept climbing and didn't say anything about it. Hand up. Nothing. Vosk turning his head to look. Hand up. Nothing. On the next landing he tried to make the hand into a fist and it wouldn't go, and the reader looked at him, and went green.

At the top of the stair the mid-levels were exactly the same. The feeds. The lanes ticking overhead. The pill stall on the landing, with a queue. The ribbon said LEGION RECRUITMENT AT RECORD. In the flesh market at Ninth Spur they were hosing down the slabs.

Teo bought two bottles of water from a machine and they drank them standing in a doorway, and Teo was sick, neatly, once, against the doorway's side, and wiped his mouth and drank some more water and said, "Right," and they went on.

---

The gate of Ninth Terrace was a ring of dark glass in an arch of white stone.

Eli stopped in front of it. His feet had stopped. He was filthy to the elbows and he had a dead man's blood dried in the creases of his fingers, and his face was swelling on one side where Teo had hit him, and there was a dust in his hair that the Terrace had never seen, and he stood in front of the arch and waited for it to see him.

Something in the glass looked at him.

The light came up green at his shoulder. The soft voice said, "Good evening." The arch let him through.

They walked up the white path between the water channels, under the trees, with the house windows dark on both sides. Eli's house had a light on in the kitchen. Someone always left it on.

Teo ran water in the kitchen sink and held Eli's hands under it, one and then the other, and scrubbed them with the brush the cook used for the pans, and the water went brown and then pink and then clear, and Teo kept scrubbing after it was clear, and then he stopped. He stood there with the brush. The side of his thumb was raw to the quick. His own hands had started to shake now, at the sink, and he looked at them and put the brush down and put them in his armpits.

"She'll want to know what happened to her brush," he said. Not to Eli. To the sink.

Eli sat down at the table. He looked at his wrist for the time, and there was nothing on it.

The fruit bowl was where he'd left it, and the letter was under it, pale, with the corner showing. He slid it out. It was heavy for a letter. He put his thumb on the seal, flat, and held it there, and then he put the letter down on the table face down, and his hands on either side of it.

Outside, in the yard, under the long house, the barracks bell was going for the night count. He heard the steward's voice come up through the window, flat, reading off the slate. A number, and somebody answered. A number, and somebody answered.

A number.

The steward waited. Then he made a mark, and read the next.
