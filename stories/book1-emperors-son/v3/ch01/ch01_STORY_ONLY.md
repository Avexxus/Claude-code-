# The Emperor's Son

## ONE · The Gift

The ground in the Scald stayed warm all night, and nobody down there had ever found out why.

Vosk came up through it on foot with the dwarf's price in his head. Eleven hundred, she'd said, in marks, not imperial, and she'd said it to the wall behind him while she wiped her hands. He had forty-one marks in a sock under his bunk and it had taken him nine years. He had come off the evening shift and gone straight down to her, and now he was coming back with the night gone, past the shut stalls and the sleepers on their mats, feet tucked into each other's coats for the company, under pipes that sweated something with a skin on it, and the heat came up through the soles of his boots the whole way and into his shins, and his shins ached with it the way they ached at the end of a double shift, which was what he had in front of him.

Nearer the Tower the air didn't sit right. It had a shine in it at head height. Bits of light drifted in the cross-streets, slow, none bigger than a seed, the colour of nothing he had a word for, and they cast no shadow on anything they passed. A girl of about eight stood in the mouth of an alley with a net of fine wire on a pole, waiting for one to come close enough. Her hands were wrapped to the elbow in rag. She didn't look at him. You didn't touch drift with skin. Everybody knew someone who had.

There were humans down here at this hour too, which was the joke of the place. Clean coats, soft shoes, standing with their foreheads to the old walls and their eyes shut, palms flat on the stone, waiting for something. Three of them in a row outside the bathhouse that had been dry since before Vosk was born. They paid orc children to mind their shoes. One of the children had already sold one of the shoes and was minding the other with great seriousness.

People who lived on the old ground got sick in ways the clinics up top didn't have names for. He passed a woman whose jaw had kept growing down one side until it had shut her eye. She was selling batteries off a blanket and she said good morning to him, though it wasn't morning yet, and he said it back. Two small boys sat on a step sharing a cake, and the nearer one held it in a hand where the fingers had grown together into one flat piece, and broke it on his knee, and gave the bigger half away.

He came round the corner into Pole Street and stopped, because everyone else had.

There were maybe forty orcs in the street, and all of them were on their knees with their faces turned to the wall, men and women, a few children. Nobody was talking. The heat was worse here, and it came in gusts, out of a doorway on the far side where a curtain of plastic strips hung down.

He got down. He put his face to the wall like the rest and showed his hands.

Someone was walking up the line. He heard the boots stop and go on, stop and go on. Young men's boots, light, quick, and when they stopped beside him he saw from the corner of his eye a pair of shins in yard trousers, and a hand hanging loose with a cutter in it, the kind you used for strapping. The hand had wire wound round the knuckles. Up the arm, at the back of the young man's neck where the chip scar should have been flat and pale, the skin had been opened and stitched shut again with the same wire, tight, in neat crosses, and the stitches wept.

"You're up late, Terrace," the young man said.

Vosk said nothing. The young man smelled his collar, actually leaned down and smelled it, and laughed through his nose and moved on.

Behind the plastic strips, something big shifted its weight, and the floor told him about it.

They stayed on their knees until the boots had gone back inside. Then a long time after that. When people did start getting up, they did it one at a time and without looking at each other, and went off in different directions, and the woman next to Vosk, who was old and had burns up both hands to the wrist, stayed down a little longer, rocking. When she got up she put her thumb to the back of his neck, on his scar, and pressed. He did it back. Her scar was hot.

"Eating," she said. "He's eating tonight."

"I didn't see."

"No," she agreed.

She walked with him as far as the foot of the Long Stair, because it was on her way, and on the way she sang, not loud, the one about Hurrak who walked out of the yard. Vosk had heard it all his life and never all of it. Nobody sang all of it. Hurrak walked out of the yard and the dogs didn't bark, and he walked past the gate and the gate didn't bite, and he walked out of the city and kept walking. In the version his mother had sung there was a part about him carrying his own name in his mouth like a stone so he wouldn't lose it, and the old woman left that part out, and he didn't ask her why.

At the foot of the stair she said, "Walk well," and went off into the Scald with her burnt hands in her armpits.

He hummed the rest of it going up.

---

The Long Stair was four thousand and some steps, and there was a reader at every landing. A ring of dark glass set in the wall at eye height for a human, at the chest for him. When you passed one, it looked. With a human it was nothing, Vosk had been told: a light, a name maybe, a little sound. With an orc the chip in the back of the neck woke up and answered it, and the answer went cold down both your arms into your hands, every hundred steps, all the way up. You got used to that.

The readers could also tell the chip to hurt you. That you didn't get used to.

Halfway up, the stair left the old ground and went through the sumps, where the city's water came back down to be cleaned. Then the foundations. Then the bottom of the mid-levels, where it was suddenly cooler and there were lights that worked, and grey walls with the Emperor's seal stamped on them at every landing, an oval with nothing in it.

The mid-levels were never all the way asleep. The sky, what you could see of it between the towers, was the colour of a lamp seen through a dirty sheet, and the traffic lanes ran across it in strings of light, three deep, the lowest close enough that you could hear the hulls tick as they cooled. A woman's face as tall as a building said something about the elves to nobody. Under her, the ribbon. PLAGUE HOLD EXTENDED, THREE SYSTEMS. LEGION RECRUITMENT AT RECORD. TERRACE PROPERTY VALUES. Somebody had thrown something at the ribbon and it had stuck there, brown, over the word VALUES.

The flesh market at Ninth Spur was opening. Vosk cut through it, because it was quicker and the stalls were full of men too busy to see him. They were hosing down the slabs. Steam came off the water. The cold-cases were going up on their trestles, and every case had a stamp on the end the size of a hand, black on white, CLONE STOCK — NO SOUL, with the grade under it. A woman in an apron was laying out tongues in rows on ice, and a man at the next stall was sorting a basket of eyes by colour, and a dwarf further down sold hands in pairs, matched, with a little paper tag on each thumb. Vosk had worked a season here when he was small, carrying ice. He knew what an orc arm sold for, which was less than a goat's.

At the end of the row a man was shouting, "Real! Real, not clone, real!" and a young woman with a shopping bag stopped and asked him, quite politely, how she was meant to tell.

"Costs more, doesn't it," the man said.

She seemed to think that was fair, and bought some.

The lift at the top of the Spur had a core, a grey lump the size of a loaf in a glass box, wet, with threads going out of it into the works, and when the lift was coming down the lump tightened, very slightly, like a hand. Orcs didn't ride the lift.

He took the culvert.

From the culvert's mouth you could see the Tower, all of it, if you leaned back. You couldn't anywhere else in the mid-levels. It came up out of the middle of everything, out of the oldest ground, pale and seamless and so big that the clouds broke round it, and the lanes bent away from it in a wide circle, and nothing anybody had built came within a mile of its foot. The rich said it was warm on your face. They said it the way they said a good wine was warm. The Terrace was built on the first rise of land that looked at it, and the houses there had their best windows facing it, and people paid for those windows more than Vosk could count.

He leaned back and looked at it, because it was there. A tall pale thing. He didn't feel anything.

---

The service gate for Ninth Terrace was a slot in a wall at the top of the culvert, one orc wide. Vosk was fourteen minutes past the count.

He knew it before he got to the slot, because the slot knew, and the chip knew, and it started before he could get his shoulder through. It always started in the jaw. It felt like biting on foil, and then the foil was in the bones of his face and down his neck, and then it went into the long muscles of his back and pulled them all at once in the same direction. He hit the wall of the slot with his head. It never lasted long. When it let go he was on his knees in the slot with his mouth full of spit and his hands open in front of him, the way they taught you to hold them, and somewhere far off the gate said, in a pleasant voice, "Late. Logged."

He got up. He went through.

The Terrace was the other side of the slot, all at once, every time. Water ran down channels cut in the white stone. There were trees, real ones, with real dirt round their feet that orcs changed every month. The air was cool and smelled of nothing much, which cost more than any smell. In the first-floor window of the third house a boy of about nine was eating breakfast in a white shirt, and down on the wet lawn the family's yard orc, a grown man, was hopping. Every time a foot came down on the grass it jerked up again as if the grass were a stove, and the other one came down and jerked, and he went on like that, faster, with his arms clamped to his sides and his face screwed shut. The boy had his spoon in one hand and his thumb on a fob in the other. He wasn't laughing. He was interested.

Vosk kept walking.

In the barracks under the long house, the others were already up and going. Somebody had kept him a bit of bread. He ate it sitting on his bunk with his hand flat on the mattress, over the sock.

Then he went out into the yard and stood under the third window from the end on the second floor, which had been the same window since he was twelve, and found a bit of gravel at the edge of the path, and threw it.

---

The money came in a dwarven purse, the kind with a clasp that bit, and Eli had put it on the kitchen table next to the fruit bowl so he could see it while he talked.

"Eleven hundred," he said. "In marks. They didn't even ask."

"Who's they?" said Teo.

"The account."

"The account didn't ask."

"It never asks." Eli turned the purse over. It was heavier than he'd expected money to be. Money was a number on a slate; he'd never held eleven hundred of anything. "That's what it's for."

The account had come with the house, and the house had come with him, before he could remember. It paid the steward and the cook and the tutors, when there had been tutors, and the orcs in the barracks, and it had paid for his first flyer and his second, and for the window that had gone in after he put a chair through it at fifteen. Your father's people, the steward called it, whenever it did something, and Eli had stopped asking which people a long time ago.

Teo sat on the counter by the cold-box with his heels against the doors, eating an apple from the bottom up, which was how he'd eaten them since they were seven.

"He asked you for money," Teo said.

"He asked me for a loan."

"For a loan." Teo looked at the apple. "He didn't ask you to come."

"He didn't have to." Eli got up. He couldn't sit still with it. "On his own? At night? With that on him, to go and sit in some dwarf's back room and let her into his neck?" He laughed. "Vosk? They'd have the boots off him before the second landing. He's soft, you know he's soft, he cried when the cook drowned the kittens."

"He was eleven."

"Well. He's still soft."

Teo finished the apple and looked round for somewhere to put the core and didn't find anywhere and held it out at arm's length, and Eli, without looking, took it out of his fingers from across the kitchen and dropped it in the bin by the door. Teo looked at his empty hand.

"Thank you," he said to the bin.

On the wall behind him the feed was running with the sound off. Grey men at a grey table. The ribbon underneath said ELVEN DELEGATION RECALLED and then THE QUEEN DECLINES TO COMMENT and then something about rates.

The letter was under the fruit bowl.

It had come that morning, by hand. A courier in a grey coat, a woman, who had stood in the doorway and waited, saying nothing, until the steward had fetched Eli down himself, and then had held it out and waited again until it was in his fingers, and then gone. The envelope was heavy and pale and there was nothing written on it, and on the back, pressed into a disc of grey wax, was the seal off the stair landings. He had seen that oval ten thousand times on landings and crates and the backs of buses. He'd never had it handed to him.

He'd looked at it for a while in the hall. Then he'd brought it in here and put the bowl on it.

"Are you going to open that," said Teo.

"Tomorrow." Eli slid the purse into the inside pocket of his coat, and patted it. "If it's his people, they've had twenty-four years. They can have tonight."

There was a knock on the yard door, two short, which was Vosk's knock, and Eli was across the kitchen before the second one had finished.

Vosk filled the door. He always had, since he'd got his growth, and he always ducked coming through it, even though the top of his head cleared the frame by an inch. He was in his work clothes still, the grey canvas, and he had a cut on his forehead that Eli hadn't noticed that morning, scabbed, with grit in it.

"What happened to your head?"

"Wall."

"Sit down. Have you eaten? You haven't eaten." Eli was already at the cold-box. "There's the end of the bird. Teo, move."

"I'm all right," Vosk said.

"Sit down."

Vosk sat on the step inside the yard door, which was where he sat, and Eli put the plate down on the step beside him, and the fork on the plate, and then a cloth, because he was always forgetting the cloth. Then he crouched in front of him with his forearms on his knees.

"We're coming with you," he said.

Vosk looked at him. Then he looked past him at Teo.

"It's quicker on my own," Vosk said.

"Don't be stupid."

"I know the way. I go down there."

"I know you go down there. That's how you got your head opened." Eli reached out and put his hand on the back of Vosk's neck, over the scar, and gave it a squeeze and a little shake, as he'd been doing since they were boys. The scar was warm under his palm and a little raised. "With all that on you? Somebody puts a knife in you for the purse, and then who carries the ice for the cook? Me?" He let go and stood up. "I've got the money. You've got the dwarf. Teo's got the route."

"I haven't got the route," said Teo.

"You'll get the route. You always get the route."

Vosk had picked up the fork. He turned it over. He put it down on the plate again, very square, next to the bird.

"Eli," he said.

"It's done. Eat your bird." Eli grinned down at him. "When have I ever not come?"

Vosk ate the bird. He ate it slowly, and somewhere near the end of it he stopped chewing, and his head went back against the door frame, and he was asleep, with the fork still in his hand and his mouth a little open.

Eli took the fork out of his hand. He fetched his second-best coat from the hall and put it over him, and it didn't cover much.

Teo looked at the clock on his slate, and then at Eli.

"So she waits," Eli said. He sat down on the kitchen floor with his back to the cold-box, where he could see Vosk's face. "Find the route."

Teo found the route.

---

They went out through the front a little after eleven.

The gate of Ninth Terrace was a ring of dark glass in an arch of white stone, and Eli walked through it without slowing, the way he walked through every door. Something in the glass looked at him. A light came up green on the stone at his shoulder, and a soft voice said, "Good evening," without a name, and the arch let him out onto the public ramp. It did the same for Teo.

Vosk wasn't with them. The front gate didn't read orcs. It bit them.

"That's stupid," Eli had said in the yard, "come through with us, I'll tell them," and Vosk had said, "It bites," and gone down to the culvert, and they found him at the bottom of the public ramp twenty minutes later, standing very still against a pillar where the light didn't reach, with his hands where people could see them.

"See," said Eli, "no trouble," and clapped him on the arm.

The mid-levels at night were brighter than the mid-levels by day. Eli loved them for it. He'd been coming down since he was fifteen, for food, for the clubs on Ancel Row, for no reason, and every time it was like taking a breath of something too strong. Noise came down from the lanes overhead and up from the streets underneath, and the feeds were so many and so big that the light off them slid over your skin like water. More people than the Terrace had in a year, all going somewhere at once. Every human face that passed under a reader got a little light. Green, green, green. You stopped seeing it.

They didn't take the Long Stair. Teo had found a service shaft off the sumps where the cleaning crews went down, and he went down it first without saying anything, and the ladder rungs were slick and warm, and the air got warmer every rung.

At the bottom of the shaft the floor was the wrong size.

It was a street. It had a gutter, and a curb, and on the other side another curb. But the curb on this side came up to Eli's chest, and the one on the far side was a long way off, so far that the lamps strung between them sagged in the middle, and the street between had been filled in with poured stuff to make a floor at a sensible height, and people had built shacks on it, and a ramp went up over the curb made of pallets and somebody's door.

Eli stood with his hands on the curb. The stone was smooth where people had held it getting up the ramp. Teo was already at the top.

The Scald was hot. Eli had known it would be. He hadn't known the heat would have a direction. It came from in front of them and a little up, as if there were a fire ahead on a high shelf, and it pushed on his face and his chest, and the further they went the harder it pushed. He took his coat off and carried it and then put it back on because of the purse. Sweat ran into the small of his back. The air tasted of metal and burning fat. There were a lot of people, orcs mostly, and they moved round Eli in the crowd without touching him, a little gap always opening half a step before he got there and closing behind.

He thought that was courtesy. He said so to Teo, who didn't answer.

Vosk walked in front. He walked differently down here. Looser. His hands weren't held out. A big orc with a split ear stepped into his way and the two of them put their thumbs to each other's necks and said something low in the orc talk, and the other one laughed and looked past Vosk at Eli's coat, and at Eli, and said something else, and Vosk said, "No," quite sharply.

"What did he say?"

"Nothing."

"He said something."

"He asked was you mine."

Eli laughed. "What did you say?"

Vosk walked faster.

The bridge went over a cut in the ground where the light was. The cut ran away on both sides, forty feet down, maybe more, and the bottom of it wasn't dark as the bottom of a hole should be; it was full of a slow shining like the inside of an eye. Drift came up out of it. On the edges children stood with nets on long poles, wrapped in rag.

The bridge itself was one long curved girder with a deck laid on top, ribbed like a spine, dark grey going brown, and it went from one side of the cut to the other in one piece. Eli put his hand on it as he stepped up. Then he stopped and put his hand back.

It was warm. Warm like a horse.

"Don't," said Vosk. "Don't touch it."

Hookline was the market under the bridge's far end, and it was called that for the hooks: the whole low ceiling hung with them on chains, thousands, and something on half of them. Coats. Lamps. Pots. A whole side of something red and white, sawn down the middle, turning slowly in the heat coming up off the cut, with a paper tag on the shank that said nothing at all, not even a grade. Music came from three places at once. A dwarf woman sold soup out of a hot-box, and the soup smelled good, and Eli slowed for it.

Teo came up very close beside him.

"Take the coat off," he said. "Carry it inside out."

"It's got the purse in it."

"I know what's in it." Teo didn't move his head. "Every stall, Eli. Nobody's looking at him. They're looking at the coat."

Eli looked round. A man at a stall of pans looked at his pans. Two orc women by a hook of lamps looked at each other. "It's a good coat," he said, and put his arm round Teo's shoulders and gave him a shake. "Look at you. You've gone grey. Vosk, look at him."

Vosk didn't look. He turned off into an alley between two stalls, and they followed him.

---

The dwarf was called Velk, or that was what it said on the shutter of the shop she worked out of the back of, which had sold water once and still had the tanks. She was short even for a dwarf and very broad, and she had a scar that ran from her hairline down through one eyebrow and on down her cheek, and the eye under it was a pale grey and didn't move when the other one did.

She looked at Eli. She looked at Teo. She looked at Vosk.

"You said you'd come on your own," she said.

"He's paying," said Vosk.

"He's paying." She looked at Eli again, at his coat, his hands, his boots. "Course he is. You're late. Come in. Don't touch anything."

The back of the shop was a long room with a low ceiling and shelves on every wall, and on the shelves were things Eli didn't have names for. A grey cube that sat at a tilt and, while he watched, tilted the other way. A coin standing on its edge on a saucer.

"Where's all this from?"

"Out," said Velk. She was washing her hands at a basin in the corner, with a brush, hard, up to the elbows. "Depot takes a third now. A third. Then his cut. Then whatever he sends up to whoever." She shook the water off. "And they wonder."

"Whose cut?" said Teo.

Velk dried her arms and didn't answer. She pulled a heavy chair into the light, high-backed, with straps on the arms, and nodded at it, and Vosk sat down in it and put his own arms in the straps.

Eli took out the purse.

She counted it on the table in stacks of fifty, wetting her thumb, and swept it into a drawer. "Hold his head."

Eli stood there.

"His head. Forward. Chin down. Unless you'd rather do the knife."

Eli held his head.

Up close, Vosk's hair smelled of the barracks soap, and the back of his neck was very hot, and the scar was there under Eli's fingers where it had always been, a pale ridge as long as a thumb. He'd touched it a thousand times. Velk swabbed it with something cold and laid her tools out on a cloth, small bright things, and picked one, and put the point of it in the old scar at the top end and drew it down.

Vosk's whole body went tight in the chair.

There was more blood than Eli had expected, and it was darker. It ran down into the collar of Vosk's work shirt and Velk didn't bother to catch it. She opened the cut with two little hooks and held it, and Eli could see down into it, past the red, to something grey and shining with threads going out from it into the meat like roots. She took hold of it with narrow pliers and began to pull, slowly, turning, and the threads came out one at a time with a sound like wet paper, and Vosk said something through his teeth in the orc talk, the same three words over and over, and his hands in the straps opened and shut.

"Talk to him," said Velk. "If you're going to stand there."

"It's all right," said Eli. "It's all right. You're doing well. You're doing so well."

The thing came out. It was the size of a fingernail, with its threads trailing, and she dropped it in a dish, and in the dish it moved. Once, a little curl of the threads, like something closing.

Teo had gone to stand by the door.

The new one came out of a sealed packet. It was bigger than the old one, darker, ridged on one side, and someone had taken a file to the mark on it, not well enough. Eli could make out part of a word. LEGION.

"Soldier's," said Velk. "Leash is out." She was pressing it home with her thumb, and the threads on the new one were moving already, finding their way. She glanced at Eli with her live eye. "You understand what you've bought him."

"Yes," said Eli.

She looked at him a little longer, and went back to her work.

She closed the cut with glue and a strip of something that pulled tight as it dried, and undid the straps. Vosk didn't get up. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his head hanging, breathing hard, and blood dripped off the end of his nose onto the floor between his boots.

Then he lifted his head.

He didn't look at any of them. He turned his face, slowly, to the far wall of the shop, and he looked at it as though there was a window there.

"What's that," he said.

His voice was strange. Eli crouched in front of him. "What's what?"

"That." Vosk had his eyes wide open. "It's. Up. It's right there."

"Tower," said Velk, wiping her tools. "Everybody, first time. You stop noticing. Sun in the next room."

Eli looked at the wall. Shelves, the cube, the coin. His ears had the faint fullness they'd always had, which was nothing, which was just ears.

Vosk's lips were moving. He put his hand up flat toward the wall and held it there, as if there were a fire.

"Is it hot?" Eli said. "Vosk. Does it hurt?"

Vosk looked at him then. He looked at Eli's face for a long time. Then he looked back at the wall, and down at his hand, and he closed the hand.

"No," he said.

Eli laughed out loud. He gripped Vosk's knee. "Look at you. Look at him, Teo." He stood up. "That one," he said to Velk, pointing at the coin on its edge. "What do you want for that?"

"It's not for sale."

"Everything's for sale. For Teo. He's been a misery all night, he deserves something. What's it do? Does it just stand there?"

Velk went on wiping.

"I'll give you two hundred. I can have it sent down tomorrow."

At the back of the room a thin dwarf boy of maybe ten was sitting on a stool behind the tanks with a cup. Eli hadn't seen him. He looked at Eli. He looked at the pocket of Eli's coat where the purse had been, and at Eli's boots, and at Eli again. Then he put down the cup and went out through a curtain at the back without a word.

"Where's he going?" said Teo.

Velk didn't look round. "Tax," she said.

Teo came across the room. He took hold of Eli's arm above the elbow, not hard. His face was very calm, the calm it went when an exam was going badly.

"Leave it," he said. "We go. Now."

"I'm asking her a price." Eli shook the hand off, not unkindly. "One minute."

Teo looked at him. Then he went back to the door and stood there with his arms folded and didn't say anything else.

Vosk was standing. Eli hadn't seen him get up. He was standing very straight with his head a little tilted, and his face was turned toward the curtain where the boy had gone, and the blood was drying on his lip, and he was listening.

"It's gone quiet," Vosk said.

---

It had. Eli hadn't noticed the market's noise until there wasn't any. No music. No one selling soup. The heat was still there, pushing through the shutter at the front, and a smell had come into it, hot metal and something sweet behind the metal that turned his stomach.

Velk had sat down on her stool behind the table with her hands in her lap. "Out the front," she said. "Not the back." She was looking at the table. "And I didn't fit it."

They went out the front.

The alley was empty. The market at the end of it was not. Everybody was still there, the stallholders, the pan man, the two orc women, the soup woman; they were all on their knees, every one, facing the nearest wall. Nobody looked up. On the hooks overhead the meat turned in the heat.

"Bridge," said Teo, very low. "Walk. Don't run."

Eli walked. Teo had him by the sleeve. Vosk was a step behind. They came out of the alley mouth and went along the wall past all the bowed heads toward the bridge end, and from the far side of the market, out of a wide arch with plastic strips hung across it, young men began to come.

Six. Eight. Orcs, young, light on their feet, in yard trousers, with wire wound round their knuckles and their necks opened and stitched with wire. They didn't hurry. They spread across the market floor between the kneeling people and the bridge end, and stopped, and one of them, not the biggest, smiled at Vosk and touched his own stitched neck and then pointed at Vosk's, where the new strip was.

"That's a nice one, Terrace," he said.

"It's paid for," said Eli. His voice came out clear and reasonable, the voice he used with the house steward. "If there's some sort of fee, I'm happy to—"

The young man looked at him. Then he looked at Vosk again, as if Eli had made a noise in another room.

The plastic strips moved.

The heat came out first. Eli felt it on his face and the backs of his hands, a hard dry push like opening an oven, and every bowed head in the market went lower, and on the hooks overhead the fat on the sawn carcass started to run. Then the strips parted, and something came through the arch that had to turn its shoulders to fit.

He was an orc. Eli would think that, later, and then not be sure. He was the shape of an orc, the heavy shoulders and the long arms, but half again as tall as Vosk and twice as broad, and the light from the cut came up under him and made him wrong. His skin was crusted all over the chest and arms and the slabs of the shoulders with something in rows, and the rows were chips. Orc chips, grey, with their threads, hundreds of them, sewn into him one by one through the skin with wire, the way you'd sew buttons on a coat, and every one of them was wet, and every one, faintly, was moving. He wore nothing on top. He had a yard apron on, black rubber, stiff with old use, like the men at the presses wore.

He didn't say anything. He looked at Vosk. He looked at the strip on Vosk's neck.

Then he looked at Eli. Not long. Up, and down. The woman at the flesh market looked at tongues like that.

"The King'll have the new one," said the young man with the smile. "And he'll have that."

"We're leaving," Eli said. It wasn't the steward voice now. "We've paid, we're—"

"Teo," Vosk said. "Take him up the stair."

"I'm not leaving you here—"

"Teo."

Teo had both hands in Eli's coat. Eli could feel them shaking, and he could hear Teo's breathing, and it was slow, slow, in through the nose, out through the mouth, as Teo had breathed through the oral finals and the time the flyer lost a fan over the sumps.

The King lifted one hand, palm up, as if he were weighing something.

Every orc kneeling in the market sagged. All at once, the pan man, the two women, the children by the cut, as if something had gone out of them through the backs of their necks. And the air in front of the King went thick and bright, and came across the market floor at them like a wall falling over.

Vosk put his hand up.

Eli would never be able to say what he saw. The air between Vosk's hand and the King's went white, and then he didn't see anything, because the noise hit him, and the floor came up under his feet and dropped away again, and a stall to his left came apart into its boards and its pots and a woman who had been kneeling behind it, and all of it went sideways through the air together and into the wall. Hooks on their chains swung out level and came back. The sawn carcass fell. Two of the young men weren't standing any more; one was lying under the soup stall with his legs out, and the other wasn't anywhere Eli could see.

Vosk was standing where he'd stood. He looked at his hand.

The King looked at him with something like interest.

Something took hold of the back of Eli's coat and yanked, and Teo's hands tore out of it, and Eli was pulled back against a chest that smelled of wire and sweat. A fist wound with wire had his collar. The other hand had a strapping cutter, and the cutter was under his ear, cold, the hooked end tucked in behind the lobe.

"Terrace," the young man shouted. He wasn't smiling now; he was concentrating. "Terrace. Hand down. Put it down or he goes up the stair without his ears. King don't need his ears."

Vosk turned his head.

He only turned it. He looked across the floor at Eli with the cutter under his ear, and his hand stayed up, and he looked, and the King closed his fist, and Vosk's left arm broke.

Eli heard it. It was very loud, and it went on, because it broke in more than one place, above the elbow and below it and in the wrist, and the arm turned in a way it had no way of turning, and Vosk made a sound Eli had never heard him make and went down on one knee.

Eli put his hand up.

He could reach. He'd lifted a flyer out of a lake once with his hands held out, at fifteen, and been sick for a day after. It was there now. He could feel it, right there, like a word on the tip of the tongue. The man's wrist. The man's throat, against his ear, a hand's width away, wet and breathing. He had his hand up and open.

Nothing came.

His hand stayed up. The cutter pressed in behind his ear and something warm ran down his neck, and his hand stayed up in the air like a child's in a classroom, and he looked at it.

Teo hit the young man in the side of the head with a soup ladle.

It was a big iron ladle off the wrecked stall and he swung it with both hands and it made a flat sound, and the young man let go and sat down. Teo hit him again on the top of the head on the way down, carefully, as if driving a peg. Then he dropped the ladle and took Eli's wrist, the wrist of the hand that was still up, and pulled it down, and held on to it.

"Bridge," he said.

"Vosk—"

"Bridge."

Vosk came up off his knee. He came up with his good hand out in front of him and his face set, and the air round that hand bent, and the floor under the King's feet split down the middle and went up in two slabs, and the King went back a step.

One step.

All round the market the kneeling people were getting up and running, every direction, falling over each other, over the stall wreck, over the woman by the wall who wasn't getting up.

Behind Eli, Vosk was screaming. It wasn't only pain. He was screaming like someone lifting a thing too heavy for him, and the market was coming apart around him and the King. The hooks were tearing out of the ceiling and falling in their chains. Pipes in the walls had split and were blowing steam and something black. Vosk was bleeding from his nose and his ears and out of the corners of his eyes, and the skin on the backs of his knuckles had split open over the bone, all four, neat as if somebody had cut them, and steam was coming off his shoulders. He had the King against the arch. He had him pinned there. The King's chest was smoking, and some of the chips sewn into it had burst and were running, and the King was pushing back, slowly, one hand flat in the air, and the arch was cracking round him in long lines, and Vosk was walking forward into it, one step and then another, his broken arm hanging.

The arch came down.

It came down all at once, on the King and the plastic strips and two of the young men who'd been standing by it, a great sliding roar of old stone, and the dust came out across the market in a wave and over Eli and Teo where they stood at the bridge end, hot, and they couldn't see anything.

Then it was quiet. Things fell, here and there. Steam hissed.

Eli pulled his wrist out of Teo's hand and went back.

---

Vosk was on his back on the market floor with the dust settling on him.

He was steaming. Steam came off him everywhere, off his chest and his face and his good arm, and where it came off him the skin had gone dark and tight and shiny, and in places it had split, as meat splits left too long in the pan. Blood came out of his ears in two slow lines. His eyes were open. The whites of them were full of blood, both, to the edges. His broken arm was under him at an angle.

Eli knelt in the dust. He didn't know what to do with his hands. He put one on Vosk's chest and took it off; it was hot as a stove.

"It's all right," he said. "It's all right. We'll get you up. Teo. Teo, help me get him up."

Teo had come back. He stood over them. He wasn't looking at Vosk. He was looking at the heap of stone where the arch had been.

"Vosk," Eli said. "Vosk, look at me."

Vosk's eyes moved and found him. His mouth was working. When he spoke there was blood in it, and the words had a hiss on them, because something in his chest wasn't right.

"Did it," he said.

"You did. You did it. You were— I've never seen anything— Teo, help me."

"No." Vosk's good hand came up off the floor, an inch, and fell back. "Eli. No. Listen."

"We'll carry you."

"Listen." His throat moved. He was looking at Eli, but his eyes kept sliding past him, up, to one side, toward the far wall of the market. "Ilder's lot. In the yard. You were." His hand came up again and didn't get as high. "This big. They had the. His dad's. The fob. Made me dance." He breathed in, and it stuck halfway. "I was on the ground. I'd wet myself, I. Everybody laughing. And you come down off the step."

"Don't talk," Eli said. "Vosk. Don't—"

"You bit him." His face did something. It might have been a laugh. Blood came out of the corner of his mouth. "Bit Ilder. On the hand. Wouldn't let go. They had to. Your steward. You were screaming at them." His eyes went to the wall and came back. "He's mine. Leave him. He's mine."

Eli was holding his hand. He didn't remember taking it. It was too hot to hold, and he held it.

"I don't remember," he said.

"I know." Vosk breathed in, and something rattled. "I know you don't. That's all right."

"You're—"

"Proud." It came out hard, all at once, as if he'd been saving the breath for it. "I'm proud. Of the. Of what he. The boy." He stopped. When it came again it was lower. "What he grew. I'd do it for him. For you. I'd." Something rattled. "I did. I picked it."

His lips went on moving after that, and Eli bent close.

"Nobody spent me."

"Eli," said Teo.

The heap of stone where the arch had been moved. A slab near the top tilted and slid and went down the side of the heap with a long scraping noise, and dust ran after it, and from under the heap, from deep under it, came heat.

"Eli. Now."

"He's talking—"

"Go," Vosk said. "Walk." He was looking past Eli again, at the wall, at whatever was behind the wall. His hand in Eli's hand had gone loose. "Oh," he said, quite softly. "It's right there."

He didn't say anything else. His eyes stayed open on the wall. Steam kept coming off him, less and less.

Eli knelt there.

Teo took him by the back of the collar and pulled him up off the floor, the whole weight of him, and Eli's legs didn't work and he went sideways and Teo held him up against the wall of the nearest stall. Then Teo slapped him across the face, hard, open-handed, once.

"Walk," Teo said.

Eli walked.

They were nearly at the bridge end when the young men came back out of the dust round the foot of the heap, three of them, four, grey to the eyes. One of them saw the two humans and stopped. He looked at Eli. He took a step.

Teo didn't let go of Eli's wrist and didn't run. He lifted his chin at the heap, where the stone was shifting and the heat was coming out between the slabs.

"He's under there," Teo said. "You want to be up here with us when he comes out?"

The young man looked at the heap. He looked at Eli a moment more, as at a purse somebody else had dropped, and then he turned and went to the heap and got down on his knees with the others and began pulling stones off it with his hands.

The last one went to where Vosk lay and knelt on his chest with one knee, and turned his head to one side by the jaw, and took a long thin hook off his belt and put the point of it in the new strip on his neck.

Teo pulled Eli onto the bridge, and the bridge was warm under his feet all the way across.

---

They went up.

Not by the shaft. They went up the Long Stair with the rest of the Scald, which was all going up the stair that night, as many as could fit, orcs and dwarves and humans in clean coats with their shoes in their hands, and nobody talking. Every hundred steps the reader on the landing looked at them, and the orcs on either side of Eli flinched as it went cold down their arms, and he got a light. Green. His came up green with the other humans'. He had blood on his hands and on the knees of his trousers and down the side of his neck from behind his ear, and dust in his hair and his teeth, and the reader looked at him and went green.

Somewhere around the sumps Eli started to shake. It started in his hands and went up his arms, and Teo didn't say anything about it, just kept hold of his wrist and kept climbing, and Eli thought, very clearly, that he had held his hand up in front of a man with a cutter and nothing had come out of it, and Vosk had turned his head to look, and his hand was still shaking, and it would have to stop shaking before he could do anything with it, ever, and it didn't stop.

At the top of the stair the mid-levels were exactly the same. The feeds. The lanes ticking overhead. The ribbon said LEGION RECRUITMENT AT RECORD. In the flesh market at Ninth Spur they were hosing down the slabs.

Teo bought two bottles of water from a machine and they drank them standing in a doorway, and Teo was sick, neatly, once, against the doorway's side, and wiped his mouth and drank some more water and said, "Right," and they went on.

---

The gate of Ninth Terrace was a ring of dark glass in an arch of white stone.

Eli stopped in front of it. He hadn't meant to. His feet had stopped. He was filthy to the elbows and he had a dead man's blood dried in the creases of his fingers, and his face was swelling on one side where Teo had hit him, and there was a dust in his hair that the Terrace had never seen, and he stood in front of the arch and waited for it to see him.

Something in the glass looked at him.

The light came up green at his shoulder. The soft voice said, "Good evening." The arch let him through.

They walked up the white path between the water channels, under the trees, with the house windows dark on both sides. Eli's house had a light on in the kitchen. Someone always left it on.

Teo ran water in the kitchen sink and held Eli's hands under it, one and then the other, and scrubbed them with the brush the cook used for the pans, and the water went brown and then pink and then clear, and Teo kept scrubbing after it was clear, and then he stopped. He stood there with the brush. His own hands had started to shake now, at the sink, and he looked at them and put the brush down and put them in his armpits.

"She'll want to know what happened to her brush," he said. Not to Eli. To the sink.

Eli sat down at the table.

The fruit bowl was where he'd left it, and the letter was under it, pale, with the corner showing. He slid it out. It was heavy for a letter. He put his thumb on the seal, flat, and held it there, and then he put the letter down on the table face down, and his hands on either side of it.

Outside, in the yard, under the long house, the barracks bell was going for the night count. He heard the steward's voice come up through the window, flat, reading off the slate. A number, and somebody answered. A number, and somebody answered.

A number.

The steward waited. Then he made a mark, and read the next.
