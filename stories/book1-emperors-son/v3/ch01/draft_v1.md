## ONE · The Gift

The ground in the Scald stayed warm all night, and nobody down there had ever found out why.

Vosk came up through it on foot with the dwarf's price in his head. Eleven hundred, she'd said, in marks, not imperial, and she'd said it to the wall behind him while she wiped her hands. He had forty-one marks in a sock under his bunk and it had taken him nine years. He walked past the shut stalls and the sleepers on their mats, feet tucked into each other's coats for the company, under pipes that sweated something with a skin on it, and the heat came up through the soles of his boots the whole way and into his shins, and his shins ached with it the way they ached at the end of a double shift.

Nearer the Tower the air didn't sit right. It had a shine in it at head height. Bits of light drifted in the cross-streets, slow, none bigger than a seed, the colour of nothing he had a word for, and they cast no shadow on anything they passed. Drift, people called it. A girl of about eight stood in the mouth of an alley with a net of fine wire on a pole, waiting for one to come close enough. Her hands were wrapped to the elbow in rag. She didn't look at him. You didn't touch drift with skin. Everybody knew someone who had.

There were humans down here at this hour too, which was the joke of the place. Clean coats, soft shoes, standing with their foreheads to the old walls and their eyes shut, palms flat on the stone, waiting for something. Three of them in a row outside the bathhouse that had been dry since before Vosk was born. They paid orc children to mind their shoes. One of the children had already sold one of the shoes and was minding the other with great seriousness.

The walls were the oldest thing in the city that wasn't the Tower. They had been something else first, before anyone put the stalls against them, and they were the wrong shape for stalls, and the stone had no joins in it. Vosk had been told that if you had the knack, the walls talked to you. Not words. A feeling, like you'd come into a room just after a lot of people had stopped speaking. He didn't have the knack. All he ever got off the walls was the warmth, and the hairs standing up along his arms, and once, years ago, a nosebleed he'd never been able to explain to the house steward.

People who lived on the old ground got sick in ways the clinics up top didn't have names for. He passed a woman whose jaw had kept growing down one side until it had shut her eye. She was selling batteries off a blanket and she said good morning to him, though it wasn't morning yet, and he said it back. Two small boys sat on a step sharing a cake, and the nearer one held it in a hand where the fingers had grown together into one flat piece, and broke it on his knee, and gave the bigger half away.

He came round the corner into Pole Street and stopped, because everyone else had.

There were maybe forty orcs in the street, and all of them were on their knees with their faces turned to the wall, men and women, a few children. Nobody was talking. The heat was worse here, and it came in gusts, out of a doorway on the far side where a curtain of plastic strips hung down, and every time the strips moved Vosk smelled hot metal and a sweetness behind it that he knew from the yards.

He got down. He put his face to the wall like the rest and showed his hands.

Someone was walking up the line. He heard the boots stop and go on, stop and go on. They were young men's boots, light, quick, and when they stopped beside him he saw, from the corner of his eye, a pair of shins in yard trousers, and a hand hanging loose with a cutter in it, the kind you used for strapping. The hand had wire wound round the knuckles. Up the arm, at the back of the young man's neck where the chip scar should have been flat and pale, the skin had been opened and stitched shut again with the same wire, tight, in neat crosses, and the stitches wept.

"You're up late, Terrace," the young man said.

Vosk said nothing. The young man smelled his collar, actually leaned down and smelled it, and laughed through his nose and moved on.

Behind the plastic strips, something big shifted its weight, and the floor told him about it.

They stayed on their knees until the boots had gone back inside. Then a long time after that. When people did start getting up, they did it one at a time and without looking at each other, and went off in different directions, and the woman next to Vosk, who was old and had burns up both hands to the wrist, stayed down a little longer, rocking. When she got up she put her thumb to the back of his neck, on his scar, and pressed, the way you did. He did it back. Her scar was hot.

"Eating," she said. "He's eating tonight."

"I didn't see."

"No," she agreed.

She walked with him as far as the foot of the Long Stair, because it was on her way, and on the way she sang, not loud, the one about Hurrak who walked out of the yard. Vosk had heard it all his life and never all of it. Nobody sang all of it. Hurrak walked out of the yard and the dogs didn't bark, and he walked past the gate and the gate didn't bite, and he walked out of the city and kept walking. In the version his mother had sung there was a part about him carrying his own name in his mouth like a stone so he wouldn't lose it, and the old woman left that part out, and he didn't ask her why.

At the foot of the stair she said, "Walk well," and he said it back, and she went off into the Scald with her burnt hands in her armpits.

He hummed the rest of it going up.

---

The Long Stair was four thousand and some steps, and there was a reader at every landing. A ring of dark glass set in the wall at eye height for a human, at the chest for him. When you passed one, it looked. With a human it was nothing, Vosk had been told: a light, a name maybe, a little sound. With an orc the chip in the back of the neck woke up and answered it, and the answer went cold down both your arms into your hands, every hundred steps, all the way up. You got used to that the way you got used to your feet.

The other way, the readers could tell the chip to do things. That you didn't get used to.

Halfway up, the stair left the old ground and went through the sumps, where the city's water came back down to be cleaned. Then the foundations. Then the bottom of the mid-levels, where it was suddenly cooler and there were lights that worked, and grey walls with the seal on them, the Emperor's seal, the oval with nothing in it, stamped in the grey at every landing at the same height so you could have measured the stair by them. Vosk had been told once by a tutor, through a door, while the tutor was teaching someone else, that the Emperor's face was in the oval for anyone loyal enough to see it. He had looked very hard at a lot of them since, on the stairs, on crates, on the backs of buses. He didn't think it was a matter of loyalty.

The mid-levels were waking up. They were never all the way asleep. Above the street, the sky, what you could see of it between the towers, was the same colour it always was, the colour of a lamp seen through a dirty sheet, and the traffic lanes ran across it in strings of light, three deep, the lowest close enough that you could hear the hulls tick as they cooled. The feeds were on. A woman's face as tall as a building said something about the elves to nobody. Under her, the ribbon. PLAGUE HOLD EXTENDED, THREE SYSTEMS. LEGION RECRUITMENT AT RECORD. TERRACE PROPERTY VALUES. Somebody had thrown something at the ribbon and it had stuck there, brown, over the word VALUES.

The flesh market at Ninth Spur was opening. That was where Vosk cut through, because it was quicker and the stalls were full of men too busy to see him. They were hosing down the slabs. Steam came off the water. The cold-cases were going up on their trestles, and every case had a stamp on the end the size of a hand, black on white, CLONE STOCK — NO SOUL, with the grade under it. A woman in an apron was laying out tongues in rows on ice, and a man at the next stall had a basket of eyes he was sorting by colour, and a dwarf further down sold hands in pairs, matched, with a little paper tag on each thumb. Vosk had worked a season here when he was small, carrying ice. He knew the bins round the back where the off-grade went. He knew what an orc arm sold for, which was less than a goat's.

At the end of the row a man was shouting, "Real! Real, not clone, real!" and a young woman with a shopping bag stopped and asked him, quite politely, how she was meant to tell.

"Because it costs more," the man said.

She seemed to think that was fair, and bought some.

The lift at the top of the Spur had a core. You could see it through a little window in the housing, if you wanted to, a grey lump the size of a loaf in a glass box, wet, with threads going out of it into the works, and a plate underneath: POOL CORE — CERTIFIED. When the lift was coming down, the lump tightened, very slightly, the way a hand tightens. Orcs didn't ride the lift. The cheap lifts in the Scald had cores you weren't meant to look at, sold as pool, that the dwarves who sold them called something else when they thought nobody was listening, and Vosk didn't ride those either.

He took the culvert.

From the culvert's mouth you could see the Tower, all of it, if you leaned back. You couldn't anywhere else in the mid-levels. It came up out of the middle of everything, out of the oldest ground, pale and seamless and so big that the clouds broke round it, and the lanes bent away from it in a wide circle, and nothing anybody had built came within a mile of its foot. The rich said it was warm on your face. They said it the way they said a good wine was warm. The Terrace was built on the first rise of land that looked at it, and the houses there were built with their best windows facing it, and people paid for those windows more than Vosk could count.

He leaned back and looked at it, because it was there. It was a tall pale thing and he didn't feel anything, the same as always.

---

The service gate for Ninth Terrace was a slot in a wall at the top of the culvert, one orc wide. Vosk was fourteen minutes past the count.

He knew it before he got to the slot, because the slot knew, and the chip knew, and it started before he could get his shoulder through. It always started in the jaw. It felt the way biting on foil felt, and then the foil was in the bones of his face and down his neck, and then it went into the long muscles of his back and pulled them all at once in the same direction. He hit the wall of the slot with his head. He wasn't sure how long it lasted; it never lasted long. When it let go, he was on his knees in the slot with his mouth full of spit and his hands open in front of him, the way they taught you to hold them, and somewhere far off the gate said, in a pleasant voice, "Late. Logged."

He got up. He went through.

The Terrace was the other side of the slot, all at once, every time. Water ran down channels cut in the white stone. There were trees, real ones, with real dirt round their feet that orcs changed every month. The air was cool, and it smelled of nothing much, which cost more than any smell. The houses stood back from the paths with their good windows toward the Tower, and in the first-floor window of the third house a boy of about nine was eating breakfast in a white shirt with a fob on a ribbon round his neck, and down on the lawn the family's yard orc, a grown man, was hopping. Just hopping. On one foot, then the other, very fast, with his arms tight to his sides and his face screwed up. The boy wasn't laughing. He was watching, with his spoon halfway up, interested, the way you'd watch a bug turn over.

Vosk put his own thumb to the back of his own neck, and pressed, and kept walking.

In the barracks under the long house, the others were already up and going. Somebody had kept him a bit of bread. He sat on his bunk and ate it and took the sock out from under the mattress and counted the forty-one marks out on his knee, slowly, as if he might have miscounted for nine years.

Eleven hundred.

He put the marks back. He sat with the sock in his lap.

Then he went out into the yard and stood under the third window from the end on the second floor, which had been the same window since he was twelve, and found a bit of gravel at the edge of the path, and threw it.

---

The money came in a dwarven purse, the kind with a clasp that bit, and Eli had put it on the kitchen table next to the fruit bowl so he could see it while he talked.

"Eleven hundred," he said. "In marks. They didn't even ask."

"Who's they?" said Teo.

"The account."

"The account didn't ask."

"It never asks." Eli turned the purse over. It was heavier than he'd expected money to be. Money was a number on a slate; he'd never held eleven hundred of anything. "It's what it's for."

The account had come with the house, and the house had come with him, before he could remember. It paid the steward and the cook and the tutors, when there had been tutors, and the orcs in the barracks, and it had paid for his first flyer and his second, and for the window that had gone in after he put a chair through it at fifteen. It had never once said no. It was his father's account, or his father's people's, and Eli had stopped thinking about the difference a long time ago.

Teo sat on the counter by the cold-box with his heels against the doors. He was eating an apple slowly from the bottom up, which was how he'd eaten them since they were seven, and which Eli had given up trying to understand.

"He asked you for money," Teo said.

"He asked me for a loan."

"He asked you for a loan." Teo looked at the apple. "He didn't ask you to come."

"He didn't have to." Eli got up. He couldn't sit still with it. "He's going down to the Scald on his own, at night, with eleven hundred marks on him, to see some dwarf in a back room about his neck. On his own. You heard him. Can you see him doing that? Vosk?" He laughed. "He'd be robbed before the second landing. They'd have the boots off him. He's soft, you know he's soft, he cried when the cook drowned the kittens."

"He was eleven."

"Well, he's still soft." Eli picked an orange out of the bowl and put it back. "It's what friends do."

On the wall behind Teo the feed was running with the sound off. Grey men at a grey table. The ribbon underneath said ELVEN DELEGATION RECALLED and then INQUISITION ESCORT INCLUDES DUELIST KNOWN AS "THE QUEEN" and then something about rates. Neither of them looked at it.

The letter was under the fruit bowl.

It had come that morning, by hand. A courier in a grey coat, a woman, who had stood in the doorway and waited, saying nothing, until the steward had fetched Eli down himself, and then had held it out and waited again until it was in his hand, actually in his fingers, and then gone. The envelope was heavy and pale and there was nothing written on it, and on the back, pressed into a disc of grey wax, was the oval with nothing in it.

He'd looked at it for a while in the hall. Then he'd brought it in here and put the bowl on it.

"Are you going to open that," said Teo.

"Later." Eli slid the purse into the inside pocket of his coat, and patted it. "Tomorrow. It's a letter. It'll keep. It's kept twenty-four years, it can keep tonight."

Teo finished the apple and looked at the core for somewhere to put it and ended up holding it.

There was a knock on the yard door, two short, which was Vosk's knock, and Eli was across the kitchen before the second one had finished.

Vosk filled the door. He always had, since he'd got his growth, and he always ducked coming through it, even though he didn't need to, the top of his head an inch under the frame. He was in his work clothes still, the grey canvas, and he had a cut on his forehead that Eli hadn't seen that morning, scabbed, with grit in it.

"What happened to your head?"

"Wall."

"Sit down. Have you eaten? You haven't eaten." Eli was already at the cold-box. "There's the end of the bird. Teo, move."

"I'm all right," Vosk said.

"Sit down."

Vosk sat on the step inside the yard door, which was where he sat, and Eli put the plate down on the step beside him, and the fork on the plate, and then a cloth, because he was always forgetting the cloth. Then he crouched in front of him with his forearms on his knees.

"We're coming with you," he said.

Vosk looked at him. Then he looked past him at Teo, who had nowhere to put the apple core and was holding it in his fist.

"It's quicker on my own," Vosk said.

"Don't be stupid."

"I know the way. I go down there."

"I know you go down there. That's how you got your head opened." Eli reached out and put his hand on the back of Vosk's neck, over the scar, and gave it a squeeze and a little shake, the way he'd been doing since they were boys. The scar was warm under his palm and a little raised. "Eleven hundred marks in the Scald at night. You'll get a knife in you for the purse. And then what? Who's going to carry the ice for the cook? Me?" He let go and stood up. "I've got the money. You've got the dwarf. Teo's got the route."

"I haven't got the route," said Teo.

"You'll get the route. You always get the route."

Vosk had picked up the fork. He turned it over. He put it down on the plate again, very square, next to the bird.

"Eli," he said.

"It's done. I've done it. Eat your bird." Eli grinned down at him. "When have I ever not come?"

Vosk ate the bird.

---

They went out through the front.

The gate of Ninth Terrace was a ring of dark glass in an arch of white stone, and Eli walked through it the way he walked through every door, without slowing. Something in the glass looked at him. A light came up green on the stone at his shoulder, and a soft voice said, "Good evening," without a name, and the arch let him out onto the public ramp. It did the same for Teo. Teo always slowed, very slightly, as he went through, and Eli had never asked him why.

Vosk wasn't with them. Vosk had gone down to the culvert. The front gate didn't read orcs. It bit them.

"That's stupid," Eli had said in the yard, "come through with us, I'll tell them," and Vosk had said, "It bites," and gone the way he always went, and they found him at the bottom of the public ramp twenty minutes later, standing very still against a pillar where the light didn't reach, with his hands where people could see them.

"See," said Eli, "no trouble," and clapped him on the arm.

The mid-levels at night were brighter than the mid-levels by day. Eli loved them for it. He'd been coming down since he was fifteen, with Teo, with other people, for food, for the clubs on Ancel Row, for no reason, and every time it was like taking a breath of something too strong. Noise came down from the lanes overhead and up from the streets underneath, and the feeds were so many and so big that the light off them shifted on your skin like water. People everywhere, more people than the Terrace had in a year, all of them going somewhere at once. Every face that passed under a reader got a little light. Green, green, green. You stopped seeing it. Twice a man in a grey coat was standing at the edge of the crowd looking at nothing, with a seal on his collar, and twice Eli felt the man not look at him, and didn't think about it.

They didn't take the Long Stair. Teo had found something better, which was a service shaft off the sumps where the cleaning crews went down. He'd found it on a slate that afternoon, and he went down it first without saying anything, and the ladder rungs were slick and warm, and the air got warmer every rung.

At the bottom of the shaft the floor was the wrong size.

Eli stood at the foot of the ladder and looked at it. They were on a street. It was a street; it had a gutter, and a curb, and on the other side another curb. But the curb on this side came up to his chest, and the one on the far side was a long way off, so far that the lamps strung across between them sagged in the middle, and the street between had been filled in with poured stuff to make a floor at a sensible height, and people had built shacks on it, and a ramp went up over the curb made of pallets and somebody's door.

"Stoop," said Vosk. "That's the Stoop."

Eli walked to the curb and put his hands on it. Stone, grown not cut, no seam in it, smooth from people's hands where the ramp met it. Along the base of the wall beyond, a doorway stood in the stone, far bigger than any door, three times his height, with no handle and no hinge and no line round it, just a doorshape pressed into the wall, a slab you could tell was meant to move. Somebody had painted something on it, long ago, and somebody else had painted over that. Down at the bottom, at the height of a person's hands, there were burns in the stone, and drill holes, a lot of them, in neat rows, none of them more than a thumb deep.

"What's in there?"

"Nobody's opened it," Vosk said.

"Somebody's tried," said Teo.

Eli laughed. He had the feeling he got at the edge of the clubs, that he'd found something nobody up top had any idea about. He wanted to tell someone. He put his palm flat on the door and it was cool, the only cool thing he'd touched since the ladder.

"How old is this?"

Vosk shrugged. "Old."

"Who builds a door you can't open?"

"Somebody bigger," Teo said, and went up the ramp.

---

The Scald was hot. Eli had known it would be. He hadn't known the heat would have a direction.

It came from in front of them, and a little up, as if there were a fire somewhere ahead on a high shelf, and it pushed on his face and his chest the whole way, and the further they went the more it pushed. He took off his coat and carried it, and then he put it back on because of the purse. Sweat ran into the small of his back. The air tasted of metal and of fat burning. The stalls were open and there were a lot of people, orcs mostly, and they moved round Eli in the crowd without touching him, a little gap always opening a half step before he got there and closing behind.

Vosk walked in front. He walked differently down here, Eli noticed. Looser. His hands weren't held out. Once, a big orc with a split ear stepped into his way and the two of them put their thumbs to the backs of each other's necks and said something low in the orc talk, and the other one laughed and looked past Vosk at Eli, and said something else, and Vosk said, "No," quite sharply, and they walked on.

"What did he say?"

"Nothing."

"He said something."

"He asked was you mine."

Eli laughed. "What did you say?"

Vosk didn't answer, and they came to the bridge.

It went over a cut in the ground where the light was. That was the only way Eli could think of it afterward. The cut ran away on both sides, forty feet down, maybe more, and the bottom of it wasn't dark the way the bottom of a hole should be; it was full of a slow shining like the inside of an eye. Bits of the shine came up out of it and drifted. Up on the edges, people stood with nets on long poles, children mostly, wrapped in rags. One of them had a jar with three of the bits in it, held up to her face, and the bits didn't light her face at all.

The bridge itself was one long curved girder with a deck laid on top, and the girder was ribbed, like a spine laid on its side, and it was dark grey going brown, and it went from one side of the cut to the other in one piece. Eli put his hand on it as he stepped onto the deck. Then he stopped and put his hand back.

It was warm. Not like the stones of the Scald. Warm like a horse is warm.

"Don't," said Vosk.

"It's warm."

"Don't touch it."

There was a stamp in the metal, low down, half rubbed away, a dwarven salvage mark Eli half-knew from the flyer yards, and under that a number. Teo bent to look at it. He straightened up and wiped his fingers on his trousers, though he hadn't touched anything, and walked across the bridge looking straight ahead.

"What is it?" Eli said behind him.

"It's a ship," said Teo.

"It's a bridge."

"It was a ship." Teo didn't turn round. "Come on."

---

Hookline was the market under the bridge's far end. It was called that, Vosk said, for the hooks, and Eli looked up and saw them: the whole ceiling, which was low here, hung with hooks on chains, thousands of them, and on half of them something. Coats. Lamps. Pots. Meat. A whole side of something red and white, sawn down the middle, turning slowly on its hook in the heat coming up off the cut, with a paper tag on the shank that said nothing at all, not even a grade.

There was noise everywhere and music from three places at once. Someone was singing. Someone else was selling soup out of a hot-box with a core in it, a little orc-grey core you could see through the vent, and the soup was good, Eli could smell it was good, and Teo caught his sleeve before he could stop.

"Don't eat anything."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

An old dwarf sat on a crate at the corner of the soup stall. He was very old. Eli hadn't known dwarves got that old; he didn't know any dwarves, except the ones who came to fix things. This one had a box on his chest. It was strapped there, and it was also not strapped there; the skin of his chest had grown over the straps at the edges, puckered and shiny, and tubes went from the box into the side of his neck and under his beard, and the box was ticking. Not loud. Just steadily, like something winding. The skin on the backs of his hands was in patches, some of it old and spotted and some of it smooth and pink as a child's, with a line round each patch where it had been put on.

He saw Eli looking. He had very bright eyes in a dead face.

"Ninety-four," he said. "You want to see a hundred, boy, you start saving now."

Eli didn't know what to say to that. He said, "Congratulations."

The dwarf laughed, a long time, and the box ticked faster while he did, and then slowed again.

They went on through the market. A girl of about twelve with a shaved head was sitting on the rail of a stall across the way with her feet tucked up, not selling anything, and when Eli looked at her she looked at the soup.

Teo was walking very close to him now. "That girl was at the Stoop," he said.

"What girl?"

"With the shaved head. Behind you. Don't look."

Eli looked. She was eating something out of her hand. "She's a kid."

"She was at the Stoop. She was on the bridge. Now she's here."

"So she lives here. It's a market."

"Eli."

"She's a kid, Teo." He put his arm round Teo's shoulders and gave him a shake. "Look at you. You've gone grey. Vosk, look at him."

Vosk didn't look. He was looking at the girl. Then he turned off into an alley between two stalls, and they followed him.

---

The dwarf was called Velk, or that was what it said on the shutter of the shop she worked out of the back of, which had sold water once and still had the tanks. She was short even for a dwarf and very broad, and she had a scar that ran from her hairline down through one eyebrow and on down her cheek, and the eye under the eyebrow was a different colour from the other one, a very pale grey, and didn't move when the other one did.

She looked at Eli. She looked at Teo. She looked at Vosk.

"You said you'd come on your own," she said.

"He's paying," said Vosk.

"He's paying." She looked at Eli again, at his coat, his hands, his boots, and Eli felt himself priced. "Course he is. Well, come in. Don't touch anything."

The back of the shop was a long room with a low ceiling and shelves on every wall, and on the shelves were things. Eli couldn't stop looking at them. A plain grey cube about the size of his fist that sat on its shelf at a very slight tilt, as if it were heavier on one side, and while he watched it tilted the other way. A jar of water lying on its side with the lid off, and none of the water coming out. A coin standing on its edge on a saucer. A length of something like rope that was the wrong colour to look at, so that his eyes kept sliding off it onto the shelf beside it. A small brass bell with no clapper.

"What are these?"

"Stock," said Velk.

"Where are they from?"

"They came out."

"Out of where?"

She was washing her hands at a basin in the corner, with a brush, hard, up to the elbows. "Out," she said. "If you break one, you've bought it, because they don't go back."

Eli touched the coin with one finger. It didn't fall over. He took his finger away fast.

"Depot's taking a third this year," she said, to nobody, still scrubbing. "A third. On things like that. On a coin. And then there's his cut on top. And then they wonder."

"Whose cut?" said Teo.

Velk dried her arms and didn't answer. She pulled a chair out from under a table into the light, a heavy chair with a high back and straps on the arms, and she nodded at it, and Vosk sat down in it and put his own arms in the straps.

Eli took out the purse.

She counted it on the table without hurry, in stacks of fifty, wetting her thumb, and while she counted she hummed something, low in her throat, a tune that went up and then turned round on itself and came down a different way. Eli half knew it. He couldn't have said from where. It snagged on something, the way a smell does, and then she finished counting and stopped humming and it was gone.

"Eleven hundred," she said, and swept it into a drawer. "Hold his head."

Eli stood there.

"His head," said Velk. "Hold it. Forward. Chin down. Unless you'd rather do the knife."

Eli held his head.

Up close, Vosk's hair smelled of the barracks soap, and the back of his neck was very hot, and the scar was there under Eli's fingers where it had always been, a pale ridge as long as a thumb. Eli had touched it a thousand times. He'd never thought about what was under it. Velk swabbed it with something cold that smelled of nothing and laid out her tools on a cloth, small bright things, and picked one, and put the point of it in the old scar at the top end and drew it down.

Vosk's whole body went tight in the chair.

There was more blood than Eli had expected, and it was darker. It ran down into the collar of Vosk's work shirt and Velk didn't bother to catch it. She opened the cut with two little hooks and held it, and leaned in, and Eli could see down into it, past the red, to something grey and shining with threads going out from it into the meat like roots. She took hold of it with a pair of narrow pliers and began to pull, slowly, turning, and the threads came out one at a time with a sound like wet paper, and Vosk said something through his teeth in the orc talk, the same three words over and over, and his hands in the straps opened and shut.

"Talk to him," said Velk. "If you're going to stand there."

"It's all right," said Eli. "It's all right. You're doing well. You're doing so well."

The thing came out. It was the size of a fingernail, grey, with its threads trailing, and she dropped it in a dish, and in the dish it moved. Just once, a little curl of the threads, like something closing.

Teo had gone to stand by the door.

The new one came out of a sealed packet. It was bigger than the old one, darker, ridged on one side. There was a mark pressed into the ridged side and someone had taken a file to the mark, but not well enough; Eli could see the curve of an oval, and part of the letters underneath. LEGION.

"Soldier's," said Velk, seeing him see it. "Out of a soldier. I've had the leash out of it. It'll pull for him, and nobody pulls on him." She was fitting it into the cut as she talked, pressing it home with her thumb, and the threads on the new one were moving, already, finding their way. "It's on their net. Same as every grunt in a Legion barracks from here to the rim. Nobody looks for an orc on it because nobody thinks one could be." She glanced at Eli with her live eye. "You understand what you've bought him."

"Yes," said Eli.

She looked at him a little longer, and then she went back to her work.

She closed the cut with glue and a strip of something that pulled tight as it dried. Then she undid the straps. Vosk didn't get up. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his head hanging, breathing hard, and blood dripped off the end of his nose onto the floor between his boots.

Then he lifted his head.

He didn't look at any of them. He turned his face, slowly, to the far wall of the shop, the one with the shelves of things that had come out, and he looked at it as though there was a window there.

"What's that," he said.

His voice was strange. Eli crouched in front of him. "What's what?"

"That." Vosk had his eyes wide open. "It's. Up. It's right there."

"That's the Tower," said Velk, wiping her tools. "Everybody gets that the first time. You'll stop noticing." She dropped the tools in a tray. "It's like having the sun in the next room."

Vosk's lips were moving. He put his hand up, flat, toward the wall, and held it there, the way you'd hold it up to a fire.

"Is it hot?" Eli said. "Vosk. Does it hurt?"

Vosk looked at him then. He looked at Eli's face for a long time, as if it was something he'd seen before somewhere and was trying to place. Then he looked back at the wall, and down at his hand, and he closed the hand.

"No," he said.

Eli laughed out loud, mostly from relief. He gripped Vosk's knee. "Look at you. Look at him, Teo." He stood up. "That one," he said to Velk, pointing at the coin on its edge. "What do you want for that?"

"Eli," said Teo, from the door.

"For Teo. He's been a misery all night, he deserves something. What's it do? Does it just stand there?"

"It just stands there," said Velk.

"How much?"

At the back of the room a boy Eli hadn't seen, a thin dwarf boy of maybe ten, got up off a stool behind the tanks, put down a cup, and went out through a curtain at the back without a word.

"Where's he going?" said Teo.

Velk didn't look round. "Tax," she said.

Teo came across the room. He took hold of Eli's arm above the elbow, not hard. His face was very calm, the calm it went when an exam was going badly.

"Pay her and we go," he said. "Now."

"I'm paying her. I'm asking her a price." Eli shook the hand off, not unkindly. "One minute."

"Eli."

"One minute, Teo."

Vosk was standing. Eli hadn't seen him get up. He was standing very straight with his head a little tilted, and his face was turned not toward the wall and the Tower now but toward the curtain at the back where the boy had gone, and the blood had stopped coming from his nose and was drying on his lip, and he was listening.

"It's gone quiet," Vosk said.

---

It had. Eli hadn't noticed the market's noise until there wasn't any. No music. No one selling soup. The heat was still there, pushing through the shutter at the front, and something else had come into it, a smell, hot metal and a sweetness behind the metal that made his stomach turn without him knowing why.

Velk had sat down on her stool behind the table with her hands in her lap. "Out the front," she said. "Not the back." She was looking at the table. "And I didn't fit it."

They went out the front.

The alley was empty. The market at the end of it was not. Everybody was still there, the stallholders, the soup man, the girl with the shaved head; they were all on their knees, every one, facing the nearest wall. The old dwarf with the ticking box was on his knees on his crate, forehead to the soup stall's side, and his box was ticking very fast. Nobody looked up. On the hooks overhead the meat turned in the heat.

"Back to the bridge," said Teo, very low. "Walk. Don't run."

Eli walked. Teo had him by the sleeve. Vosk was a step behind them. They came out of the alley mouth into the market and went along the wall past all the bowed heads, toward the bridge end, and from the other side of the market, out of a wide arch with plastic strips hung across it, young men began to come.

Six. Eight. Orcs, young, light on their feet, in yard trousers, with wire wound round their knuckles and their necks opened and stitched with wire. They didn't hurry. They spread out across the market floor between the kneeling people and the bridge end, and stopped there, and one of them, not the biggest, smiled at Vosk and touched his own stitched neck and then pointed at Vosk's, where the new strip was.

"That's a nice one, Terrace," he said.

"It's paid for," said Eli. He heard his own voice come out clear and reasonable, the voice he used with the house steward. "If there's some sort of fee, I'm happy to—"

The young man looked at him. Then he looked at Vosk again, as if Eli had made a noise in another room.

The plastic strips moved.

The heat came out first. Eli felt it on his face and on the backs of his hands, a hard dry push like opening an oven, and every bowed head in the market went lower, and on the hooks overhead the fat on the sawn carcass started to run. Then the strips parted, and something came through the arch that had to turn its shoulders to fit.

He was an orc. Eli would think that, later, and then not be sure. He was the shape of an orc, the heavy shoulders and the long arms, but he was half again as tall as Vosk and twice as broad and the light from the cut came up under him and made him wrong. His skin was wrong. It was crusted all over the chest and arms and the slabs of the shoulders with something in rows, and the rows were chips. Orc chips, grey, with their threads, hundreds of them, sewn into him one by one through the skin with wire, the way you'd sew buttons on a coat, and every one of them was wet, and every one of them, faintly, was moving. He wore nothing on top. He had a yard apron on, a real one, rubber, black, stiff with old use, like the men at the presses wore.

He didn't say anything. He looked at Vosk. He looked at the strip on Vosk's neck.

Then he looked at Eli. Not long. Up, and down. The way the woman at the flesh market had looked at the tongues.

"He'll have the new one," said the young man with the smile. "And he'll have that."

"We're leaving," Eli said. His voice wasn't the steward voice now. "We've paid, we're—"

"Teo," Vosk said. "Take him up the stair."

"I'm not leaving you here—"

"Teo."

Teo had both hands in Eli's coat. Eli could feel them shaking and he could hear Teo's breathing, and it was slow, slow, in through the nose, out through the mouth, the way Teo had breathed through the oral finals and the time the flyer lost a fan over the sumps.

The King lifted one hand, palm up, as if he was weighing something.

Every person kneeling in the market sagged. All of them, all at once, the soup man and the girl and the old dwarf, as if something had gone out of them through the backs of their necks. The old dwarf's box stopped ticking, and started again. And the air in front of the King went thick and bright, and it came across the market floor at them like a wall falling over.

Vosk put his hand up.

Eli would never be able to say what he saw. He saw the air between Vosk's hand and the King's go white, and then he didn't see anything, because the noise hit him, and the floor came up under his feet and dropped away again, and a stall to his left came apart into its boards and its pots and a woman who had been kneeling behind it, and all of it went sideways through the air together and into the wall. Hooks on their chains swung out level and came back. The sawn carcass fell. Two of the young men in yard trousers weren't standing any more; one was lying under the soup stall's hot-box with his legs out, and the other wasn't anywhere Eli could see.

Vosk was standing where he'd stood. He looked at his hand.

The King looked at him with something like interest.

Then he closed his hand, and Vosk's left arm broke.

Eli heard it. It was very loud, and it went on, because it broke in more than one place, above the elbow and below it and in the wrist, and the arm turned in a way it had no way of turning, and Vosk made a sound Eli had never heard him make and went down on one knee. And came up again. He came up with his good hand out in front of him and his face set, and the air round that hand bent, and the floor under the King's feet split down the middle and went up in two slabs, and the King went back a step.

One step.

All round the market, the kneeling people were getting up and running, in every direction, falling over each other, over the stall wreck, over the woman by the wall who wasn't getting up. The girl with the shaved head ran right past Eli, close enough to touch, and didn't look at him.

Something took hold of the back of Eli's coat.

It was one of the young men, the one with the smile. He'd come round behind them while Eli was watching, and he had Eli by the collar in one wired fist, and in the other hand there was a strapping cutter, and he wasn't smiling now; he was concentrating. He pulled Eli back against him. The cutter came round.

Eli put his hand up.

He could reach. He'd always been able to reach; he'd known since he was six and pulled a door off its hinges in a temper, and his tutors had made faces about it, and at fifteen he'd lifted Teo's flyer out of the lake with nothing but his hands held out and been sick afterwards for a day. It was there now. He could feel it, right there, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. The man's wrist. The man's throat, which was right there against his ear, a hand's width away, wet and breathing. He had his hand up and open.

Nothing came.

His hand stayed up. The cutter came round toward his face, and his hand stayed up in the air like a child's in a classroom, and he looked at it.

Teo hit the young man in the side of the head with a soup ladle.

It was a big iron ladle off the wrecked stall, and he swung it with both hands, and it made a flat sound, and the young man let go and sat down. Teo hit him again on the top of the head on the way down, carefully, the way you'd drive a peg. Then he dropped the ladle and took Eli's wrist, the wrist of the hand that was still up, and pulled it down, and held on to it.

"Bridge," he said.

"Vosk—"

"Bridge."

Behind them Vosk was screaming. It wasn't pain. Or it wasn't only pain. He was screaming the way you'd scream lifting something too heavy, and the market was coming apart around him and the King. The hooks were tearing out of the ceiling and falling in their chains. Pipes in the walls had split and were blowing steam and something black. Vosk was bleeding from his nose and his ears, and out of the corners of his eyes, and the skin on the backs of his knuckles had split open over the bone, all four knuckles, neat as if somebody had cut them, and steam was coming off his shoulders. He had the King against the arch. He had him pinned there. The King's chest was smoking, and some of the chips sewn into it had burst open and were running, and the King was pushing back, slowly, one hand flat in the air, and the arch was cracking around him in long lines, and Vosk was walking forward into it, one step and then another, his broken arm hanging.

The arch came down.

It came down all at once, on the King and on the plastic strips and on two of the young men who'd been standing by it, a great sliding roar of old stone and dust, and the dust came out across the market in a wave and over Eli and Teo where they stood at the bridge end, hot, and they couldn't see anything.

Then it was quiet. Things fell, here and there. Steam hissed.

Eli pulled his wrist out of Teo's hand and went back.

---

Vosk was on his back on the floor of the market with the dust settling on him.

He was steaming. That was the first thing. Steam came off him everywhere, off his chest and his face and his good arm, and where it came off his skin the skin had gone dark and tight and shiny, and in places it had split, the way meat splits when it's been too long in the pan. Blood was coming out of his ears in two slow lines. His eyes were open. They were full of blood, the whites of them, both, all the way to the edges. His broken arm was under him at an angle.

Eli knelt in the dust. He didn't know what to do with his hands. He put one on Vosk's chest and took it off; it was hot as a stove.

"It's all right," he said. "It's all right. We'll get you up. Teo. Teo, help me get him up."

Teo had come back. He stood over them. He wasn't looking at Vosk. He was looking at the heap of stone where the arch had been.

"Vosk," Eli said. "Vosk, look at me."

Vosk's eyes moved and found him. His mouth was working. When he spoke there was blood in it, and the words came out with a hiss on them, because something in his chest wasn't right.

"Did it," he said.

"You did. You did it. You were— I've never seen anything— Teo, help me."

"No." Vosk's good hand came up off the floor, an inch, and fell back. "Eli. No. Listen."

"We'll carry you."

"Listen." His throat moved. He was looking at Eli, but his eyes kept sliding past him, up, to one side, toward the far wall of the market, toward where the Tower was. "You were. You were this big." His hand came up again and didn't get as high. "Ilder's lot. In the yard. They had the fob, the. His dad's fob. Made me dance. I was. I was on the ground, I'd. Wet myself. Everybody laughing. And you came down off the. Off the step. You were this big." His face did something. It might have been a laugh. Blood came out of the corner of his mouth. "You bit him. You bit Ilder. On the hand. You wouldn't let go. They had to. Your steward came. You were screaming at them. He's mine. Leave him. He's mine."

Eli was holding his hand. He didn't remember taking it. It was too hot to hold, and he held it.

"I don't remember," he said.

"I know." Vosk breathed in, and something rattled. "I know you don't. That's. That's all right." His eyes came back from the wall and found Eli's face again and stayed on it. "Nobody ever. Said it like that. Like it was. Something. You looked at me like I was. Something."

"You are. You're—"

"I'm proud." It came out hard, all at once, as if he'd been saving the breath. "I'm proud of. Of the. Of what you. The boy, what he. Grew. I'd do it for him. I'd do it for you. I'd. I did. I picked it. Nobody spent me." He swallowed blood. "Tell Hurrak's. No. Tell. There's a sock. Under my. Forty-one. Give it to the cook, she. She always."

"Eli," said Teo.

The heap of stone where the arch had been moved. Not much. A slab near the top tilted and slid off and went down the side of the heap with a long scraping noise, and dust ran after it, and from under the heap, from deep under it, came heat.

"Eli. Now."

"He's talking—"

"Go," Vosk said. "Walk. Walk well. Walk." He was looking past Eli again, at the wall, at whatever was behind the wall. His hand in Eli's hand had gone loose. "Oh," he said, quite softly. "It's right there."

He didn't say anything else. His eyes stayed open on the wall. Steam kept coming off him, less and less.

Eli knelt there.

Teo bent and took hold of him by the back of the collar and pulled him up off the floor, the whole weight of him, and Eli's legs didn't work and he went sideways and Teo held him up against the wall of the nearest stall. Then Teo slapped him across the face, quite hard, open-handed, once.

"Walk," Teo said.

Eli walked.

At the bridge end he looked back, because he couldn't not. The heap of stone was moving again. Out of the dust round its foot the young men in yard trousers were coming back, three of them, four, and one went straight to where Vosk lay and knelt on his chest with one knee, the way you'd kneel on a crate to get the lid off, and turned his head to one side by the jaw, and took a long thin hook off his belt and put the point of it in the new strip on his neck.

Teo pulled him onto the bridge, and the bridge was warm under his feet all the way across.

---

They went up.

Not by the shaft. Teo didn't trust the shaft now. They went up the Long Stair with the rest of the Scald, which was all going up the stair that night, as many people as could fit, orcs and dwarves and humans in clean coats with their shoes in their hands, and nobody talking. Every hundred steps the reader on the landing looked at them. Green, green. Eli's light came up green with everyone else's. He had blood on his hands and on the knees of his trousers and dust in his hair and down his collar and in his teeth, and the reader looked at him and went green.

On the six hundredth landing, or the seven hundredth, a man was sitting on the step with his back to the wall and his legs drawn up out of the way. He had a paper on his knee with fruit in it, and he was eating something. A peach. It was the only clean thing Eli had seen since the bridge. When Teo pulled Eli past, the man moved his knees a little further in to let them by, still chewing, and said nothing, and Eli didn't look at his face.

Somewhere around the sumps Eli started to shake. It started in his hands and went up his arms, and Teo didn't say anything about it, just kept hold of his wrist and kept climbing, and Eli thought, with great clarity, that he had held his hand up in the air in front of a man with a cutter and nothing had come out of it, and that his hand was still shaking, and that it would have to stop shaking before he could do anything with it, ever, and it didn't stop.

At the top of the stair the mid-levels were exactly the same. The feeds. The lanes ticking overhead. The ribbon said LEGION RECRUITMENT AT RECORD. In the flesh market at Ninth Spur they were hosing down the slabs.

Teo bought two bottles of water from a machine and they drank them standing in a doorway, and Teo was sick, neatly, once, against the doorway's side, and wiped his mouth and drank some more water and said, "Right," and they went on.

---

The gate of Ninth Terrace was a ring of dark glass in an arch of white stone.

Eli stopped in front of it. He hadn't meant to. His feet had stopped. He was filthy to the elbows and he had a dead man's blood dried in the creases of his fingers, and his face was swelling on one side where Teo had hit him, and there was a dust in his hair that the Terrace had never seen, and he stood in front of the arch and waited for it to see him.

Something in the glass looked at him.

The light came up green at his shoulder. The soft voice said, "Good evening." The arch let him through.

It did the same for Teo, who slowed going through, very slightly, as he always did.

Neither of them said anything. They walked up the white path between the water channels, under the trees, with the house windows dark on both sides and turned toward the Tower, and on the second floor of the third house the boy in the white shirt was asleep, presumably, with his fob on its ribbon on the table beside the bed. Eli's house had a light on in the kitchen. Someone always left it on.

Teo ran water in the kitchen sink and held Eli's hands under it, one and then the other, and scrubbed them with the brush the cook used for the pans, and the water went brown and then pink and then clear, and Teo kept scrubbing after it was clear, and then he stopped. He stood there with the brush. His own hands had started to shake, now, at the sink, and he looked at them and put the brush down and put them in his armpits.

"I told you," he said. Not to Eli. To the sink.

Eli sat down at the table.

The fruit bowl was where he'd left it, and the letter was under it, pale, with the corner showing. He slid it out. The grey wax disc on the back had a little crack in it now from the weight of the bowl, a hair-thin line running across the oval with nothing in it, edge to edge. He held it. It was heavy for a letter. He put his thumb on the seal, flat, and held it there, and then he put the letter down on the table in front of him, face down, and his hands on either side of it.

Outside, in the yard, under the long house, the barracks bell was going for the night count. He heard the steward's voice come up through the window, flat, reading off the slate, the way it did every night. A number, and somebody answered. A number, and somebody answered.

A number.

The steward waited. Then he made a mark, and read the next.
