# The Emperor's Son

## ONE · The Gift

The ground in the Scald stayed warm all night, and nobody down there had ever found out why.

Vosk walked up out of it with the dwarf's price in his head. Eleven hundred, she'd said, in marks, not imperial. She'd said it to the wall over his shoulder while she wiped her hands, and then she'd put two fingers on the back of his neck to show him where. Tomorrow night, she'd said, or it went to someone else. He had forty-one marks in a sock under his bunk. It had taken him nine years.

He had come off the evening shift and gone straight down to her, straight past the bell for the night count, and now he was heading back with the night gone, past the shut stalls and the sleepers on their mats, feet tucked into each other's coats for the company, under pipes that sweated a slick with a skin on it. The heat rose through the soles of his boots the whole way and into his shins, and his shins ached with it as they ached after a double shift, which was what he had ahead of him.

Nearer the Tower the air didn't sit right. It had a shine in it at head height. Bits of light drifted in the cross-streets, slow, none bigger than a seed, and they cast no shadow on anything they passed. A girl of about eight stood in the mouth of an alley with a net of fine wire on a pole, waiting for one to float close enough. Her hands were wrapped to the elbow in rag. She kept her eyes on the light. You didn't touch drift with skin. Everybody knew someone who had.

There were humans down here at this hour too, which was the joke of the place. Clean coats, soft shoes, standing with their foreheads to the old walls and their eyes shut, palms flat on the stone, waiting. Three of them in a row outside the bathhouse that had been dry since before Vosk was born. They paid orc children to mind their shoes. One of the children had already sold one of the shoes.

People who lived on the old ground got sick in ways the clinics up top had no words for. He passed a woman whose jaw had kept growing down one side until it had shut her eye. She was selling batteries off a blanket and she said good morning to him, though it wasn't morning yet, and he said it back. Two small boys sat on a step sharing a cake, and the nearer one held it in a hand where the fingers had grown together into one flat piece, and broke it on his knee, and gave the bigger half away.

He turned the corner into Pole Street and stopped, because everyone else had.

There were maybe forty orcs in the street, all of them on their knees with their faces turned to the wall, men and women, a few children, and not a sound out of any of them. The heat was worse here, and it blew in gusts, out of a doorway across the street where a curtain of plastic strips hung down.

He got down. He put his face to the wall like the rest and showed his hands.

Someone was walking up the line. He heard the boots stop and go on, stop and go on. Young men's boots, light, quick, and when they stopped beside him he saw from the corner of his eye a pair of shins in yard trousers, and a hand hanging loose with a cutter in it, the kind you used for strapping. The hand had wire wound round the knuckles.

"You're up late, Terrace," the young man said.

Vosk kept still. The young man smelled his collar, actually leaned down and smelled it, and the back of his neck came past the corner of Vosk's eye. Where the chip scar should have been, a pale ridge like anyone's, the skin had been opened and stitched shut again with the same wire, tight, in neat crosses, and the stitches wept. He laughed through his nose and moved on.

Behind the plastic strips, something big shifted its weight, and the floor told him about it.

They stayed on their knees until the boots had gone back inside, and a long while after. When people did start getting up, they did it one at a time and without looking at each other, and split off in different directions, and the woman next to Vosk, who was old and had burns up both hands to the wrist, stayed down longer, rocking. When she got up she put her thumb to the back of his neck, on his scar, and pressed. He did it back. Her scar was hot.

"Eating," she said. "He's eating tonight."

"I didn't see."

"No," she agreed.

She walked with him as far as the foot of the Long Stair, because it was on her way, and as they went she sang, not loud, the one about Hurrak leaving the yard. Vosk had heard it all his life and never the whole of it, from anyone. Hurrak walked out of the yard and the dogs didn't bark, and he walked past the gate and the gate didn't bite, and he walked out of the city and kept walking. In the version his mother had sung there was a part about him carrying his own name in his mouth like a stone so he wouldn't lose it, and the old woman left that part out, and he didn't ask her why.

At the bottom step she said, "Walk well," and headed off into the Scald with her burnt hands in her armpits.

---

The Long Stair was four thousand and some steps, and there was a reader at every landing. A disc of dark glass set in the wall at eye height for a human, at the chest for him. When you passed one, it looked. For a human, Vosk had been told, it was only a light, a name maybe, a soft chime. With an orc the chip in the neck woke up and answered it, and the answer went cold down both your arms into your hands, every hundred steps, to the top. You got used to that. The readers could also tell the chip to hurt you, and you didn't get used to that.

Halfway up, the stair left the old ground and passed through the sumps, where the city's water ran back down to be cleaned. After that the foundations, and then the underside of the mid-levels, where it was suddenly cooler and there were lights that worked, and grey walls with the Emperor's seal stamped on them at every landing, an oval with nothing in it.

On a landing in the foundations he stopped to get his breath and went over the price again. It came out the same as it had at the bottom. He stood there with it and thought about a window, and he'd stood too long. The reader on the wall had gone on looking. The cold came down his arms and at the wrists it turned round and started back up, and he tasted foil at the back of his jaw, faint. Two men in grey came down past him with their eyes on their slates and stepped round him. He got his feet going. On the next landing the reader looked and let him by, like all the rest.

The mid-levels were never fully asleep. The traffic lanes ran overhead in strings of light, three deep, the lowest close enough that you could hear the hulls tick as they cooled, and the feeds ran on every wall with the sound off. PLAGUE HOLD EXTENDED, THREE SYSTEMS. LEGION RECRUITMENT AT RECORD. TERRACE PROPERTY VALUES.

The ground up here was wrong too, only quieter about it. The floors had been laid level and leaned anyway, all of them in one direction, so that a drink spilled on the Spur ran off toward the Tower whichever way the street went. Plants in window boxes grew sideways. Dogs didn't last. Everybody had the headache. It sat behind the eyes and in the ears, all day, every day, and there was a stall on every landing selling a blue pill for it that never worked, and people bought the pill and complained to each other about it instead of saying good morning. Sometimes in the gutter water there was a thin smear of light that slid away from a straight stare, and the cleaners hosed it down the drains with everything else.

The flesh market at Ninth Spur was opening. Vosk cut through it, because it was quicker and the stalls were full of men too busy to see him. They were hosing down the slabs. Steam rose off the water. The cold-cases were going up on their trestles, and every case had a stamp on the end the size of a hand, black on white, CLONE STOCK — NO SOUL, with the grade under it. A woman in an apron was laying out tongues in rows on ice, and a man at the next stall was sorting a basket of eyes by colour, and a dwarf further down sold hands in pairs, matched, with a paper tag on each thumb. Vosk had worked a season here when he was small, carrying ice. He knew what an orc arm sold for, which was less than a goat's.

At the end of the row a man was shouting, "Real! Real, not clone, real!" and a young woman with a shopping bag stopped and asked him, quite politely, how she was meant to tell.

"Costs more, doesn't it," the man said.

She had a pound. He cut it from something under the counter, not from the cases, and wrapped it in a page of feed sheet, and she put it in her bag with the bread.

The lift at the Spur's head had a core, a grey lump the size of a loaf in a glass box, wet, with threads going out of it into the works, and when the lift was coming down the lump twitched, slightly, the way cut meat twitches on a block. Vosk went round it to the culvert.

From the culvert's mouth you could see the Tower, all of it, if you leaned back. You couldn't anywhere else in the mid-levels. It rose out of the middle of everything, out of the oldest ground, pale and seamless and so big that the clouds broke round it, and the lanes bent away from it in a wide circle, and every building in the city kept a mile back from its foot. The rich said it was warm on your face. They said it the way they said a good wine was warm. The Terrace was built on the first rise of land that faced it, and the houses there had their best windows turned to it, and people paid for those windows more than Vosk could count.

He leaned back and stared up at it, because it was there. A tall pale thing. He didn't feel anything.

---

The service gate for Ninth Terrace was a slot in a wall at the top of the culvert, one orc wide. Vosk was fourteen minutes past the count.

He knew it before he got to the slot, because the slot knew, and the chip knew, and it started before he could get his shoulder through. It always started in the jaw. It felt like biting on foil, and the foil spread into the bones of his face and down his neck, and from there it went into the long muscles of his back and pulled them all at once in the same direction. He hit the wall of the slot with his head. It never lasted long. When it let go he was on his knees in the slot with his mouth full of spit and his hands open and held out, as they taught you to hold them, and somewhere far off the gate said, in a pleasant voice, "Late. Logged."

He got up. He went through.

The Terrace was the other side of the slot, all at once, every time. Water ran down channels cut in the white stone. There were trees, real ones, with real dirt round their feet that orcs changed every month. The air was cool and smelled of cut grass. In the first-floor window of the third house, the Ilders', a boy of about nine was eating breakfast in a white shirt, and down on the wet lawn the family's yard orc, a grown man, was hopping. Every time a foot touched the grass it jerked up again as if the grass were a stove, and the other one came down and jerked, and he went on like that, faster, with his arms clamped to his sides and his face screwed shut. The boy had his spoon in one hand and his thumb on a fob in the other. He wasn't laughing. He was interested.

Vosk kept walking. He had his thumb on his own scar.

In the barracks under the long house, the others were already up and dressing. Somebody had kept him a bit of bread. He ate it sitting on his bunk.

He went out into the yard and stood under the third window from the end on the second floor, which had been the same window since he was twelve, found a bit of gravel at the edge of the path, and threw it.

It took three. The third rang off the glass, and the window came up, and Eli put his head and his bare shoulders out into the cold, with a crease down one cheek from the pillow and the hair flat on that side.

"It's the middle of the night."

"It's after the count."

"That's what I said." Eli folded his arms on the sill and put his chin on them. "Go on."

Vosk had had it ready since the foundations. He'd said it over on every landing after. Now it came out short.

"I want a loan."

"A what?"

"Eleven hundred. Marks."

Eli lifted his chin off his arms. He studied Vosk, the work canvas, the grit to the knees, and then he laughed.

"What for?"

Vosk put two fingers on the back of his own neck, where the dwarf had put hers.

Eli watched the fingers. "Does it hurt?"

"No."

"Then what's wrong with it?"

Vosk took the fingers down.

"There's a dwarf," he said.

"There's always a dwarf. Where?"

"Down."

"Down where?"

"The Scald."

Eli's face changed, up there in the window. "Huh," he said.

"Forty-one now," said Vosk. "Then a mark a month."

Eli's lips moved. He'd been bad at sums with the tutors and he was bad at them still, and he did this one on his fingers along the sill. "That's ninety years."

"Eighty-eight."

Eli put his face down on his arms and laughed into them until the sill shook. When he came up his eyes were wet. "You'll be dead."

Vosk waited.

"Don't be stupid. Keep your forty-one."

"A loan," Vosk said.

"Fine. A loan. When?"

"Tonight. After."

"After what?"

"Double."

"Why are you on a double?" Eli wasn't looking at him. He was looking over the barracks roof, at the culvert wall and whatever he could see past it from up there. "The Scald," he said. "Tonight."

"Yes."

"Leave it with me." The window came down, and came up again an inch. "Leave it with me," Eli said through the inch, and shut it.

Vosk stood in the yard. The cut on his forehead had opened again in the cold, and a line of it had run down into his eyebrow. He wiped it off with his wrist.

---

The steward was out on the barracks step with his slate, doing the yard by numbers. He didn't lift his head for it. He read a number and a job, and the job went into your neck as he said it, a warmth that turned you the right way, and you went. Vosk's number came near the end.

"Double," the steward said to the slate.

The warmth came, and stayed on past the word.

The trees were his. He'd had them nine years. Every house on Ninth had four, two at the front and two at the back, and the dirt round their feet went sour in a month and had to come out. The new came up the culvert in sacks on the backs of the Spur crews and was left stacked at the top of the slot, and Vosk dug the old out and put the new in, house by house, down one side of the Terrace and back up the other. The old came out grey, in clumps that broke like ash. The new came out of the sacks dark and wet and smelling of something he had no word for, and when he'd firmed it down round the trunk he kept his hands in it after the job was done, both of them, to the wrist.

That morning his neck turned him to the Ilders'.

He went in by the side and kept to the paving as far as the first tree, and after that there was nothing for it but the lawn. The grass was still wet. At the far end, on the path, the Ilders' man was raking, and when Vosk's boots went onto the grass he stopped raking and stood with the rake upright in both hands.

Vosk dug.

The boy came out onto the front step in his white shirt and a school coat, with a slate under his arm, to wait for whatever was coming down out of the lanes for him. He saw Vosk. He studied the boots on his lawn. He took the fob out of his coat pocket.

Vosk kept digging.

The boy pointed the fob at him the way you'd point at a dog you wanted to come, and pressed it. Nothing happened in Vosk's feet. At the far end of the lawn the Ilders' man went up off the path with both heels, came down, and went up again, the rake going one way and his arms the other.

The boy frowned at the fob. He pointed it at Vosk again, harder, with his arm out straight, and at the end of the lawn the Ilders' man went up and came down on his knees on the path and stayed down.

A flyer came out of the lanes and settled at the kerb. The boy put the fob away and got in.

The Ilders' man got up. He came along the paving with the rake and stopped by Vosk's tree, his boots an inch off the grass, and spoke in the orc talk without looking at him.

"You've been down."

"Pole Street."

"Eating?"

Vosk nodded.

"That's three nights." The man looked at his own boots, and the inch. "Their young one's getting the hang of it," he said. "Kid's fob. Does their own. His dad's does anybody's." He went back up the paving with the rake.

Vosk did the tree by the front step last, and quickly, and kept his eyes on the dirt.

---

The noon bell went for bread. In the barracks yard the two little ones from the end bunks were playing Hurrak. The smaller one was Hurrak, and the bigger one was everything else, the dogs first, barking, and then the gate. When Hurrak walked past, the gate bit him on the forearm and hung on, and Hurrak screamed and beat the gate about the head with both fists.

Vosk pulled the gate off by the collar.

"Gates don't bite that hard."

"This one does," said the gate.

Hurrak had stopped crying to look at the marks on his arm. He counted them. "Then what?"

"Then he walks out of the city."

"Then what?"

"Keeps walking."

"Where to?"

Vosk tore his bread and gave them half each.

"My mam says they caught him," said the gate, with its mouth full.

"Your mam wasn't there," said Vosk.

Inside, on his bunk, he got the sock out and tipped it onto the blanket. He counted it in fives. Forty-one. He put it back in the sock a mark at a time, knotted the sock, and sat with it in his lap.

Leave it with me.

He put it back under the bunk, and pushed it to the back against the wall with two fingers, and went out to the trees.

By the late afternoon he was on the far side, at the houses by the gate, with the barrow half full of grey. The gate said good afternoon to somebody. Eli came through it laughing, with a young man in a coat the colour of plums, and the young man saw Vosk on his knees at the tree and stopped and said something, and Eli laughed again and called across the channel.

"Tonight!"

He held up nine fingers and waggled them. Vosk's double ran to ten. The young man in the plum coat studied Vosk the whole time with his head on one side, the way people on the Spur studied a stall.

Vosk lifted a hand off the dirt.

Eli went off up the white path toward the culvert end, walking backwards for a few steps to wave. The young man went the other way. Vosk put his hand back in the dirt and found the root he'd been working round, and worked round it.

---

The steward woke Eli at a quarter past eleven that morning by standing at the foot of the bed until he woke.

"There's a woman at the door, sir."

Eli turned over. "So let her in."

"She won't come in."

"Then let her out."

The steward didn't smile. He held his slate flat against his chest with both hands. "She has something for you. She won't give it to me. She has been on the step since eight. She said not to wake you."

Eli came up on one elbow. "Since eight?"

"I offered her a chair."

He went down in his shirt and bare feet. She was standing on the step outside the open door, not leaning on anything, a woman of about fifty in a grey coat buttoned to the throat, with her hair cut short as a man's. The shoulders of the coat were wet. It hadn't rained on the Terrace. She had an envelope in one gloved hand, and when she saw him on the stairs she held it out, and went on holding it out while he crossed the hall.

"Who's it from?"

She waited until his fingers had closed on it. Then she let go, and turned, and went down the white path toward the gate.

"Thank you," Eli said to her back.

The envelope was heavy and pale and the front was blank. On the back, pressed into a disc of grey wax, was the seal off the stair landings. He had seen that oval ten thousand times, on landings and crates and the backs of buses.

"Will there be an answer, sir?" said the steward.

"She didn't say there was a question."

He carried it into the kitchen. The cook was at the hot-plate. He put the letter on the table, and stood the fruit bowl on it, and the cook watched him do it and turned back to her pan.

"Eggs," Eli said.

"It's gone eleven."

"Eggs," he said, and smiled at her, and she did eggs.

The steward came in after him and stood by the table while he ate. Eli talked with his mouth full.

"I need some money."

"Sir."

"Eleven hundred."

The steward's thumb was already moving on the slate. "Imperial."

"Marks."

The thumb stopped. Then it went on, and the steward turned the slate face in against his shirt to wait, and before he could lower it the slate chimed against his chest. He turned it out and read it.

"Your father's people," he said. He said it whenever the account paid, in the same voice, and Eli had stopped asking which people years ago.

"Tell them thank you."

"Sir."

In the hall the house bell rang. The steward went out to it, and left the door a hand's width open, and Eli, buttering, heard him.

"Ninth, the house." A pause. "Yes." A pause. "Eleven hundred." A longer one. "Marks, madam." Longer. "Eleven. One, one, and two noughts." A silence Eli got most of a slice buttered in. "Yes, madam."

Eli laughed into his toast. "One, one and two noughts," he told the cook. The cook didn't laugh.

The steward came back as far as the door. He stood there with the slate held flat in front of him, as though somebody had set a thing on it to be carried, and then he went up the stairs without a word.

---

Dace was waiting at the gate with his hands in his pockets, rocking heel to toe, in a coat the colour of plums. The gate looked at him and said, "Good afternoon, Dace," and let him out onto the ramp. It looked at Eli and said, "Good afternoon," and let him out after.

"Still?" said Dace.

"It's shy."

Dace asked every time.

The Vennicks were up on the Seventh, where the houses stood higher and further apart and the Tower filled the whole end of every street. Their roof was a garden. Long chairs were set out on it in a row, all turned the one way, and people lay in them with their faces up and their eyes shut, getting the warm.

Lisane got up out of hers when she saw him.

"You came."

"I always come."

"You do." She kissed his cheek and kept hold of his arm. "We've had an offer on the house."

"Don't take it. Where would we lie?" He was looking past her down the row. "Is that Ilder? Ilder!"

Ilder lifted a hand without opening his eyes.

Lisane let go of his arm.

Eli took the chair beside Ilder's and turned his face up like the rest. There it was, past the edge of the roof, pale, so close from up here that he could see nothing else. He shut his eyes. Dace said it was warm today, and somebody said it was always warm, and somebody else said not like today. Eli kept his face up and said it was very warm.

One of the Vennicks' men came along the row with a tray. He was in white to the gloves, and he bent low at each chair and held the tray down, so you didn't have to sit up. Eli sat up anyway. He took two of the little pastries and ate one and held the other out.

"Here."

The man looked at the pastry.

"Go on. They're good."

The man took it in his gloved fingers and held it.

"Eat it, then."

"They're not to eat on the roof," said Lisane, from her chair.

"I won't tell." Eli patted the man's arm, twice, above the glove. "Good man."

The man went on down the row with the tray in one hand and the pastry in the other.

"You'll spoil him," said Dace.

"They like it."

"How's your big one, anyway?"

"He was at my window this morning," Eli said. "Middle of the night. Throwing stones." He swung his legs round to face them. "Wants eleven hundred marks."

That got them. Dace took his feet down.

"For what?"

"A chip. There's a dwarf in the Scald who does them."

"Does what with them?" said Lisane.

"Better ones." Eli spread his hands. "A better chip."

"Better at what?"

"He didn't say." Eli grinned round at all of them. "He's going to pay me back. A mark a month."

Dace was quicker at sums than Eli. "That's—"

"Eighty-eight years," said Eli, flat and low, in Vosk's voice, and they laughed, all of them, Dace with his head right back. Eli laughed with them, and said it again, Eighty-eight, and they laughed harder.

Ilder laughed less. He had his glass standing on his chest, and he was rubbing his thumb over the heel of his other hand, where there was an old white mark, a ragged crescent. He'd had it as long as Eli had known him.

"Vosk," Ilder said. "I remember Vosk. He could dance."

"Vosk?" said Eli. "He's got feet like barrows."

Ilder smiled into his glass.

"Down the Scald," said Dace, "with eleven hundred on him. They'll have him on a slab by morning. Graded."

"They'll rob him first," said the girl at the end.

"They'll all rob you in the end," said Dace. "Give them a reason. A better chip—"

"Not Vosk."

"Why not Vosk?"

"Because it's Vosk." Eli took a glass off the next tray that came by. "And they won't have him on any slab. I'll be with him."

There was a pause down the row. Somebody opened their eyes.

"You," said Ilder.

"Me."

"You've not been below Ancel Row in your life."

"So I'll go below Ancel Row."

"You won't."

"Tonight," said Eli.

Ilder lifted his glass off his chest, and the others lifted theirs, the girl at the end, Dace, a boy Eli didn't know, all down the row. Lisane didn't have a glass. Eli lay back in the warm with his eyes shut and his own glass on his stomach.

"Show them, then," said Dace. "If you're going down there. Do the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing. Lift something."

They were all watching now, even the ones who hadn't been listening. Eli looked round for something to lift. There was an empty chair at the end of the row. He put his hand out at it.

It was easy. It was always easy. He thought about the chair, its four feet on the tiles, and waited for the give.

The chair sat there.

Somebody giggled. Dace said, "Ooh."

"I've had wine," said Eli, and lay back down, and they laughed at that too. The Vennicks' man was coming back up the row with the tray, and Eli flicked two fingers at it as it passed, without looking, the way he'd flick a crumb. The tray jumped in the man's hand. Glasses danced. He caught it to his chest with both arms, elbows clamped in, eyes screwed shut, and stood there in his white gloves holding on to it while it rattled, and the whole row laughed, louder than for anything yet.

"Sorry!" said Eli, laughing. "Sorry. Good man."

The talk went on to something else.

When he left, Lisane walked him down. On the stair she stopped him with her hand flat on his chest.

"Eli. It's the last one."

"The last what?"

"Of these." She pointed up, at the roof. "We go down at the end of the month."

"Down?"

"Down," she said.

"Then come tonight!" He took the hand off his chest and held it. "Come with us. See what it's like. I'll look after you."

She looked at him a while. Then she took her hand back, and said have a good time, and went up.

The Vennicks' man was at the foot of the stair with the tray under his arm. He still had the pastry, flat now, in a smear of cream on the white glove. He opened the door for Eli and stood back from it.

---

Teo's house was the narrow one at the culvert end of Ninth, three floors and a bare roof, and every window in it looked at the culvert wall, which was why it was cheap. Teo's mother kept it on what the big houses paid her to teach their children to read. She'd taught Eli to read in it, at the kitchen table, with Teo kicking him underneath.

She opened the door with chalk on her hands.

"He's upstairs," she said. "He's had a day."

"Haven't we all," said Eli, and took the stairs two at a time.

The trunk was in the middle of Teo's floor with its lid back. It had been packed in the spring. Teo was sitting beside it in his socks, taking things out.

He took out a book, and got up, and put the book on the shelf, in the clean place in the dust where it had stood till the spring, and sat down again and took out the next thing. A cup with a chip off the handle. There was a clean ring on the windowsill, and he stood the cup in the ring.

The slate lay on the bed. Eli picked it up. PLAGUE HOLD EXTENDED, THREE SYSTEMS, and a list of the three under it, and one line, further down, that Teo had marked.

"Oh, Teo."

"Mm."

"That's rotten. That's really rotten." Eli sat down on the bed, on a pile of folded shirts. "They'll lift it."

"They said that in the spring."

"They'll lift it by winter. And you'll go, and it'll be awful here without you, and you'll write and tell me about the food."

Teo took a small grey stone out of the trunk, turned it over, and put it on the sill beside the cup, in a clean place the size of a stone.

"They'd given me a room," he said. "There was a picture of it. The window was on the water, and you could—"

"Listen. Tonight."

Teo turned the stone round so it sat the way it had sat before.

"Come to mine at nine," Eli said. "We're going down."

Teo looked up.

"The Scald. You, me and Vosk."

"Why?"

"I'll tell you at mine."

"Why not now?"

"It's a surprise."

"For who?"

Eli laughed and got up off the shirts, and some of them slid to the floor. "Nine," he said. "Wear boots."

On the stairs he heard Teo get up behind him, and pick the shirts up, and fold them again.

---

When he got home the cook had moved the fruit bowl to wipe the table, and the letter lay out on the wet wood by itself, the grey wax up. She saw him see it.

"It'll mark," she said.

He put the bowl back on it.

The steward was in the hall when he came out, with a purse on the hall table in front of him and his hand flat beside it, not on it.

"A man brought it at four, sir. He waited."

"They all wait today." Eli picked it up. The clasp bit him, and he laughed and sucked the finger. "Vosk's lates. Take them off."

"Sir."

"Both of them. He was with me."

The steward had the slate up. He didn't write anything on it. "They're logged, sir. Lates go up."

"Up where?"

The steward lifted his eyes from the slate to Eli's face, and then a little past it, to the stair, and the house above the stair, and whatever was above the house.

"Up," he said.

"Then bring them down."

The steward wrote. He held the slate a moment, and it chimed, the same small chime as the morning, and he turned it face in against his shirt.

"Off, sir."

"There," said Eli. "Was that hard?" He went up two stairs and turned round on the third. "Where's my good coat? The long one."

"Pressed, sir. In your room." The steward looked at the purse in Eli's hand, and at Eli. "For the Spur, sir?"

"Further."

The steward said nothing to that. When Eli came down again in the coat with the collar up and the purse in his hand, the steward was still in the hall, and he looked at the coat from its hem to its collar, the whole length of it, as he looked over a bill before it went up.

"Very good, sir," he said.

---

The purse was on the kitchen table next to the fruit bowl, where he could see it while he talked.

"Eleven hundred," he said. "In marks."

Teo was sitting on the counter by the cold-box with his heels against the doors and his slate on his knee, eating an apple from the bottom up, which was how he ate them. With his other thumbnail he was working at the skin down the side of his thumb. He did that when he read, and the thumb was always half healed.

"From who?"

"The account." Eli turned the purse over. It was heavier than he'd expected money to be. Money was a number on a slate. "Asked at breakfast. It was back before the steward had his slate down. Somebody rang the house to check. A woman." Eli laughed. "No one ever rings him. He's been in a mood all day."

"What did she want?"

"To check. I just said."

Teo gave the purse a long look and went back to the slate.

The account had come with the house, and the house had come with Eli, before he could remember. It had paid for both his flyers and for the window he'd put a chair through at fifteen, and it never asked what anything was for.

"He asked you for money," Teo said.

"A loan."

"A loan." Teo turned the apple in his fingers. "He didn't ask you to come."

"He didn't have to." Eli got up, crossed to the window and came back. "On his own? At night, down there, with that on him?"

Teo didn't say anything.

"They'd have the boots off him before the second landing. He's never had eleven hundred of anything. He'd carry it in his hand." Eli sat down again. "He would. In his hand."

Teo finished the apple and glanced round for somewhere to put the core. Eli flicked two fingers at the yard door without looking round. The core left Teo's hand and floated across the kitchen, not fast, to the open yard door, and dropped on the step inside it, where somebody would sweep it.

Teo considered his empty hand. He put the thumb in his mouth. He had none of it himself. The tutors had sat him in front of a feather on a plate when he was nine, and given up by lunch, and he'd gone home pleased.

On the wall behind him the feed was running with the sound off. Grey men at a grey table. The ribbon underneath said ELVEN DELEGATION RECALLED, then THE QUEEN DECLINES TO COMMENT, and a line about rates.

The letter was under the fruit bowl.

"Are you going to open that," said Teo.

"Tomorrow." Eli slid the purse into the inside pocket of his coat, and patted it. "Whoever they are, they've had twenty-four years. They can—"

There was a knock on the yard door, two short, which was Vosk's knock, and Eli was across the kitchen before the second one had finished.

Vosk filled the door. He ducked coming through it, though the top of his head cleared the frame by an inch. He still wore his work clothes, the grey canvas, with dirt dried to the knees, and he had a cut on his forehead that Eli hadn't noticed that morning, scabbed, with grit in it.

"What happened to your head?"

"Wall."

"Sit down. You haven't eaten. There's the end of the bird. Teo, move."

"I'm all right," Vosk said.

"Sit down."

Vosk sat on the step inside the yard door, which was where he sat. The apple core was by his boot, and he moved the boot. Teo slid down off the counter. Eli got the end of the bird out of the cold-box and put the plate down on the step beside Vosk, and the fork on the plate, and a cloth after, because he was always forgetting the cloth. He crouched down facing him with his forearms on his knees.

"Your lates are off," he said. "Both of them. I told the steward you were with me."

Vosk put two fingers up to the cut on his forehead and took them down again.

"And I've got it." Eli patted the coat. "I'll carry it."

Vosk's eyes went to him, and past him, to Teo.

"It's quicker on my own," Vosk said.

"So we'll be quick."

"I know the way. I go down there."

"I know you go down there. That's how you got your head opened." Eli reached out and put his hand on the back of Vosk's neck, over the scar, and gave it a squeeze and a little shake. The scar was warm under his palm, a raised ridge. "I'm not handing eleven hundred marks to a dwarf I've never met. Would you? And nobody's putting a knife in anybody with me stood there." He let go and stood up. "They know what a Terrace coat is."

Vosk had picked up the fork. He turned it over. He put it down on the plate again, very square, next to the bird.

"Eli," he said. "Not in the coat."

"Eat your bird."

Vosk ate the bird. He ate it slowly, and near the end of it he stopped chewing, and his head went back against the door frame, and he was asleep, with the fork still in his hand and his mouth open.

Eli took the fork out of his hand. He fetched his second-best coat from the hall and put it over him, and it covered about half of him.

Teo checked the clock on his slate and glanced at Eli.

"So she waits," Eli said. He sat down on the kitchen floor with his back to the cold-box, where he could see Vosk's face. "And I'm not walking four thousand steps. Find us a way down that isn't the stair."

Teo turned the slate round. He'd had it up since the purse came out: the sump crews' rota, two weeks of it, and a crews' shaft off the Spur with a cage in it, a change at half past eleven, and nobody due in the shaft after that until two.

Eli didn't look at it. "Good," he said.

---

They left the house just after eleven.

## TWO · The Scald

Going down the white path they passed the Ilders'. The Ilders' man was out in the dark, sweeping round the front step, and he was standing on the paving to do it, right at the edge, with his boots an inch off the grass. Eli nodded to him. The man didn't nod back, which was the Ilders all over; they didn't train them. The youngest had been given a fob in the spring, and most mornings you could hear him at it from the kitchen, slap, slap on the wet lawn.

At the arch the glass looked at Eli and said, "Good evening," and let him out onto the public ramp. It said the same to Teo.

Vosk wasn't with them. The front gate didn't read orcs. It bit them.

"That's stupid," Eli had said in the yard. "Come through with us. I'll tell it." Vosk had said, "It bites," and gone off down the side of the house toward the culvert. He'd folded the second-best coat in four and left it on the step.

They found him at the bottom of the public ramp a few minutes later, standing still in the shadow of a pillar, with spit drying on his chin and his hands where people could see them.

"See," said Eli. "No trouble." He clapped him on the arm.

Vosk wiped his chin on his wrist. The slot didn't like orcs out after the count.

The mid-levels at night were louder than by day. Noise poured down from the lanes overhead and up from the streets underneath, and there were so many feeds you could have read by them. Every human face that passed under a reader got its light. Eli had stopped seeing that at about sixteen. He walked down the middle of the walkway with his coat open and the purse knocking on his ribs, and people stepped round him, and he let them.

On the corner of Ancel Row the man on the door of the club with the red lamps saw him coming and unhooked the rope.

"Mr Eli."

"Not tonight." Eli didn't slow down. "We're going down."

The doorman looked at Teo, and at Vosk behind Teo, and at the coat. He hooked the rope back on its post.

"Down, sir."

"Down," said Eli, and laughed, and walked on. Behind him he heard the doorman say it again to somebody inside, and the somebody laugh.

Past Ancel Row the Spur got narrower and darker, and the walkway leaned. The flesh market was shut, the cold-cases chained under tarps, and the gutters still ran pink from the evening hosing. At the Spur's head the lift's core sat in its glass box and twitched when a car went down. Vosk went round it. Eli bent to the glass to look at it, and Teo said, "Eli," and he came away.

Teo took them past the lift to a pump house with its door propped open on a brick. Inside was a square hole in the floor with a rail round it, and a cable going down into the hole, black with oil, humming.

Vosk stood just inside the door with his back to the wall, where he could see the street. Eli walked once round the rail and came back to him.

"You've got your face on," he said. "Like you're waiting for the count."

Vosk didn't answer. Out in the street a flyer went over low, and the light off it came in through the door and went across the floor and up the wall and was gone.

At half past the cable stopped humming. Something came up the hole, slowly, ticking, and the top of a cage rose out of it, and then the cage, with five orcs in it in waders wet to the chest and a human with a slate on a cord round his neck. The human saw Eli. He saw the coat. He stood in the cage with the gate half open and looked at Teo, and at Teo's slate, and at the coat again, and then at nothing in particular.

"Gentlemen," he said, and came out round them, and went out of the door.

The orcs came after him one at a time. They smelled of the sumps. The last of them was old, with grey in the stubble on his head, and he stopped in front of Vosk with the water running out of the tops of his waders onto the floor and said something low in the orc talk. Vosk said something back, one word. The old one looked at Eli a while, from the boots up. Then he went out.

"What did he say?"

"Shift's over," said Vosk.

Teo got into the cage first, without a word.

It was a long way. The cage went down grinding, and every yard of it was warmer than the last, and after a while Eli stopped trying to guess how far. Partway down, the shaft opened on one side onto the sumps: black water sliding past in a channel as wide as a street, with lamps on the far wall laying long smears on it, and a smell of shut cellars.

There the cage stopped.

It swung a little and was still. The cable ticked overhead. The water went past.

"Teo."

"It does that." Teo had the slate up. "It says here it does that."

"For how long?"

Teo turned the slate toward the lamps and read on.

Eli laughed. It went out over the water and nothing came back. He turned round and put his back to the mesh. Vosk was at the far side of the cage with one hand on the rail, and the light off the water moved on his face.

"Give it here," Vosk said.

"Give what?"

"The purse. Before we're down."

"I've got it."

"I'll carry it."

"You'll carry it in your hand." Eli patted the coat over it. "Anyway it's in here. Nobody's going near this."

Vosk kept the hand out. It had the tree dirt still in the creases of the knuckles. After a while he put it back on the rail.

"Forty-one's under my bunk," he said. "At the back. Against the wall."

"I don't want your forty-one."

"It's the first of it."

"Eighty-eight years," said Eli, flat and low, in Vosk's own voice, and grinned at him.

Teo looked up from the slate.

Vosk looked at Eli. Then he turned his head and watched the water go by, and the cage jerked and dropped a foot and went on down.

It stopped at last at a grille where the cable ended. The last of it was a ladder. The rungs were slick and warm. Eli's forearms had started to burn. Below him Teo's breathing came up the shaft, slow and even. Above him Vosk made no sound at all.

---

At the bottom of the shaft the floor was the wrong size.

It was a street. It had a gutter, and a curb, and on the other side another curb. But the curb on this side came up to Eli's chest, and the far one was a long way off, so far that the lamps strung between them sagged in the middle, and the street between had been filled in with poured stuff to make a floor at a sensible height, and people had built shacks on it, and a ramp went up over the curb made of pallets and somebody's door.

Eli stood with his hands on the curb. The stone was smooth where people had held it getting up the ramp. Teo was already at the top.

A boy was sitting on the curb beside the ramp with his knees up, eating something out of a paper. He watched Vosk come out of the shaft, and Teo, and Eli. He looked at Eli's coat the longest. Then he folded the paper over what was left and put it inside his shirt, and got up and went off along the top of the curb, not hurrying, and dropped down the far side into the street.

"Hello," Eli said, after him.

The Scald was hot. Eli had known it would be. He hadn't known the heat would have a direction. It came from ahead and above, out of the dark past the shack roofs, and it pushed on his face and his chest, and the further they went the harder it pushed. He took his coat off and carried it, and put it back on because of the purse. Sweat ran into the small of his back. The air tasted of metal and burning fat.

There were a lot of people, orcs mostly. They sat in the shack doors and cooked on drums in the street and lay on mats along the foot of the curb, and they moved round Eli without touching him, a gap opening half a step before he got there and closing up behind. A woman in a doorway saw him and let her curtain down. Somewhere ahead of them, toward the heat, somebody whistled two notes, and further off somebody else whistled them back.

Eli whistled them back too. Nobody laughed.

"Polite, aren't they," he said to Teo. Teo didn't answer.

Vosk walked ahead. He walked differently down here. Looser. His hands hung at his sides. A big orc with a split ear stepped into his way and the two of them put their thumbs to each other's necks and spoke low in the orc talk, and the other one laughed and eyed Eli's coat past Vosk's shoulder, and Eli, and added a few words, and Vosk said, "No," sharply.

"What did he say?"

"Nothing."

"He said something."

"He asked was you mine."

Eli laughed. "What did you say?"

Vosk walked faster.

The street ran out at a cut in the ground. It ran away on both sides, forty feet down, maybe more, and the bottom of it wasn't dark but lit from somewhere under it, a dull white that moved, and drift rose out of it in ones and twos. Along the edge children stood with nets on long poles, wrapped to the elbow in rag.

One of them, a girl, was working a light that wouldn't come. It hung a yard past the rim of her net, turning, and every time she reached the pole out after it the air off the cut pushed it back, and she leaned out further, with her rag feet on the lip of the drop.

Eli flicked two fingers at it on his way past.

The light slid sideways into her net and lay there.

She didn't pull the net in. She looked at it, and back along the pole to her own wrapped hands, and then at Eli's fingers, and at his coat. She came off the edge a step at a time with the pole held out level between them. Then she turned and ran along the rim toward the bridge, with the net up over her head and the light in it.

"Don't do that," said Vosk. "Not down here."

"She'd have been there all night."

Vosk watched her go. She was over the bridge already, a small dark shape and a light bobbing on a pole, and down into whatever was on the far side of it.

The bridge was one long curved girder with a deck laid on top, ribbed like a spine, dark grey going brown, and it spanned the cut in one piece. Eli put his hand on it as he stepped up, stopped, and put his hand back.

It was warm. Warm like a horse.

"Don't touch it," said Vosk.

---

Hookline was the market the bridge ran down into, and it was called that for the hooks: the whole low ceiling hung with them on chains, thousands, and goods on half of them. Coats. Lamps. Pots. A whole side of something red and white, sawn down the middle, turning slowly in the heat rising off the cut, with a paper tag on the shank, blank, not even a grade. Across the floor from the bridge end stood an old stone arch with plastic strips hung across it, and the heat poured out of there harder than anywhere. Music played from three places at once. A dwarf woman sold soup out of a hot-box, with a ladle as long as her arm, and the soup smelled good, and Eli slowed for it.

Teo stepped in close beside him.

"Take the coat off," he said. "Carry it inside out."

"It's got the purse in it."

"I know what's in it." Teo kept his head still. "Every stall, Eli. Nobody's looking at him. They're looking at the coat."

Eli turned to see. A man at a stall of pans studied his pans. Two orc women by a hook of lamps looked at each other. Up on a crate by the arch, the net girl sat with her pole across her knees.

"It's a good coat," Eli said, and put his arm round Teo's shoulders and gave him a shake, and let him go, and went to the soup.

"Three," he said.

The dwarf woman looked at him. She looked past him at Vosk. She ladled out three cups, slowly, without spilling, and set them on the edge of the hot-box in a row. Eli went through his trouser pockets and found a note, imperial, folded small, and put it down beside the cups.

"Keep it."

She picked the note up and held it. She didn't put it away. Her eyes went past him across the floor, and Eli looked where she was looking, and the girl had got up off the crate by the arch and was slipping in through the plastic strips, not hurrying.

He gave Teo a cup. Teo held it. He held one out to Vosk, and Vosk looked at it and didn't take it, and Eli drank his own standing there, and it burnt his tongue, and it was good.

"Vosk," he said, loud, over the music. "Which way?"

Vosk didn't look round. He turned off into an alley halfway along the market's side wall, between two stalls. Eli put the third cup down on the corner of a stall of lamps, and they followed him.

---

The dwarf was called Velk, or that was what it said on the shutter of the shop she worked out of the back of, which had sold water once and still had the tanks. She was short even for a dwarf and nearly as broad as she was tall, and she had a scar that ran from her hairline down through one eyebrow and on down her cheek, and the eye under it was a pale grey and stayed still when the other one moved.

She looked at Eli. At Teo. At Vosk.

"You said you'd come on your own," she said.

"He's paying," said Vosk.

"He's paying." She ran her eye over Eli again, his coat, his hands, his boots. "Course he is. You're late." She jerked her head at the shutter. "It's hot. I told you it was hot."

"It's boiling." Eli flapped his coat.

"Come in. Don't touch anything."

The back of the shop was a long room with a low ceiling and shelves on every wall, and on the shelves were things Eli had no names for. A grey cube that sat at a tilt and, while he watched, tilted the other way. A coin standing on its edge on a saucer.

Eli bent to the coin. He laid it down flat on the saucer without touching it, a little push with nothing at all, and stood it back up on its edge, and looked round at Teo to see if he'd seen.

"Don't," said Velk. She was at a basin in the corner with her back to him, washing her hands, fast, with a brush, up to the elbows, and her live eye was on him in the dark side of the tank.

"I didn't touch it."

"Don't."

"Where's all this from?"

"Out." She shook the water off. "Depot takes a third now. A third. Then his cut. And whatever he sends up to whoever." She dried her arms.

"Whose cut?" said Teo.

Velk pulled a heavy chair into the light, high-backed, with straps on the arms, and nodded at it, and Vosk sat down in it and put his own arms in the straps.

Eli took out the purse.

She counted it on the table in stacks of fifty, wetting her thumb, so fast that Eli lost the count at the second stack, and swept most of it into a tin with a mark scratched in the lid and the rest into a drawer, and held her hand out again.

"And two," she said.

"It was eleven."

"It was eleven last night." She kept the hand out. "Tonight you're late, and it's hot, and there's three of you."

"I haven't got two."

Her live eye went to his wrist.

Eli unbuckled the watch and put it in her hand. A birthday present. It kept the time on four worlds he'd never been to and it had a little moon on it that went round. She put it in her apron without a glance.

In the far corner, behind the tanks, a thin dwarf boy of maybe ten sat on a stool with a cup. Eli hadn't seen him. The boy's eyes went to Eli's bare wrist. To the pocket of Eli's coat where the purse had been, and Eli's boots, and Eli. He put down the cup and slipped out through a curtain at the back.

"Where's he going?" said Teo.

Velk answered without turning. "Tax." She pulled the lamp down on its chain. "Hold his head."

Eli stood there.

"His head. Forward. Chin down. Unless you'd rather do the knife."

Eli held his head.

Up close, Vosk's hair smelled of the barracks soap, and the back of his neck was hot, and the scar was there under Eli's fingers where it had always been, a pale ridge as long as a thumb. He'd touched it a thousand times. Velk wiped it with a cold pad and laid her tools out on a cloth, small bright things, picked one, put the point of it in the old scar at the top end and drew it down.

Vosk's whole body went tight in the chair.

There was more blood than Eli had expected, and it was darker. It ran down into the collar of Vosk's work shirt and Velk let it run. She opened the cut with two small hooks and held it, and Eli could see down into it, past the red, to a grey, shining thing with threads running out from it into the meat like roots. She took hold of it with narrow pliers and began to pull, slowly, turning, and the threads came out one at a time with a sound like wet paper, and Vosk said three words through his teeth in the orc talk, the same three over and over, and his hands in the straps opened and shut.

"Talk to him," said Velk. "If you're going to stand there."

"It's all right," said Eli. "It's all right. You're doing well. You're doing so well."

The thing came free, the size of a fingernail, with its threads trailing, and she dropped it in a dish, and in the dish it moved. Once, a small curl of the threads.

Teo had gone to stand by the door, watching the shutter. Out in the market, one of the three musics had stopped.

The new one came out of a sealed packet. Bigger than the old one, darker, ridged on one side, and someone had taken a file to the mark on it, not well enough. Eli could make out part of a word. LEGION.

"Soldier's," said Velk. "Leash is out."

She pressed it home with her thumb, and took the thumb away fast and shook it, and it didn't take. The threads on it moved, and stopped, and lay down flat in the wet. She said a short word in dwarven and opened the cut wider with the hooks, and Vosk's back came up off the chair, and Eli held his head down with both hands and all his weight on them. She pushed it in again and held it, with her lips pulled back off her teeth. This time the threads went, feeling their way down into him, and under Eli's fingers the skin round the cut went hot, and hotter, so that he nearly let go.

She glanced at Eli with her live eye. "You know what you've—"

"Yes," said Eli.

She watched him, and closed the cut with glue and a strip that pulled tight as it dried, and undid the straps, all of it fast. There was a blister coming up white on the ball of her thumb. Vosk stayed in the chair. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his head hanging, breathing hard, and blood dripped off the end of his nose onto the floor between his boots.

He lifted his head.

His eyes passed over all of them. He turned his face, slowly, to the far wall of the shop, and stared at it.

"What's that," he said.

His voice was strange. Eli crouched down to him. "What's what?"

"That." Vosk had his eyes wide open. "It's. Up. It's right there."

"Tower," said Velk. She was rolling her tools up in their cloth. "Everybody, first time. Sun in the next room."

Eli turned to the wall. Shelves.

Vosk put his hand up toward the wall with the palm out and the fingers spread, and held it there. His head came round, slowly, and down, toward the floor and the front of the shop and the market beyond it.

"And them," he said. "All of them." He shut his eyes. "It's loud."

"It's lit," said Velk. She tied the roll. "It'll settle." She put the roll under her arm. "Or it won't."

"Is it hot?" Eli said. "Vosk. Does it hurt?"

Vosk opened his eyes and found him. He studied Eli's face. He looked down at his hand, and he closed the hand.

"No," he said.

Eli let his breath go. He put his hand on Vosk's knee.

Teo crossed the room. He took hold of Eli's arm above the elbow, not hard. His face was very calm.

"Get him up," he said. "Now."

Vosk's hands went to the arms of the chair.

"He's just had his neck opened." Eli shook Teo's hand off, not unkindly, and kept his own where it was. "Give him a minute. Sit, Vosk. Sit."

Vosk sat.

Teo held Eli's eye, and went back to the door, and stood there with his arms folded, and said no more. Out in the market a second music stopped.

Velk had the watch out of her apron. She checked it once, and put it back. She went to the curtain at the back and put her face to the gap at its edge, and listened, and let it fall.

"Out the front," she said. "The back's mine."

She left by the back, with the tin under her arm. Through the curtain, already going, she said, "And I didn't fit it."

Vosk was standing. Eli hadn't seen him get up. He stood straight with his head tilted, and his face was turned toward the front of the shop, and the blood was drying on his lip, and he was listening.

"It's gone quiet," Vosk said.

---

It had. Eli hadn't noticed the market's noise until there wasn't any. No music. No one selling soup. The heat was still there, pushing through the shutter at the front, and a smell had come into it, hot metal and a sweetness behind the metal that turned his stomach.

They went out the front.

The alley was empty. The market at the end of it was not. Everybody was still there, the stallholders, the pan man, the two orc women, the soup woman; they were all on their knees, every one, facing the nearest wall. Every head stayed down. On the hooks overhead the meat turned in the heat. The cup Eli had left was still on the corner of the lamp stall, with a skin on it.

The alley let out halfway along the market's side wall. The bridge end was to their left, thirty steps along the wall, back where they'd come in. The arch was off to the right and across the floor, past the soup stall and the hanging meat, with its plastic strips.

"Bridge," said Teo, very low. "Walk. Don't run."

Eli walked. Teo had him by the sleeve. Vosk was a step behind. They moved along the wall past all the bowed heads toward the bridge end, and out of the arch, through the plastic strips, young men began to come.

Six. Eight. Orcs, young, light on their feet, in yard trousers, with wire wound round their knuckles and their necks opened and stitched with wire. They took their time. They crossed the market floor between the kneeling people, spread out between Eli and the bridge, and stopped. The dwarf boy from the shop was with them, at the back. He pointed at Eli, once, and one of the young men paid him, and he ran off between the stalls. The girl with the net was there too, by the arch, with her pole on her shoulder and the light gone out of it.

One of them, not the biggest, smiled at Vosk and touched his own stitched neck and pointed at Vosk's, where the new strip was.

"Lit you up like a lamp, Terrace," he said. His eyes went to Eli's coat, and back. "And I hear the coat does tricks."

"It's paid for," said Eli. His voice came out clear and reasonable, the voice he used with the house steward. "If there's some sort of fee, I'm happy to—"

The smiling one glanced at him and turned back to Vosk.

The plastic strips moved.

The heat came first. Eli felt it on his face and the backs of his hands, a hard dry push that dried the wet out of his eyes, and every bowed head in the market went lower, and on the hooks overhead the fat on the sawn carcass started to run. The strips parted, and something came through the arch that had to turn its shoulders to fit.

He was the shape of an orc, the heavy shoulders and the long arms, but half again as tall as Vosk and twice as broad, and the light rising off the cut lit him from underneath. His skin was crusted all over the chest and arms and the slabs of the shoulders in rows, and the rows were chips. Orc chips, grey, with their threads, hundreds of them, sewn into him one by one through the skin with wire, the way you'd sew buttons on a coat, and every one of them was wet, and every one, faintly, was moving. Above the waist he was bare. Below it he had a yard apron on, black rubber, stiff with old use, like the men at the presses wore.

Low on his ribs the wire was still bright, and the skin round it was puffed and red.

He didn't say anything. He looked at Vosk, and at the strip on Vosk's neck, and Vosk took a step toward him that Eli doubted he'd meant to take, and stopped.

His eyes moved to Eli. Up, and down. The steward went over a bill that way, before it went up.

"King'll have the new one," said the smiling one. He tipped his head at Eli. "And that. Coat like that, in Hookline. Worth more than any chip down here."

"He's with me," Eli said. It wasn't the steward voice now. "We've paid. He's with—"

"Teo," Vosk said. "Take him up the stair."

"He's with me—"

"Teo."

Vosk walked out from the wall, away from them, onto the open floor.

Teo had both hands in Eli's coat. Eli could feel them shaking, and he could hear Teo's breathing, and it was slow, slow, in through the nose, out through the mouth, as Teo had breathed the time the flyer lost a fan over the sumps.

The King lifted one hand, palm up, and held it there, weighing.

Every orc kneeling in the market sagged. All at once, the orc at the pans, the two women, the orc children by the cut, their heads dropped onto their chests, and whatever was in them went out through the backs of their necks and across the floor to him. And Vosk jerked. His head went back hard, and his mouth came open, and steam rose out of his collar. The air before the King went thick and bright, and rolled across the market floor at him.

Vosk put his hand up.

The air at his palm went white. The noise hit, and the floor heaved under Eli's feet and dropped away again, and everything round Vosk went, and hardly any of it toward the King. A stall to his left burst into its boards and its pots and a woman who had been kneeling behind it, and all of it flew sideways through the air together and into the wall. Hooks on their chains swung out level and swung back. The sawn carcass fell. A post by the soup stall broke in half, and the hot-box went over, and the soup went across the floor in a sheet. A pot went past Eli's face close enough to lift his hair, and the wall at his back cracked. Two of the crew were down; one lay under the soup stall with his legs out, and the other wasn't anywhere Eli could see.

Vosk hadn't moved. He looked at his hand. The palm had split across, three times, and it was steaming. He tried to close it, and it closed slowly, finger by finger.

The King watched him.

Somebody had the back of Eli's coat and yanked, and Teo's hands tore out of it, and Eli was hauled back against a chest that smelled of wire and sweat. A short one, with a gap where his front teeth should have been, had come along the wall behind them while they were all watching Vosk. A fist wound with wire had Eli's collar. The other hand had a strapping cutter, and the cutter was under his ear, cold, the hooked end tucked in behind the lobe.

"Terrace," the gap-toothed one shouted. He wasn't smiling; he was concentrating. "Terrace. Hand down. Put it down or he goes up the stair without his ears. King don't need his ears."

Vosk turned his head.

He only turned it. He found Eli across the floor with the cutter under his ear.

"Vosk," Eli said. It came out high. "Vosk, put it down. Put it down. Please."

Vosk's hand stayed up, and the white at his palm went thin, and he looked at Eli, and the King closed his fist, and Vosk's left arm broke.

Eli heard it. Loud, and it went on, because it broke in more than one place, above the elbow and below it and in the wrist, and the arm turned in a way it had no way of turning, and Vosk made a high thin sound through his nose and went down on one knee.

Eli put his hand up.

He'd done this before. At fifteen he'd pulled the whole flyer up out of the lake with the water pouring off it, and been sick for a day after. A cutter was nothing. A cutter was a fork. Take it off him. Take it.

Nothing came.

He pushed. The cutter went in behind his ear and a warm line ran down his neck. His hand was up and open in front of his face and it was a hand. That was all it was.

They were all looking at it. The crew, the kneeling heads come up off the floor, Vosk. It had been like that on the roof with the chair, everyone looking, and the chair sitting there on its four feet.

Across the floor Vosk was still down on one knee, still looking at him, waiting for it. The King turned his fist over, and Vosk's shoulders came up round his ears, and steam came out of his mouth.

Teo swung a soup ladle at the gap-toothed one's head.

A big iron ladle the blast had thrown against the wall at his feet, and he swung it with both hands, and it landed on the shoulder instead, and the cutter jerked off Eli's ear and round and opened Teo's palm in passing. Teo made no sound. He swung again. This time it found the side of the head and made a flat sound, and the gap-toothed one let go and sat down. Teo hit him again on the top of the head as he went, carefully, as if driving a peg. He dropped the ladle, got Eli by the collar with his good hand, and pulled him along the wall toward the bridge end.

"Bridge," he said.

"Vosk—"

"Bridge."

Eli went. He went backwards along the wall with his hand still up in front of him, and he didn't pull against Teo, and by the third stall he was going faster than Teo was pulling.

Vosk rose off his knee. This time he kept his good hand down. He turned, the whole of him, until he faced the arch and the King before it, and stood there with his good arm and his broken arm both hanging, and Eli saw him shut his eyes.

It all came out of him at once.

There was no aim in it. Most of it went down. The floor under his own feet went to gravel in a ring, and the cracks ran out from him in every direction. The floor under the King split down the middle and went up in two slabs, and so did the floor under the soup stall, and under the kneeling people along the far wall, who were up now and running, and some of them rose with it. The hooks tore out of the ceiling in rows and came down in their chains. A pipe burst in the wall a yard from Eli's head and blew steam and black spray. Then the whole market was running, for the bridge, the pan man, the two women, the children off the edge of the cut with their nets, and the crew were running in among them with their cutters still in their fists and nobody to use them on. One of them went backwards over the edge into the cut without a sound. Teo put Eli flat against the wall and held him there, and the crowd went past them onto the bridge and filled it, rail to rail, shoving, and somebody went off the side.

Vosk opened his eyes. Over the arch, where a row of hooks had torn out, the ceiling hung down in a belly of old stone with a crack along it to the wall. He looked up at it. He looked at the King, standing under the edge of it. He started to walk.

He walked at the arch, one step and another, and with every step more of him gave. The skin on the backs of his knuckles split open over the bone, all four, and the backs of both hands after. Blood ran from his nose and his ears and the corners of his eyes. Steam poured off his shoulders in sheets. The fingers of his good hand had come open, and he was trying to shut them, Eli could see him trying, and they bent back instead, all four, and stayed back. He kept walking. He couldn't put it anywhere. It went where it went, through the floor, through the stalls, up into the ceiling, and he carried it with him to the arch and let it.

The King had one hand flat in the air and he was giving ground. One step back, under the arch. Another, into the strips. Bits of the floor were hitting him and he let them. His chest was smoking, and some of the chips sewn into it had burst and were running.

Eli had his hand up. It was pointed at Vosk's back now. Come back.

Vosk got as far as the lip of it.

The arch came down.

It came down front first, a great sliding roar of old stone and ceiling. The lip of it took the King across the chest and put him over on his back in the strips, and the rest came down after him, on his legs and his middle and the two young men who'd been standing by him, and the front edge of it came down on Vosk at the lip and knocked him flat, and the dust rolled out across the market and over Eli and Teo where they stood by the bridge, hot, and they were blind in it.

Teo found Eli's wrist in the dust and pulled the hand down and held on to it.

It went quiet. Things fell, here and there. Steam hissed. Somebody on the bridge was crying out the same word.

Eli pulled his wrist out of Teo's hand and went back.

---

Vosk was on his back at the foot of the heap with the dust settling on him. The front edge of the arch lay across him from the hips down, and the rest of the heap was on top of it.

Steam came off him everywhere, off his chest and his face and his good arm, and where it rose the skin had gone dark and tight and shiny, and along the forearms it had split. Blood came out of his ears in two slow lines. His eyes were open. The whites of them were full of blood, both, to the edges. His broken arm was under him at an angle. The fingers of the other hand still stood back.

Eli knelt in the dust. He put a hand on Vosk's chest and snatched it off again. He got both hands under the edge of the stone and lifted, and nothing moved, and he lifted again.

"We'll get you out," he said. "We'll get you out. Teo. Teo, help me get it off him."

Teo had come back. He stood over them. His eyes were on the heap where the arch had been, not on Vosk. Somewhere on the far side of it a stone went down by itself, and then another.

"Vosk," Eli said. "Vosk, look at me."

Vosk's eyes moved and found him. His mouth was working. When it came there was blood in it, and a hiss on it, because something in his chest had gone.

"Did it."

"You did. You did it. Teo—"

"No." His hand came up off the floor, an inch, and fell back. "Listen."

"We'll carry you."

"Ilder."

Eli bent down.

"Ilder's lot." A breath. "Their yard. You were." The hand came up again, not as high. "Small."

"Don't talk."

"His dad's. Fob." The breath stuck halfway and he waited for it. "Made me. Dance."

"Vosk—"

"Wet myself." His mouth pulled down at one corner and stayed. "Everybody. Laughing."

His eyes went past Eli, up, to one side, to the far wall of the market, and came back.

"You come off the step."

Eli was holding his hand. He didn't remember taking it. It was too hot to hold, and he held it.

"Bit him." Blood came out of the corner of his mouth. "Wouldn't let go. Screaming at them." A breath. "He's mine. Leave him."

"I don't remember," Eli said.

"I know." Something rattled. "That's all right."

Eli opened his mouth.

"I'm proud," Vosk said.

The breath went in and stopped halfway. His chest worked at it, and his eyes held on to Eli's while it did, and then it came loose.

"I'm proud to die. For the."

His eyes left Eli's face. They went along the wall and over the broken stalls and up into the dust, looking, and came back.

"Man," he said. "For the man that boy." The breath again. "Grew into."

He stopped. Eli waited.

"I picked it."

His lips went on moving after that, and Eli bent close, and most of it was breath.

"Nobody." A long gap. The rest of it, when it came, was hardly there. "Spent me."

"Eli," said Teo.

The heap of stone where the arch had been moved. A slab near the top tilted and slid and went down the side of the heap with a long scraping noise, and dust ran after it, and from under the heap, from deep under it, came heat. A hand came up out of the gap it left. It was as broad as a shovel, and there were chips sewn across the back of it in rows, and it took the next slab by the edge and pulled, and the slab came.

"Eli. Now."

"He's talking—"

"Go," Vosk said. He was looking past Eli again, at the wall, at whatever was behind the wall. His hand in Eli's hand had gone loose. "Oh," he said. "It's right there."

He didn't say anything else. His eyes stayed open on the wall. Steam kept coming off him, less and less.

Eli knelt there.

Teo took him by the back of the collar and pulled him up off the floor, the whole weight of him, and Eli's legs gave and he went sideways and Teo held him up against the wall of the nearest stall. Teo slapped him across the face, hard, open-handed, once, with the cut hand, and left blood on him.

"Walk," Teo said.

Eli walked.

They were nearly at the bridge end when three of the crew came back out of the dust from wherever they'd run to, grey to the eyes. One of them saw the two humans and stopped. He looked at Eli. He took a step.

Teo kept hold of Eli's wrist and held to a walk. He lifted his chin at the heap, where something under the stones had begun to shove.

"He's coming out," Teo said. "Want him to find you over here with us?"

The crewman looked at the heap, and at Eli a while longer, and turned and went to the heap, where one of the others was already on his knees, and got down beside him and began pulling stones off it with his hands.

The last one crossed to where Vosk lay and knelt on his chest with one knee, and turned his head to one side by the jaw, and took a long thin hook off his belt and put the point of it in the new strip on his neck.

Teo pulled Eli onto the bridge, and the bridge was warm under his feet all the way across.

---

They went up.

They climbed the Long Stair with the rest of the Scald, which was all climbing that night, as many as could fit, orcs and dwarves and humans, all of them quiet. A woman ahead of Eli carried a child asleep on her shoulder with its mouth open, and somewhere below an old man had sat down on a step and the climbers were going round him. Every hundred steps the reader on the landing read them, and the orcs on either side of Eli flinched as it went cold down their arms, and he got his light with the other humans. He had blood on his hands and on the knees of his trousers and down the side of his neck from behind his ear, and dust in his hair and his teeth, and the reader looked at him and went green.

Somewhere around the sumps his hands started. The shaking climbed his arms. Teo kept hold of his wrist and kept climbing and kept quiet about it. Hand up. Vosk turning his head to look. Put it down. Please. Slap, slap on the wet lawn. He could dance. On the next landing he tried to make the hand into a fist and it wouldn't go, and the reader looked at him, and went green.

At the top of the stair the mid-levels were exactly the same. The feeds. The lanes ticking overhead. The ribbon said LEGION RECRUITMENT AT RECORD. In the flesh market at Ninth Spur they were hosing down the slabs.

Teo bought two bottles of water from a machine and they drank them standing in a doorway, and Teo was sick, neatly, once, against the doorway's side, and wiped his mouth and drank some more water and said, "Right," and they walked on.

---

The gate was where it had been, the ring of dark glass in its white arch.

Eli stopped before it. His feet had stopped. He was filthy to the elbows and he had a dead man's blood dried in the creases of his fingers, and his face was swelling on one side where Teo had hit him, and there was grey dust in his hair from under the Scald, and he stood at the arch and waited for it to see him.

Something in the glass looked at him.

The light came up green at his shoulder. The soft voice said, "Good morning." The arch let him through.

They walked up the white path between the water channels, under the trees, with the house windows only now lighting on both sides and the sky over the Tower going grey. Eli's house had a light on in the kitchen. Someone always left it on.

Teo ran water in the kitchen sink and held Eli's hands under it, one and then the other, and scrubbed them with the brush the cook used for the pans, and the water went brown, and pink, and clear, and Teo kept scrubbing after it was clear, and at last he stopped. He stood there with the brush. The side of his thumb was raw to the quick, and the cut across his palm had opened again in the hot water. His own hands had started to shake now, at the sink, and he stared at them, put the brush down, and put them in his armpits.

"She'll want to know what happened to her brush," he said. Not to Eli. To the sink.

Eli sat down at the table. He checked his wrist for the time, and the wrist was bare.

The second-best coat was on the step inside the yard door, folded in four.

He could ring the account. A clinic, a flyer, men to go down and bring him up. It had never once asked what anything was for. He sat with that, and left it.

The fruit bowl was where he'd left it, and the letter was under it, pale, with the corner showing. He slid it out. Heavy, for a letter. He put his thumb on the seal, flat, and held it there. He slid the letter back under the bowl, and pushed the bowl down on it with both hands, and kept them there.

Outside, in the yard, under the long house, the barracks bell was going for the morning count. He heard the steward's voice come up through the window, flat, reading off the slate. A number, and somebody answered. A number, and somebody answered.

A number.

The steward waited. "Vosk?" he said, up at the kitchen light, not loud.

Eli kept his hands on the bowl.

The steward made a mark, and read the next.
