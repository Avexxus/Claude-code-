# The World — An Overview

*A plain-language tour of the saga as it stands at the end of October 2026. The story is still being written, so every character name here is a working name, and where something big hasn't been decided yet, this says so instead of guessing. Spoilers throughout, including the ending.*

---

## The pitch

Two men opened a door they should never have touched. Derrek was a far-future wizard-scientist with few peers, and Jon was his top apprentice, the one Derrek saw as his equal. Their portal fused them with time, and eternity made them gods. The portal also tore a hole in the shield protecting their universe from what lies above it. Since then Jon has reset time over and over, runs of about ten thousand years each, a thousand of them or more, and every reset rips the hole wider. Derrek knows something Jon doesn't: each reset also sweeps back the horrors trying to get in. So Derrek has made himself the greatest evil the world has ever known, so that Jon will keep hating him, keep fighting him, and keep resetting. And Jon, warm and sincere and beloved, is the true villain of the series. Around their war turns a whole world: giants whose oldest rite once forged gods, a dragon empire founded on a betrayal, elves bound into one authoritarian will, dwarves who sell to every side, humans who worship an emperor nobody has seen, and soul thieves who have mostly become monsters. Every book ends at a window at the heart of a tower, with two men.

## How reality works

**Matter came first.** The physical universe and its forces were already there, running on their own laws with nobody behind them. What came later was *experience*. The One was once a single consciousness whose deepest wish was to know what it was not, so it came apart into every mind, soul and spark of awareness that would ever exist. Even the One lived inside physics: it mastered the universe; it didn't author it. So every living being is a body plus a fragment of the One, and that fragment, its self and the source of its magic, is called **Remnant**. Soul and Remnant are the same thing. A rock has no fragment of its own, which is why rocks don't think or do magic. But matter can *hold* a soul. A body is only matter made alive, so the more body-like a material is, the better it holds one. That held soul is what an enchantment is, and the soul inside feels something: dread, with no brain to understand it.

"The One" is just what people in our universe call theirs, the way we say "the sun." It stays in the background and its story is never told on the page. Every race came into being in that single shattering. For the long-lived races it's only a few lifetimes back. For the short-lived races it has faded into myth. New souls are gathered from loose Remnant drifting in the world into each child at conception, and nobody is reincarnated. The brain belongs to the body, not the soul. Whether a self survives death, and where it goes, is left open on purpose. Nothing is ever truly destroyed, so bringing someone back is possible in principle, though the odds are close to nothing.

**The regress.** Above our reality sits another, above that another, forever, with no first level, and it's the same story at every level. Each plane of reality is a being that came apart so far it can no longer pull itself back together. It has forgotten it was ever one thing, and what it dissolved into becomes the plane below. Beside every coming-apart, some part always holds on. That's our One: one level up, a single being came apart. The pieces that let go each became a One with a universe of its own, and ours is one of them. The others are our **sibling worlds**, siblings rather than copies. The pieces that held on are the **Unified Ones**.

**The level above** is the same kind of thing as our One, only older and stronger. It runs on Remnant too, and its population is varied: predatory, kind, indifferent. The **Unified Ones** hold together and want to protect the shatter; they built the bubble that keeps our level safe. Every level of the regress has the same three positions: a **keeper**, a **consumer** and a **Blight**. They are titles, not natures, and whoever holds one has their own motives. "Blight" is meant to sound like rot.

**Three worlds.** Each dimension holds three worlds: ours, and two lifeless copies of it tucked alongside. The elves call them **heaven** and **hell**. The elves built a rift device that reaches them, and they own it and are credited with it, the way a people might be credited with electricity. Whether they made those worlds or found them is argued over, and whether the worlds were there all along is unknown. "The elves built heaven" is what the world believes, not necessarily what happened.

**The breach.** Jon and Derrek's portal didn't create anything. It **opened the shield**, a door to what lies above. It stands in the main city, the first and largest rift, and another rift has appeared every time time has been torn open since. Rifts leak magic because the level above is bleeding through, so they are reservoirs every race fights over, though they are never the source of anyone's magic. Whether that spot was always a rift is unanswerable: it was always that spot, and Derrek always chose it.

The cruel part is that **every reset does two opposite things.** It rips the breach wider, and it throws the invaders back out. Crossing costs them enormous effort, and each reset scrambles them and dumps them home to pay that cost again. The resets were breaking the door and sweeping the doorstep at the same time. Derrek knows both halves. If Jon ever stops, what's on the other side crosses once and stays.

The portal also tore open every universe connected to theirs. In each sibling world, a different pair of people opened their own portal at the same moment, driven by a pull the universe was born with alongside reunion. Jon and Derrek broke first, and each later pair came an eternity after the last, so Jon and Derrek are the center of their family of universes. Within the endless regress they're still just "one wheel of one car on one world." If their war ends badly, one chunk of reality goes dark.

**The keeper.** Something answered the portal: a being whose whole universe *is* itself, a potential universe that never came apart, a Unified One at full scale. It is our universe's **keeper**, the one who maintains the bubble around it ("good," in quotes), and that is how it lends the two men their time powers. At the breach, Jon and Derrek saw it standing outside. It's unnamed on purpose, too vast to understand from below: the shared patron of both men, neither of them exactly willing, more puppeteer than patron. It never supported the Blight. Human faith worships it without ever meeting it. What it wants is undecided by design. Its appearance is the one presence before which even Derrek goes quiet and respectful, and the world can't look away.

**Time gods.** Since every sibling world has its pair, there are many time gods, and they have a society. Its founders were the third and fourth men to break their world open, a pair who stayed friends. One of them once stepped into Jon and Derrek's war and was torn apart; which one is kept deliberately unclear. The survivor built the society on one law: watch, never join, or lose its protection. He still watches, under a cover name, "the Historian." He's closer to an operative than a scholar, he has watched longer than anyone but Jon and Derrek, and he likes Jon. Eventually the society cracks into civil war.

**In-betweeners** don't descend from the One at all. They are an equally ancient lineage living in their own bubble between the worlds, outside the regress entirely (why and how is still to be worked out). They stay alien and apart: remakers, obeying physics but running an older system, **the Rule**, instead of Remnant. An in-betweener never really stands in our world. It carries its **Void**, a pocket of its own, which it can lay over real rooms or real land. Inside, it sets a binding condition that traps everyone until the terms are met, and its **Chain** drags victims in. It can split into many pieces, but split too far and no piece remembers who it was; that's its true death. It's bound by its Rule the way a spider is "bound" by its web, and within its domain a rule-beast can't die, though it can feel. A Rule can even cover a whole landscape, a maze of warped space where peoples are born and die without ever getting out.

**What ages.** Remnant is ageless; only flesh grows old. A bigger soul slows aging but never stops it. From shortest-lived to longest: **orcs, humans, dwarves, elves, dragons, giants**, and then the great gods, who only *seem* eternal. True immortality is a different thing entirely, a matter of what a being is, and only Jon, Derrek and the rule-beasts have it. Jon and Derrek are the most powerful beings in the story, beyond the gods, and no one can coerce them. No lesser being can kill Derrek. Only a god armed with power from beyond the regress could threaten him, and only one powerful enough to outsmart him.

**A dead sky.** Beyond Earth the universe is real but lifeless. The One chose Earth, and it is everyone's homeworld. When the races finally spread into space, they settle empty worlds. There are no aliens anywhere.

## How magic works (briefly)

Magic is a being reaching into reality and making it do what they want. Your Remnant extends a kind of **appendage** that grabs the universe; how far and how hard it reaches depends on your Remnant, your skill and your focus. Shake particles and you get heat. Pull charges apart and you get lightning. Crush or rip the heart of an atom and you get an explosion. Dent space and you get gravity. Turning matter into other matter is the hardest act of all, and botching it blows the atom apart. Humans call all of this "science," because dragons were their first teachers, and in this world faith, magic and science are one act under different names.

There are three dials. **Remnant** is how strong you are. **Control** is how skilled. The third is an **inner sense**, a perception that comes from Remnant and that nobody can explain: "you mostly feel with a second inner sense." Basic magic needs only control, but finer work needs a finer sense, so a dull sense makes deep magic costly rather than forbidden. You can be a "numb titan," enormously strong and nearly blind. The bill is **fatigue**: magic is work, and you tire like a laborer. **Remnant itself can never be burned or spent.** Nobody audits the physics, and nobody is a "fire mage." Words like fire or gravity just describe what an act looked like afterward, and a character's magic grows out of who they are.

Rarely, someone turns that grip on another soul. That's **soul magic**, forbidden and dangerous to the user, and it's where possession, ghosts and the worst crimes in the world come from. Taking someone else's Remnant is "stealing their production"; exactly how a thief puts it to use is still being worked out. Sensing other beings' souls at all is a rare skill that only some very talented masters ever learn. The exception is the elves, whose **bond** is innate, constant and collective, and theirs alone. It makes them the best soul-sensers there are, but it doesn't track any one elf: an elf who dies far away dies alone, outside the bond.

Power can also come from **pacts** (a patron teaches you its technique, you pay for every use in your own effort, and a tether ties you to it) and **enchantments** (a soul held in matter), and from the in-betweeners' Rule. Machines can copy magic's effects, but never the act itself.

**Gods.** Every god was born of flesh, on one world. Dragons, giants and elves can grow into gods of their own; humans get there only by fusing with time, as Jon and Derrek did, or by theft. The singular gods, the ones who rose as themselves, despise changelings, though real power earns respect anyway, and nothing about how someone rose decides whether they act like a god or a demon. A religion is a portrait of its god and a recruiting network for the champions it gives pacts to, and every race has one, worshipping gods, Unified Ones, in-betweeners, or false gods who were never real at all. The greatest giant god is the most powerful god after Jon and Derrek, and he is the dragon god's nemesis. The current thinking gives each race two gods, one self-made and one who rose through soul magic, though the slate is still clay. Angels and demons are one kind of being under two names, and the same kind as changelings: the vastest masters of soul magic.

Over the ages, understanding grows: medieval people see fire, water, earth and air; the modern era names the forces underneath; the far future wields them at monumental scale.

## The ages of the world

**The age of giants.** The world is already ancient by the medieval era, full of ruins nobody understands. The giants, with the largest souls of any people, felt a duty to keep order. **They wrote every law**, punished every breach swiftly and ruthlessly, and under them crime effectively didn't exist. The age was beautiful and rich in culture. It also held the other races in forced protection, without consent and without any way out. The orcs were born into bondage then. The elves alone always stood outside giant control. Giants and dragons call it the golden age; the elves call their own era "their golden age." The rest of the world doesn't remember it at all, because the dragons erased the record during the Darkest Age. The moral line between giants and dragons is deliberately murky.

**Betrayal day.** The dragons, the giants' seconds, coveted their halls and believed they'd rule better. They spun a coalition narrative and sent humans and orcs to fight, promising freedom as equals. Why the giants fell, every culture tells differently. The opening day is known, though. The dragon who struck was the **giant emperor's closest advisor**, a brilliant user who knew he'd never be king and made sure his line would live in the history books forever. In the heart of the capital, the giant king wrapped himself in a field that turned all matter to mist and began crushing the world around the dragon. The dragon spat out a rock he'd made and hidden in his stomach, shattered it, and unleashed everything he had in one last blast as his flesh melted off his bones. The dragon died. He'd known he would, and did it out of love for his people and resentment of a crown he'd never wear.

**Out of the ruin rose the Tower**, the center of everything that follows. It is the giants' last great Convergence: the emperor used the bodies of his city as his material and trapped the city's souls inside the stone, and he dissolved into it, too many souls at once. The Tower is the largest giant city there ever was, compressed into one shape, and it still faintly feels. The land around it stayed radioactive for a long time. The same day, dragons the advisor had chosen killed giant rulers *and* the reigning dragon rulers and took their thrones. Where keeps held out, real war followed. The giants' heroes died; the dragons hunted down the giants who turned to rage; the rest went cold, and a secret few faked obedience while nursing a hatred that would pay off ages later. The dwarves sold arms to both sides.

**The Age of Dragons**, which everyone else calls **the Darkest Age**. The dragons aimed every race at dragon purposes. Humans became an indentured people and orcs were re-chained at once. The misery is real, and it's why the races hate dragons. But the name is unfair: dragons freed the world from the giants, ruled capably, and carried the giants' work into the age whose science later empires stood on. They taught humans, too. The age was long. The dragon empire has never known peace, either, because the dispossessed houses still live under the line that usurped them.

**The rise of humanity.** Humans won by exploiting that fracture. They united a coalition with their charisma, and some dragons joined them to save themselves. The dragons fought for a long time, killing kings and leveling cities, and for an age the two ruled side by side. Both giants and dragons have ruled from the shadows, in different eras. Dragons still hold remote giant-built palaces, and some descendants of the advisor "king" still rule kingdoms. But **the third age is named for humans.** Through it all, **the city never moved**: the giants' capital became the dragons' seat, which became the human capital, with the Tower at its heart. Derrek took the Tower from the heart of the dragon empire. The irony no one noticed in time is that humans, not dragons, ruled the *actual* darkest age, the age that holds Derrek.

Here science didn't grow from curiosity. **Science and magic rose together alongside humanity's endless war for dominance.** Humans barely had an edge, and Derrek's endless loops refined it; he worked at a dragon-owned company and studied at a dragon school. Before the elves left, Earth reached a high-tech peak: every race had spaceflight, drones and AI. Computing was born from the dwarves' magic-infused metalwork, and humans coerced the knowledge out of them. An AI has no soul, so it can do no magic and is nearly powerless in the physical world, but inside the digital world it's sovereign.

**The exodus and the modern day.** The elves left Earth on a single seedship, and Jon led Mika and the elves away. Inside elven society it came as an order from the crown, not a vote. Everyone else read it as a grab for the stars, and a cold war followed; in the end the other races drove the elves off. What came after wasn't an apocalypse but a wounded decline lasting centuries. By the modern era, humans dominate at full scale in a magic-infused technostate where science serves profit and war, and Derrek has nearly erased the elves from memory. Humans living in elven-built cities believe their ancestors built them. When the elves return, they find the world more depraved than the one they left.

**The far future.** The elves teach the other races the craft to spread across the stars, not seeing that their own peace came from the bond and not from wealth. Abundance only feeds the others' greed, and heartbroken and complicit, the elves pull away again. The far future is the age of mastery, and **it is still brutal**: the galaxy conquered scarcity and keeps tearing itself apart anyway. *We fixed everything except what beings are.* Earth is a planet-wide city with the Tower at its heart, the human capital though not the only capital, and the Galactic Council sits there, in human buildings. Officially the council is in stalemate; in practice humans are winning, by sheer numbers and by the very laws the giants and dragons wrote, while blocs form inside it. Human expansion runs on law, custom and business, and now and then a "peacekeeping" mission that is really an invasion of a world under dragon or giant rule. The rich live on far colonies with hidden fleets, and drones do the labor. Clones have souls and magic, and everyone pretends they don't. Governments run a legal trade in organs with a black market underneath, a dwarven and human enterprise; orcs are left out of it, and dragons are glad of the bodies to study. The rift is at its widest, and reality is breaking. Humanity faces three crises: the war the dragons started, the Orc King's plague, and the elves, sworn to kill the human Emperor. Each race crosses the stars its own way. Humans traverse hell; dwarves squeeze dimensions; elves shrink one of their dimensions to cross; dragons power their jumps with crews strapped into focusing chambers.

## The peoples

**No race is a monolith**; each has rival powers and its own fracture. **Every race carries every vice**, greedy, proud, jealous and wrathful. What differs is which one surfaces, what story covers it, and how long a clock its people live on. **Strife is the natural state**, peace is rare and hard-won, and nobody has clean hands. One cycle runs under all of it. The long-lived races personally remember atrocities humans forgot generations ago. To the innocent humans of today, that hatred looks like baseless prejudice, so wounded pride answers it with the next atrocity.

### Giants
Longest-lived of all. One giant can span every era. They are dragon-sized with a human shape, cunning and focused, and the most potent users alive. They *grow* seamless stone instead of carving it. Their oldest rite is **Convergence**, which works on bodies. The funeral version releases the dead giant's soul. The original rite was something greater: it forged gods, by silencing a crowd of souls into a single voice, a lost secret remembered as "soul mortar." The greatest giant god is a million minds in perfect unison. By the time the dragons took the throne, the god-forging was gone, and only the basic craft of forging walls was left. After the dragons slaughtered their kings, the giants twisted what remained into a weapon and then a business. They went cold, and underneath they wage a war of attrition on timescales even dragons can't match: **they build the dragons' warships so dragons will kill each other.** These **cathedral ships** are made from many dead giants each, and when the giant dead ran low, from lives bought in bulk, mostly orcs. In the far future, everyone thinks the giants are dwindling; really they're quietly rebuilt and rich beyond belief. When a dragon destroys the heart of a giant forge world, they stop pretending and take their own fleets to war.

### Dragons
The strongest users short of gods, time gods and in-betweeners, and second-longest-lived, so some remember betrayal day. They stay on four legs: the magical grip is their hands, and servants do the labor. They are humanity's first teachers, the premier scholars and historians of the world, and the ones who erased its history. They are a **feudal empire** of families, split between the houses the advisor raised and the dispossessed, who in the far future buy cathedral ships to murder each other. They're builders, too, and fly ships of their own make. They wield many breaths: fire, lightning, rot, frost, corrosion, transmutation. A dragon can sense your dwarven shield, admire it, and kill you with a breath that changes what the shield *is*. Their weakness is the burst: supreme in short bursts, then drained, so a patient foe or a coordinated group can bring one down at terrible cost. In space their great weapon is the **focusing chamber**, which pours a dragon's full power into one plasma beam, though only a few times before exhaustion. Since humanity's rise, young dragons have moved their wealth into human commerce and law, while the old stay in their castles.

### Elves
Ancient but mortal. What defines them is **the bond**: elves sense one another as a people, lean toward the collective, and cooperate without friction. That's why they advance fastest and why the bond rejects any foreign self like a graft, making them **the one race the human machine could never absorb.** They have never been weak. The unity has a hard edge. **Elven society is authoritarian**, wholly subservient to the king, and its two pillars are extreme discrimination against other races and harsh punishment for their own. They pursue reunion at all costs and use it to justify total control. The elf queen's famous vigilance comes from working the bond hard. Elves alone feel resets. A reset doesn't delete people; it changes the chances every conception depends on, so the lost **never existed**, and elves grieve for people no one can remember. Hunted for their organs in the far future, they have one more reason to keep to themselves, though one elf can outmatch a handful of humans.

The elves reach the two lifeless worlds through their rift device: **heaven**, a utopia wrapped in light, and its sister, a prison of darkness, drought and famine, cruel on purpose. Hell was first meant for soul thieves, those who stole a fellow elf's soul or body; over time it filled with dissidents and vagrants too. The bond works inside each world but not between them, which creates one of the setting's ironies: entering heaven cuts you off from the trillions of your people, so **heaven is beautiful and empty, and hell is full**. Hell holds whole societies, some living in relative peace, and the noise of it is angry. Powerful changelings down there keep its population healthy, like a farm, and it has become a forge for gods full of elf-haters. Many have broken out over the ages, and many **dark elves** (a placeholder name) have been born outside it since. They come back war-trained and hate-filled and are hunted across the galaxy. The elves' **Inquisition** exists mainly to stop whoever causes the resets, which means it is unknowingly hunting its own god. Its secret is worse: it quietly sells captured dark elves through dwarven traffickers to rich humans, and "hunting soul thieves" was the cover. A dwarven chip that lets its wearer pass as an elf, perfected by dissecting elven captives, is the one deliberate crack in their armor.

### Humans
Short-lived, endlessly multiplying, the widest learners, and arguably the most powerful people of all. Their mask is **supremacy**: the belief that they are the One's true, rightful anointed children. Pride may be their deadliest vice, though not their only one. Their one weird specific is **fervent zealotry**. They worship Derrek and Jon's patron, in a faith Derrek started as he conquered and enslaved them, by force and charisma both. They build on gifts from longer-lived races (elven medicine, giant architecture, dragon law, dwarven craft) while treating the givers as tools or enemies, and the churn grinds humans hardest of all, by their own hands. True believers and realists despise each other. In the far future they're ruled openly by **the Emperor**, whose face no one knows. His Legion defies the elven Inquisition, and his people's goal is to prove humans were never "lesser" and to win independence from the elder races.

### Orcs
Shortest-lived, most crowded, weakest in magic, enormously gifted physically. Greed is their most common expression: humanity's game, played with no mask that works, which is why they're hated for doing openly what others do in silk. They were chained three times: born into bondage under the giants, re-chained by the dragons right after fighting for them, and subjugated again by humans. Refusing to accept being less, they were forced to be less, and "they earned it" is purely the world's lie. Each orc remembers only their own lifetime's wounds, but the people know they were wronged, the way you know a bruise by the ache. Their word for that ache is **hate**, and their culture runs on it. They lean on pact magic because borrowed power is the most open road left to them. In the far future they're **the galaxy's cattle**, bought by the trillion as ship material. Then there's **the Orc King**, a symbolic title, since orcs never had kings. He's a singularly gifted orc born out of his people's suffering, carrying a **pact over entropy**: rot, plague, famine. He moves through the galaxy like a plague, his dead raised as soulless flesh, freeing orcs as he tears down worlds. He is due for a full rework, and his patron is undecided.

### Dwarves
Their root is **independence at all costs**, and greed is the strategy they chose to protect it. Selling to your ally's enemy is betrayal; selling to both sides is just business. They made their craft so indispensable that conquering them was never worth the price. On the surface they're a loud, fiercely loyal family, ruthless to outsiders, yet when an in-betweener came for them, **they sold off the dwarves they considered weak.** Several emperors run bureaucratic autocracies tied closely to business, and dwarves keep a district in every race's cities. They are the race that **thrived most of all**. The dwarves they sold, **the Returned** (a placeholder name), were born inside the in-betweener's maze-land and learned new magic there: phasing, re-forming, compressing warped space, and the secrets of captured in-betweener trinkets. Those who win their way out are free, grateful and resentful at once, and driven never to be bound again. In the far future, dwarven make is the seal of mastery behind the drives and focusing chambers, and **gnomes are the dwarves' indentured class**, sold to whoever wants one.

### Gnomes, goblins and trolls
Medieval gnomes are the one happy people, bright, loud and indifferent to wealth or rank. They're seen as the lowliest race, yet they're strong users and superb assassins. They think their gentleness keeps them safe, but they keep their homes only because no one sees them as a threat, and their villages are likely gone by the end. Goblins are the orcs' own underclass, abused until they act like the orcs' animals, though they're no different from anyone else, only weaker. Trolls whisper in their ghettos. No goblin city or troll fortress survives, and the far-future galaxy just calls a goblin "a small orc."

### Changelings: a crime, not a race
A changeling is anyone, of any people, who has mastered forbidden soul magic and bound stolen souls to their own. **There are no weak changelings**, but most of them are now monsters; only a few clever ones still move at court. The tell is a mismatch between soul and body, and the smart ones shield it or hop into bigger bodies. The ones who last take only a soul or two at a time; at that pace the original self stays in control. Take too many too fast and it goes wrong: a giant who rots into a house of horrors, a young dark elf who ruptures. There are two roads: **move** into another body and leave the old one dead, or **consume**, drawing souls into your own body. A dying elder can be scavenged, but gods are untouchable. **Everyone knows souls can be stolen**: *it's your father, it's your uncle, it's the priest at the church.* They're the least trustworthy people in the series, and utterly alone. Related crimes: a true **ghost** is a soul someone deliberately holds together after death, which costs constant effort, and kill the holder and the ghost is freed or destroyed. Every haunting is a crime scene. The dead can walk in other ways too: plague, flesh animated without a soul, souls bound to corpses or machines, and what changelings leave behind.

## Jon and Derrek

At the portal, Derrek, sure he was the greatest being who ever lived, **demanded** power and was cursed with time powers that seize him at random. Jon collapsed in awe, and the being gave him control and **tied Derrek to Jon's jumps**: whenever Jon jumps, Derrek goes too. **Derrek sees time; Jon jumps**, and Derrek's whole craft is aiming those jumps. Then came the first window, where Derrek showed Jon the Blight and Jon had to keep jumping or the universe would end. **Only Jon can reset.** Every run lasts about ten thousand years, and there have been a thousand or more. Both men are eldritch now, broken things in ageless bodies. Their bond is older than either remembers: in a life since erased, Jon saved a young Derrek from a cult of priests, on a whim, because he liked killing. Derrek's devotion is an echo of that forgotten life. They still fight.

**Derrek** never supported the Blight. At the very start he wanted, like the dragons before him, to free his people, and that is why he opened the portal. Now he needs Jon to hate him, because hatred keeps Jon resetting, and resetting holds back the Blight. Admitting he does it out of love would break Jon. So he chose to be **the greatest evil the world had ever known**. Underneath it all, what he wants is to be seen by Jon, just once, as his equal, and he'll sacrifice anyone for it. His real skill is manufacturing one more reason for Jon to reset. He's proud of his student, his people and his world, he's crushed by guilt for tearing it open, and he has only ever loved Jon. He is also a murderous, tyrannical dictator who earns no forgiveness. In the far future he is **humanity's Emperor**, ruling by overwhelming force, and the only person he fears is Jon. He engineered the dragon strike on the giants' forge world. He dabbles in soul magic but never lets anyone into his own body. At the window he always loses, and the reset is also his way of keeping Jon from finishing him. Over the war his methods escalated: first exhaustion, then horrors to break Jon's heart, then a son he planned for the day Jon stopped jumping.

**Jon** is the true villain, the worst person in the series, and he isn't hiding it. He is warm, honest and sincere; the charm is his resting state and costs him nothing, and the cold face shows only when there is nothing to gain. He is the elves' hidden god, as Derrek is humanity's open one. The soft word for it is "gardening," but **he asks for devotion**. He is breeding a warrior people for an endless war to kill Derrek, a cult leader who rules with love while his single focus corrupts everything. He doesn't truly care whether the elves exist; they're a shield, hard to destroy because they are collective. In his own eyes he is the tragic victim and a tired hero. No one ever drew more than slight disgust or pity from him, except Derrek, who drew his hatred.

The one secret Jon keeps is **Lirael**. She was the hero of her people and the sister of Mika, the elven prince. Derrek set the Council on Mika, and Jon learned that saving Mika required a dead sister, so he killed her, on purpose, with her own attack, again and again across the loops. It broke him at first and changed him; eventually he accepted the cost. He did care for her, and still does. He keeps her dead so that her loving memory of him stays intact, and only Derrek knows. Derrek has been gathering the pieces of her soul into an enchantment in the Tower, which survives every reset, to give her back to Jon one day. Jon finally stops jumping because he learns he is being manipulated, and because he is tired of killing her. He tells himself this is the final run. It's a lie. His arc was never a redemption; it was his collapse, and Jon's growth, the thing that would make any other hero win, is what dooms the world.

## The shape of the saga

**The story is Jon and Derrek's war.** Everything else is the world it happens in. The author thinks of it as the first campaign in his own setting, and others are welcome to borrow the world.

**Book numbers and eras are still placeholders**, and scenes float until they find their homes. What is set: **Book 1** opens with Derrek's son fleeing and ends at the window as the universe collapses, with no plea and no time travel shown. **A later book returns to that same window** and reveals the jumps and the pleas: Derrek begging, bringing his son, the son himself, and the Historian, who breaks his own law in that last moment because it is now or never. **Another later book is Jon's**: he wakes in the past, and it shows what he does when his pawns aren't watching, his road from Derrek's student to the rift, the jumps, Lirael and the seedship. The series spans resets and sibling universes, and **every book ends at the window, where there are only ever two men.** The book itself is a loop: it always starts on page one and always ends at the Tower, and every reread is a reset.

**Book 1.** At the edge of a human city, a young man flees a guard, and we wonder what he stole. He's the Emperor's runaway son, and no one knows the faceless Emperor is Derrek. He is a pawn, and he has no name; it will come last. Jon scoops him up and helps him escape. He's a capable man raised in the seat of power, and the book is his rise. Jon comes and goes, eccentric and charming. The hero sees an elven city where Jon lingers a moment too long at Lirael's statue, meets the last friendly giant, and leads a dark-elf woman free at the end of her long escape; she becomes his love. He gathers a crew: his master, Jon, the dark elf, a friend who fled the palace with him, an elven warrior queen Jon chose as his rival, and a cheerful gnome who is secretly an ancient changeling. The spark is a dragon destroying a giant forge world, a strike Derrek engineered, which sets off open war; the road to the Tower runs through a giant space war.

Inside the Tower, a rule-beast that Derrek trapped by rehearsing their encounter thousands of times waits in its Void. The master breaks the way open by becoming a black hole. Derrek kills the friend with casual brutality. The queen dies dueling Derrek's unloved apprentice, who then burns alive from within while Derrek yawns. The dark elf strikes with Lirael's own signature move, and Derrek turns it inside out on her in front of Jon. The changeling hurls every stolen soul at Derrek, his murdered brother last, fails, and crumbles to dust in the first quiet he has ever known. Jon, cold now in his true form, defeats Derrek. **Everyone dies.**

Dying at the window, Derrek tells the truth. Jon has heard it countless times and always forgets, so this time the speech is aimed at the son: they broke the world when they were young and arrogant, and Derrek has spent eternity holding it together. Asked why he killed his son's friends if he wants peace, Derrek sighs: would he believe the stakes otherwise? Derrek has destroyed everything to force Jon's hand. **Jon never answers.** Whether he resets is the reader's call, and a reread means he did. In the author's drafted ending, Jon looks out at horrors spilling from rifts in the sky, sighs, remembers meeting Lirael, and says, *"Well, at least I'll get to see her again,"* and the book fades to white. Exactly where that line finally lands is still being decided.
