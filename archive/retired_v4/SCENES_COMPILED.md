## Showpiece: the entropy-breath god-killer 🌊 (Book-3 set-piece, built this session)
**The mechanism (per the aging law, Constitution 4a):** even a god is *flesh that ages* — so **entropy/death magic** doesn't out-muscle the god (impossible), it **accelerates the dissolution/aging even a god's flesh can't escape.** More Remnant = more resistance = succumbs *later*, **never immune.** That gradient IS the scene's structure.
**The scene:** an enemy "god" **teleports onto a dragon's immaculate giant-built starship** — crewed by humans, elves, dwarves, orcs (a representation of the whole planet), the dragon on his throne, the crew at their stations **behind the god.** The dragon — who does **NOT care about his crew at all** (cold race-characterization; they're expendable, not a heartbeat's hesitation) — **fires preemptively**, unleashing his **secret entropy breath** straight through the god (and through his own crew behind him). *The dragon is the CASTER, never a casualty — do not invent dragon-peril.*
**The escalation (the gradient made visible):** the breath "doesn't seem to do much at first." Then the dragon's crew behind the god begin to **age/dissolve toward maximum entropy — in ASCENDING order of Remnant/lifespan:** the **orc bursts first** (least resistance), then **humans**, then **dwarves**, then **elves** — each death buying a beat, the horror **climbing the ladder.** The **god stands unmoving**, cocky, certain he's beyond it — *boasts* ("your crew is dead, how will you get home?"). The **dragon sweats with doubt** (his secret technique seems to have failed). Then the god — most resistant, having **never felt a bodily sensation** in his existence — **trembles, confused**, and **begins to age/dissolve LAST.** Dawning horror: he was never *exempt*, only *delayed* — his resistance bought him a front-row seat to his own doom (he watched the ladder climb toward him). The being who believed himself beyond flesh learns he was always flesh.
**Why it lands:** the god and the orc die the *same death*, separated only by Remnant — the duality and the aging law fused (greatest and lowliest, the same kind of thing, taken in order by the only thing that ever differed). *(Entropy magic is rare/terrible — a dragon's secret breath — high on the difficulty scale; science-FLAVOR only, NO homework on "how it forces maximum entropy.")*

## Scene 1 — The Giant on the Scarred Reach
**Where it fits:** Book 1, second half. Mika (newly crowned) and his small company are en route to secure dwarven aid, crossing the dead country toward the holds. The giant blocks their road — their first encounter with an elder race, and the first sign the wider world holds things far above their scale.

The giant does not move for them. "I have long since stopped doing things for the lesser races," it says, in a voice like a door that hasn't opened in an age. Mika — seventeen days a king, offended before he's thought better of it — snaps back: "I am no lesser race. I am an elf."

The giant looks at him properly for the first time. Something shifts behind eyes built on a scale nothing mortal was meant to hold. "So you are." A long pause, weighing something older than the mountains around them. "Long ago, your people stood by and chose not to involve themselves in the lives of others. We have since learned the lesson we should have taken from you then." It steps aside, and the road opens — a scar in the stone leading down, older than any current border, older than the mountains pretending to be natural. Then its gaze moves, and settles on Brannock, and there is no warmth in it now, only weariness. "I see no reason to stop you here. That requires more care than I have." Back to Mika, or to all of them, or to no one: "Do what you must inside. But don't say I didn't warn you. The one who lives there may be old, and he may have lost his armies — but he is far from weak, and you would be fools to mistake his ruin for weakness."

It turns back to whatever it was doing before they arrived, which is nothing at all, and does not watch them go.

## Scene 2 — The Holds: the investigation, the fight, and the escape
**Where it fits:** Book 1, immediately after the giant lets them pass. The company arrives at the dwarven holds to secure aid and uncovers an embezzlement — the treasurer Koth, who is in fact a changeling. This is the reader's first confirmed sighting of a changeling in the entire trilogy.

**2a — The investigation.** Varr does not know what's killing his caravans, and says so plainly — which frightens the company more than a name would have. Drivers found scattered, not looted; cargo sometimes left untouched where a bandit would have stripped it bare; the rare survivor half-mad and unable, or unwilling, to describe what he saw before he ran. "I have buried men who couldn't tell me what killed their fellows," Varr says. "I don't fear what I can't name. I fear that none of my scouts who got close enough to name it have come back to." The giant's warning sits heavier for it. Mika doesn't ask what "lost his armies" meant. He doesn't want to know yet.

While the aid terms are drawn, Brannock — a factor's eye never off a column of numbers, even his own king's — flags a small thing: a shipment of ore recorded delivered to the royal forges that the foreman's separate log shows never arrived. He mentions it as a curiosity, not an accusation. Varr summons the treasury for clarity, and Koth, treasurer of thirty impeccable years, has an answer ready before the question finishes: a reallocation, properly logged in the older secondary ledgers dwarves keep for exactly this kind of accounting. It's clean. It's plausible. It's the kind of answer that ends most inquiries.

But Brannock doesn't like clean answers to dirty questions, and cross-references the secondary ledger Koth named — and its numbers are off too, pointing to a third reallocation. He raises it again. Koth, smoother this time, blames a currency miscalculation from a border trade and produces a scapegoat: a junior clerk who "misfiled" the transaction. The clerk, found, is terrified and useless — he genuinely doesn't remember, or has been quietly moved somewhere he can't be questioned closely. The company spends a wasted day convinced they've found their thief in a frightened boy, before concluding he's exactly what he looks like: someone paid or threatened into taking a fall he doesn't understand.

But the pattern itself starts to nag: every time the trail warms, someone else's careless mistake cools it, and it is never Koth's. Brannock stops trusting the treasury's own books entirely and goes to the source no treasurer can touch — the mine-head weigh-stations, sealed and stamped by foremen who report to a different department and don't know each other well enough to conspire. The numbers there are honest, cross-consistent across three separate stations, and they say something the ledgers never could: real ore, physically extracted and sealed, simply stops existing the moment it reaches one office. Not reallocated. Not sold. Gone between the mountain and the books.

That's undeniable, and it goes to Varr directly. In the counting-hall, with the king present and the mine-head stamps laid on the table, Koth tries his last verbal card — calm, reasonable, redirecting: perhaps the foremen themselves are colluding, falsifying their own seals to cover a theft further down the chain. It's a good lie. It buys him another minute. But three foremen who've never met, working three separate stations, can't produce numbers this consistent by accident or by coordinated fraud — and every one of those honest lines converges on exactly one office. His.

**2b — The fight.** There is nowhere left for a sentence to go. Koth doesn't reach for one. What he reaches for is worse, and the room understands it a half-second too late: a treasurer of thirty years should not know how to do what happens next.

Power comes off him wrong — too much, too fast, humming with the borrowed edge of something that was never his. The nearest guard is dead before Varr can stand. A second falls covering the king, and Koth's strike that follows isn't aimed to kill — it opens Varr's shoulder to the bone and puts him down, screaming.

The strike that catches Varr isn't meant to kill him and never was — not out of mercy. Years of patient work sit inside that king: positions nudged, counselors placed, a puppet built one small manipulation at a time, exactly the kind of leverage that compounds instead of spending itself. A dead Varr is a project finished the wasteful way — the whole slow architecture abandoned, and every hour of it spent again from nothing on whoever inherits the throne. Wounding him and running costs nothing he hasn't already budgeted for. Killing him would cost years.

In the chaos of bodies and blood and a king down, Koth breaks for the door and is gone into the hold's corridors, and every soul in that counting-hall would swear, for the rest of their lives, that they watched a corrupt dwarf with murder in his hands and no business having power like that vanish down a passage they know as well as their own hands.

**2c — The escape and interrogation.** He breaks for the door as the hall erupts, and this time there's no clean head start — Brannock is already shouting the pursuit into motion behind him, boots on stone closing the gap with every stride. They see him take the passage — watch him go, no ambiguity about which way — and lose him only for the few seconds it takes to round the same corner themselves.

What they find on the other side is a corridor with one person in it: a guard, walking it at an ordinary pace, alone.

"Koth," Brannock snaps, closing the distance. "The treasurer — he came through here, we saw him, which way?"

"No one's come through. I've had this stretch since the horn sounded." Said plainly, without the seams showing — not an empty thing failing to fake a reaction, but a competent one giving exactly the reaction the moment calls for.

It doesn't hold up, and everyone in the corridor knows it in the same instant: they watched a man run this way with their own eyes, this is the only body in the passage, and he's telling them it's empty. There's no room in that contradiction for an honest mistake. They press him — how, standing right in the man's path, could he have seen nothing, heard nothing, felt nothing pass — and get nothing back but the same flat denial, offered without a flicker of the strain a lie should cost. There's nowhere to take the questioning that produces an answer, because there isn't one to find. A king is bleeding two corridors behind them and the search has a hundred better places to spend its next minute than a guard who won't break. They leave, furious and unsatisfied, certain something is wrong and unable to say what.

He lets himself smile only once the corridor is his again, and no one is left in it to see it.
