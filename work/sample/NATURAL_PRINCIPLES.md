# Natural prose: principles for the v9 pass (2026-11-08)

> 🌊 Claude's working notes, not canon. Why prose reads as "found" rather than "arranged", boiled down to techniques a draft can be checked against. Drawn from widely discussed craft principles (Hemingway's omission theory, Le Guin on trusting the reader and on unequal rhythm, McCarthy's bare chains of action, Abercrombie on cost and aftermath, Mantel's close third person, the screenwriter's "enter late, leave early", the common rule on coincidence). Principles only: no wording or style is copied from any of them. Patrick's own habits (STYLE_PROFILE) beat any of these where they differ.

**Patrick's brief:** "more choppy and broken… explaining too much, not showing enough… a few too many convenient things only serving the plot… more human and natural, not structured from scripts."

**The one rule over all of them:** change HOW it's written, never WHAT happens.

---

## A. Cut what the reader can already do

**1. Leave out what's known (the iceberg).** If the reader already has a fact from the scene, or can work it out, the sentence that states it weakens the scene. What's left out still presses on the page.
- *Test:* delete the sentence. Is anything lost that the next page doesn't give back? If not, it stays deleted.

**2. Cut the second half of an explained reaction.** Prose that sounds written by a machine shows a reaction, then explains it: "He could have cleared the yard. He didn't want to touch them again." The first half is the human part.
- *Test:* a pair of sentences where the second says what the first already meant.

**3. No "because" for motive.** People don't narrate their reasons to themselves while acting. "Because", "so that", "so", "to make sure" and "meant to" give a motive the reader should infer.
- *Test:* count motive clauses per scene. Keep one only if the motive is a surprise.

**4. Action without stated motive.** Show the act. Let the reason come later, in someone's mouth, or never.
- *Test:* is any act followed straight away by its reason?

**5. No buttons.** Don't let the narrator close a paragraph by summing it up, drawing the moral or giving the ironic line. End on the thing itself, or one beat before it.
- *Test:* read the last sentence of every paragraph. Does it comment, or does it show?

## B. Let the reader close the gap

**6. Implicature over statement.** A remembered line, an object or two things set side by side can carry a conclusion the narrator never states. "He'll write it up, Jon had said" does more than "Jon had known the guard died for nothing."
- *Test:* a sentence that says what something *means*. Could an echo or an object say it instead?

**7. Gaps the reader closes.** Hold back a link and the reader makes it. A conclusion the reader makes counts for more than one they are handed.
- *Test:* for each reveal, could the reader have got there one line earlier with the explanation cut?

**8. Specific over general.** One exact, slightly odd thing (two peach stones on the crane, chalk marks that come back on the wrong doors) beats a summary. It also has to be something this person would notice.
- *Test:* nouns like "things", "everything", "some way", or adjectives standing in for an object.

## C. Get inside without announcing it

**9. Free indirect discourse.** The narration takes on the character's idiom and thinking, without "he thought" or "he realized". Close third person, carried by word choice.
- *Test:* could the sentence come out of Eli's mouth, in his words? If it sounds like a report, rewrite it or cut it.

**10. Interiority as fragments.** A mind under stress doesn't think in full grammatical sentences. "Tutors, reports, orders. Never a face."
- *Test:* any full, balanced sentence about a feeling in the middle of a crisis.

**11. Keep to the point of view.** The narration reports only what this character could perceive or has been told. Another person's inner senses (an elf feeling other elves) reach the page through their words ("she said"), not as narrated fact.
- *Test:* who could know this sentence? If only another character could, put it in their mouth.

## D. Rhythm that isn't equalized

**12. Uneven sentence pressure.** Long driving runs joined by "and", then a dead stop. Equal sentences sound machined. (This is Patrick's clearest habit: run, then drop.)
- *Test:* three sentences in a row of similar length and shape.

**13. Uneven paragraphing.** A one-line paragraph after a dense one. A long paragraph broken where the camera moves, not where the grammar would.
- *Test:* paragraph lengths across a page. Do they vary as much as the action does?

**14. Delete connective tissue.** "Then", "after that", "it was then that", "at last", "so" and bridging summaries are scaffolding. A hard cut trusts the reader to jump.
- *Test:* the first word of each paragraph. How many are transitions?

**15. Abrupt time skips.** Skip time without a bridge. The white space does the work.
- *Test:* any "later that day" or "over the next weeks" sentence whose only job is to move the clock.

## E. Scenes that start late and don't wrap up

**16. Enter late, leave early.** Start a scene after it has begun (mid-line, mid-act) and leave before it resolves. Fill in the setup afterwards, briefly, or not at all.
- *Test:* does the first line come before anything changes? Does the last line come after the last change?

**17. Not every beat needs a point.** Some moments just sit there, unresolved and unexplained. A scene can trail off.
- *Test:* is every scene's last line a turn or a revelation? Then some should be neither.

## F. Talk that doesn't line up

**18. Dialogue that misses.** Real talk has non-answers, interruptions, answers to the wrong question, people talking past each other, and sentences that never finish. Scripted talk is question, answer, question, answer.
- *Test:* what share of questions get a direct answer? Under about two-thirds feels human.

**19. People don't explain their world to each other.** Characters mention their world sideways, while doing something else, and assume the other person knows. An "as you know" speech is scaffolding.
- *Test:* a speech whose main job is to tell the reader a fact the listener didn't need.

## G. Cause, not luck

**20. The coincidence audit.** Luck that hurts the hero is fine; luck that helps the hero is cheating. Every lucky arrival, timely piece of information or fortunate find should become one of:
- **cost** (it comes, but late, or at a price),
- **character choice** (someone made it happen, and you can see their hand), or
- **messier causality** (it was already there, and the story stopped looking past it).
- *Test:* for every helpful event, ask "why now?" If the only answer is "the plot needed it", fix the *how* without changing the *what*.

**21. Cost arrives, then lingers.** Violence and effort leave marks that are still there scenes later: a stiff hand, a scar, a grudge, a missing ration. Nothing resets.
- *Test:* is there an injury, loss or debt that is never mentioned again when it would still matter?

**22. Break the tidy list.** Triplets and balanced lists read as arranged. Stop at two, run to five, or end on the odd item.
- *Test:* any list of exactly three parallel items in the narration.
