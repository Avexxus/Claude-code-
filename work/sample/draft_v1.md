## One: Property

The guard didn't get tired, and that was the problem.

Eli did. It started in his thighs, a hot, stupid ache he had never earned on any training floor, and climbed into his chest. He had been raised to fight standing still: duelling rings, sparring masters who bowed before they hit him, tutors with slates marking his form. In thirty-one years nobody had taught him to run. Nobody had imagined he would need to.

Behind him the guard's boots struck the slag in a rhythm that never broke. The rig on the man's back hummed, a ribbed hump of grown flesh in a steel cage, and every few strides it gave a wet little click, like a throat swallowing. Eli knew the model. The palace guard wore better.

"Stop!" The voice came out of a speaker at the guard's collar, flattened and huge. "By imperial warrant! Return what you took!"

*What you took.* Eli nearly laughed, and that cost him a breath he couldn't spare.

The city ended here, and it ended badly. Behind him the towers stood pale and ribbed out of the bedrock, grown rather than built, the way every proper city's towers were grown now, seamless as bone. Ahead, past a fence of plain electric wire, lay the yards: slag flats, cranes, long open sheds, and the pens.

He went over the fence. It bit him, a dumb jolt with no mind behind it, and he landed badly and kept going.

The pens were full. They were always full. Orcs stood in rows under the shed roofs, thousands of them, grey-green and heavy-shouldered and bare to the waist, waiting their turn on the ramps that ran up into the hull-presses. The yard grew ship hulls, and you grew a hull out of something. Each orc had a neat pale scar at the base of the skull where the chip went in at birth. *Care*, the program was called. Every orc in the Empire was cared for.

At the fence the guard didn't bother climbing. He lifted a hand-slate and spoke to it.

Every orc in the yard turned its head at the same moment.

They came out of the pens in a single slow wave, stepping over the rails, over each other, hands open and reaching. Their bodies came. Their eyes didn't agree with their bodies. Eli saw an old one near the front with a split lip, mouthing something over and over, and his legs walked him forward anyway.

Eli reached.

His tutors had never had a better word for it. You reached, and the world was a thing you could take hold of, and he had been born able to take hold of a great deal of it. The air in front of him went hard as a wall and he swung it, and a whole front rank of orcs went sideways into the next one like stalks under a scythe. He felt bones go through his grip the way you feel a dropped tray through a floor.

More came. They came over the ones he'd dropped. He could sweep the yard clean if he wanted to: there was enough in him for that, there always had been. Something in him balked. Later he would try to decide what. It wasn't goodness. It was closer to the feeling of being asked to eat with his hands.

"That's a lot of orcs," someone said.

A man was sitting on the counterweight of the nearest crane, twenty feet up, eating a peach. He wore a long coat far too warm for the weather, and he had the kind of face that looks pleased to see you before it knows who you are.

"You look," the man said, "like you could use an exit."

He dropped off the crane, landed lightly, and walked through the advancing orcs the way you'd walk through tall grass. Not one of them touched him. He arrived at Eli's side, still chewing, and laid a warm hand on Eli's shoulder.

"Breathe," he said. "You're doing beautifully."

Every orc in the yard fell down.

They didn't stumble. They dropped, all of them, in every pen and on every ramp, as if one string ran through the lot of them and somebody had cut it. The ones on the ramps slid. A crane went on turning overhead, carrying nothing. The yard was suddenly so quiet Eli could hear the peach.

"Kill switch," the man said. "Every chip has one. Terrible design. Somebody should write a letter."

Eli looked at him. The man looked back, friendly, chewing.

By the fence the guard had stopped. His rig still clicked. He was backing away now, slate held out in front of him like a holy symbol, talking fast into his collar.

"He's seen your face," the man said gently. "He'll file a report. The report goes up, and then it goes up again, and it doesn't stop going up until it reaches whoever you're running from." The hand on Eli's shoulder didn't move. "It's your call. I'm not going to make it for you."

Eli had ordered men flogged. He had watched a steward's hands broken for theft, at sixteen, because his tutors said a prince should see what his word cost. He had never done it himself.

He reached across forty yards of slag and closed his grip around the guard's throat, and kept it closed. It took longer than he'd thought. The guard's heels drummed. When it was done the body sat down against the fence as if it were tired, and the rig on its back went on clicking, patient and wet, because nobody had told it to stop.

"There," said the man in the coat. "Was that so hard?" He squeezed Eli's shoulder once and let go. "What did you steal, by the way? I've been dying to know."

Eli wiped his mouth. His hand was shaking, and he put it in his pocket.

"Me," he said.

The man stared at him, and then he laughed, a big, delighted, helpless laugh that bent him over. "Oh, that's *good*," he said. "Oh, I like you."

A flyer came in low over the sheds, an old plain-engine thing with a cracked canopy, and set down hard on the slag. Teo leaned out of it. He had fled the palace with Eli nine days ago, carrying nothing but a stolen access code and the conviction that this was the best idea anyone had ever had. He looked at the field of orcs, and then at the guard against the fence, and then at the stranger.

"Did you do all that?" he asked Eli.

"No," said Eli.

"Mostly no," said the stranger. He offered Teo the peach stone, saw Teo's face, and pocketed it instead. "Jon. I'm a friend of the family."

"Which family?" said Eli.

Jon climbed into the back of the flyer as if he'd been invited. "That," he said, settling in, "is a long story with a very bad ending. Is there anything to eat in here?"

He rode with them for six days. He told terrible jokes and good ones, and seemed to know which back roads the drones didn't watch, and slept like a child. On the seventh morning he climbed out at a fuel stop in the middle of nothing and stretched.

"I have to go and see some elves about a thing," he said.

"When will you be back?" said Eli, and hated how it sounded.

Jon smiled at him, and it was a wonderful smile, and it was gone before he'd finished turning away.

"When it gets interesting," he said.

## Two: The Master and the Healer

Over the next eight months Jon came and went like weather.

He was there for a week on a water world where Eli and Teo crewed a fishing hull for cash, gutting things with too many fins. He was gone for the whole of the winter after. He reappeared on a transit platform with a bag of oranges and a black eye he refused to explain, and taught Eli to cheat at cards, and vanished again. He never said where he went. When Eli asked, he said "elves," in the tone other men used for "the dentist."

Eli learned things in those months that the palace had never taught him. He learned what food cost. He learned that the Emperor's face was on nothing, because the Emperor had no face anyone knew, and that every second shopfront still kept a little shrine to the Emperor's god, lit with a cheap bulb. He learned that people talked about Remnant the way they talked about fuel: you mined it, you sold it, it went in the clever machines and made them cleverer. *Battery's going*, a dockhand said once, kicking a cargo loader that had begun to hesitate, and spat. Nobody seemed to wonder what was in it. Eli had never wondered either.

He also learned that he was dangerous and didn't know how to be. He had been taught to push, hard and clean, the way a prince was taught to push. He could break a wall from across a room. He could not tell, with his eyes shut, where the wall was.

So he went looking for a master.

He found one at a rift.

The rift was on a world nobody would have bothered with otherwise: a grey, cold place, too far from anything. But there was a wound in the ground there, three miles across, where the rock was glassy and the air tasted like a struck bell, and Remnant came up out of it the way oil comes up out of a bad well. So there was a city on its rim, and the city had been at war for longer than anyone in it had been alive. Five companies held wells on the rim. Two empires had fought over it, then a third. Somebody had built a temple on the near edge and somebody else had shelled it, and somebody had rebuilt it.

Soldiers came off the transports in amplifier rigs: steel cages over grown-flesh humps full of pooled Remnant, the kind that let an ordinary man reach three times as far as he had any right to. Eli watched one burn out on his second day. The boy was perhaps nineteen and holding a line on the north wells, and he pushed through his rig for twenty seconds longer than his body could carry. He didn't explode. He just went dark inside, like a house with the lights going room by room, and sat down in the mud with blood running out of his ears and eyes. His rig was pulled off him before he was cold and fitted to the next boy.

"Burnout," said the woman who sold Eli his noodles that night. "That's the soldier's death. Rig doesn't care. Rig's fine." She tapped her own temple. "It's the meat that goes."

The master lived in the shelled temple. He was old, brown and spare, and he had trained rig soldiers for every company on the rim at one time or another. He looked at Eli for a long moment when Eli came up the broken steps, and then he looked at Eli's hands.

"Who taught you?" he said.

Eli told him: the best tutors in the Empire, three of them, a duelling master from the dragon schools.

"They taught you to shout," said the master. "Sit down."

He kept Eli for four months. He never once let him push. He made him sit blindfolded in the temple and find a single moth in the room, and when Eli couldn't, he made him sit there until he could. He made him lift a cup of water without spilling it, and then lift the water without the cup. He said Eli was what the old masters called a numb titan, enough strength for an army and the touch of a man in mittens, and that it would kill him one day if he didn't fix it. The second sense, the one nobody could explain, the one you felt the world with: Eli had it the way everyone had it, and had never been asked to use it.

"You'll never be fine," the master said in the third month, watching Eli thread a needle across the room without touching it. "You started too late. But you'll be less likely to kill your friends by accident."

At the end of the fourth month Eli asked him to come with them.

The master laughed at him. Eli told him who he was, and showed him the warrant Teo had pulled off a guard post: RECOVER IMPERIAL PROPERTY, it said, under the seal, and gave a description of him down to the scar on his chin.

The master read it twice. Then he went to the temple door and looked out over the rift for a long time, at the glassy ground and the wells and the far fires of the company lines.

"I have wanted to see the inside of that Tower," he said at last, "since I was younger than you are." He handed the warrant back. "All right. I'll come and watch you die somewhere better than this."

Jon turned up two days later and was charming to the master for an hour. The master was polite and gave him nothing. Afterwards, when Jon had gone to find something to eat, the old man stood beside Eli for a while without speaking.

"Where did you find him?" he asked.

"He found me."

"Yes," said the master. "I thought he might have."

---

They needed a healer. Teo found one, in a dwarven mining district on a world called by a number. The district had a gnome quarter, and the gnome quarter had a healer.

Gnomes were what dwarves bought. That was the plain way of saying it. In the dwarven districts they were indentured, sold on contracts that ran for life, and they did the work dwarves didn't want, and they were known for being cheerful about it, which made everyone a little uneasy. The healer was the cheeriest of the lot. He was small even for a gnome, round in the face and bald on top, with enormous ears and a laugh you could hear across the market. His contract was held by the district council. People came to him from three valleys.

They had to wait for him. There was a line.

Eli stood at the back of the clinic and watched a woman come in carrying a boy of about six. The boy was grey and sweating and his breath rattled. The woman's face had the particular stillness of someone who'd stopped letting herself hope three days ago and was doing it anyway.

The gnome took the boy from her as gently as anything Eli had ever seen. He chatted to him, nonsense, a story about a goat. He carried him through a curtain at the back of the room.

He was gone a long time. Teo shifted his weight. Out in the market someone was selling clone kidneys off a cart with a hand-painted sign that said *REAL*.

When the gnome came back out through the curtain, he came alone, and his round face was soft with sorrow. He took both the woman's hands.

"I'm so sorry," he said. "I wasn't able to save your loved one."

She thanked him. That was the part Eli would remember. She held his little hands in hers and thanked him for trying, over and over, and he patted her back and said the boy hadn't felt any pain at the end, none at all, he'd made sure of that.

When she'd gone, the gnome washed his hands in a basin and turned to the next in line, and saw Eli and Teo, and his face lit up like a lamp.

"Oh, *strangers*," he said. "Thank goodness. Everybody here is dying. Are you dying?"

"No," said Eli.

"Pity. I'm very good at it." He dried his hands. "What do you want?"

Eli told him they were travelling a long way and needed someone who could set a bone and close a wound. The gnome listened with his head on one side.

"You'll have to buy me," he said. "The council holds my paper. I'm worth a great deal; I'm much loved." He thought about it. "I'll get you a discount. I know where the councillor's son buries his debts."

They bought his contract for less than the price of the flyer. The gnome signed his own transfer with a flourish and tucked the paper inside his shirt.

"What do we call you?" Teo asked him.

"Oh, anything. Everyone does." He beamed. "The children here call me Doc. It's not a name. It's a job description. I've always liked that."

On the way out of the district, he stopped at a troll's toll gate on the river bridge, a squat grumbling creature no taller than his own shoulder, and paid its toll for all three of them with a joke so filthy the troll laughed until it had to sit down.

"Never let it be said," said Doc, climbing into the flyer, "that I don't bring a little light into the world."

## Three: The Statue

Jon took them to the elves because, he said, the elves were the only people in the galaxy who could still build a city that made you want to cry.

It was true. The city stood on a world of blue grass and high cold rivers, and it had been grown the way the giants' cities had been grown in the old days, whole and seamless, but finer. Bridges like spun sugar. Towers that seemed to lean toward each other in conversation. The light came down through them in colours.

The elves at the gate looked at Eli and Teo the way you look at something on the bottom of a shoe.

It took three hours to get in. Papers. A search. An inquisitor in grey with a face like a closed book, who asked Eli the same eleven questions three times in the same order and watched his eyes when he answered. At the end Jon said something to the inquisitor in their language, quietly, and showed him a token on a chain, and the inquisitor looked at the token for a long moment and stamped the papers with something close to hatred.

"What did you show him?" Eli asked, inside.

"A favour," said Jon. "I helped the king once, when he was a boy. It doesn't buy much. It buys a day pass." He smiled. "Don't steal anything. They'll take your hands, and they won't be nice about it."

He was joking, Eli thought. On the second day they walked through a square where an elf was kneeling on the stones with his hands bound behind him and his mouth sewn shut with silver wire, and people walked past him without looking, and Eli stopped thinking Jon had been joking.

"What did he do?" Teo whispered.

"Disagreed," said Jon. "Out loud. Come on, there's a place near here that does a fish thing you won't believe."

The elves were one people in a way Eli had never seen anything be. They didn't jostle in the streets. They didn't need to speak to pass in a doorway. Once, at a market, a child fell and every elf within fifty yards turned its head before the child had finished falling. They moved, Doc said, like a shoal. He said it admiringly. He said it the way a man admires a well-stocked larder.

The king's name was everywhere and his face was nowhere. Mika. On the arches, on the coins, in the mouths of the street-sellers, said quietly. Every order in the city came from him, Jon said, and every elf in the city carried it out, and none of them would have dreamed of asking why.

"Do you know him well?" Eli asked.

"No one knows Mika well," said Jon. "He's very much in charge." He said it lightly, and changed the subject.

They were told about the dark elves in the city's great hall of records by a young archivist who had been ordered to be helpful and was trying to manage it. The dark elves were the worst thing in the galaxy, she said. They were born in the prison realm, the place of darkness and famine that held the soul thieves and the traitors, and they came out of it full of hate. They murdered elves. They sold themselves to humans. They were not, she said carefully, elves at all any more, not truly, not in the way that mattered. The Inquisition hunted them across the stars. It was a holy work.

"Have you ever met one?" Eli asked.

She looked at him as if he'd asked whether she'd ever licked a sewer.

On their last afternoon they crossed a long square with a statue at its centre.

It was a woman, elven, cut in white stone at about twice life size. She stood with one hand raised, the fingers slightly curled, as if she were about to close them on something only she could see. Her face was young and grave. There were flowers at her feet, hundreds of them, fresh, and elves standing in a loose ring around her in silence, some with their eyes closed.

"Lirael," said Jon. "The hero of her people. She was the king's sister. A long time ago." He said it like a man reading a plaque.

At the statue's foot an old elven woman was weeping. She did it without sound, the tears simply running, her hands folded. Teo, who was kind in the way of someone who has never been made to pay for it, crouched down beside her and asked in his bad elvish if she was all right, if she'd lost someone.

"Yes," she said.

"Who?"

She looked at him with her wet, ancient eyes.

"I don't know," she said. "I never know. None of us do." She turned back to the statue. "We come here because it seems the place to bring it."

The others walked on. Eli turned to say something to Jon and found that Jon had not moved.

He was standing in front of the statue with his hands in his coat pockets, looking up at the stone face. His expression was perfectly pleasant. There was nothing in it at all. He stood there for a moment, and then for a moment more, and it went on just a little too long, long enough that the old woman glanced up at him, long enough that Eli started to open his mouth.

Then Jon turned around, smiling.

"Fish thing," he said. "I promised. Come on."

## Four: The Weak Place

Two months later Jon gave them a set of coordinates and said, "You'll want to be there on the ninth. Don't be late, it's rude."

"What's there?"

"An opportunity," said Jon, and left again.

The coordinates led to the edge of elven space, to a dead moon where the elves kept something they did not talk about. There was a place on the moon, in a crater with a black floor, where the world was thinner. Human freighters dropped through near there, into the prison realm, to come out half a galaxy away; that was how human ships crossed, through the elves' hell, because it was faster than anything else and because nobody had ever made the elves stop them. The elves crossed their own way. They squeezed down one of the world's dimensions and slipped through the gap, and while they were in it they were cut off from each other, every one of them alone. Doc said elves hated it more than dying.

On the near side of the crater the Inquisition had a station. On the far side, a dwarven ship sat on the black floor with its ramp down.

They watched from the crater rim for a day. On the evening of the ninth, inquisitors in grey walked a line of chained figures across the black floor from their station to the dwarven ship. Dark elves. Eli could tell by their skin, ash-grey, and by the way they walked, like people who had walked a long way and didn't expect it to end. At the ramp a dwarf in a good coat counted them, and an inquisitor counted the money, and the chained figures went up the ramp one by one into the dark.

"They're *selling* them," said Teo.

"Of course they are," said Doc. "Who did you think was buying? Rich men like pets that can fight." He was eating a sweet he'd found somewhere. "Hunting soul thieves. It's a lovely cover. I'd use it."

"The archivist said—"

"The archivist," said the master, "believes what she's told. Most people do." He was looking past the ship, at the place where the crater floor went strange, the air above it bending like heat over a road. "Something's coming through."

It came through badly.

The air over the black floor split like wet paper, and a woman fell out of it onto her hands and knees, and the air closed behind her with a sound like a cough. She was dark-skinned the way the chained ones were, ash and slate, and thin past thin, and she had a short curved knife in each hand. Her clothes were rags tied over rags. She came up off the ground already moving, and she didn't run away from the inquisitors. She ran at them.

She killed two before they had their weapons up. Eli saw it from the rim and didn't understand it until afterwards: she didn't push the way he pushed. She went in close and *touched* them, a palm on a chest, fingertips on a throat, and they folded, as neat as paper.

Then there were nine more, and the dwarven ship's guns came round, and she was alone.

Eli went down the crater wall without deciding to.

It was the first time he had used what the master taught him. He didn't sweep. He felt the guns on the dwarven ship, all six of them, the way he'd felt the moth in the temple, and closed his grip on each barrel and bent it, gently, like bending wire. He felt the inquisitors' weapons. He felt the woman, small and fast and furious at the centre of it all, and he put a wall of hard air around her back just as an inquisitor's blade came at it.

She turned and looked straight at him across the black floor. Her eyes were silver.

*Oh*, thought Eli, and was immediately ashamed of thinking it, and thought it again anyway.

"He's thinking it," Doc said loudly behind him to no one, scrambling down the rocks. "You can see it on his face. He's thinking *oh*."

The master killed four of the inquisitors without seeming to move. Teo shot the dwarf in the good coat in the leg, then stood over him, horrified, saying "Sorry! Sorry!" The woman killed the rest. When it was done she stood in the middle of the bodies with her knives out, breathing hard, and looked at the four of them.

"Who sent you?" she said. Her voice was rough. It sounded unused.

"No one," said Eli. "A friend gave us a date."

She laughed. It wasn't a nice sound. "No one escapes alone," she said. "That's what they say, down there. No one escapes alone." She looked back at the place in the air she'd come through, closed and quiet now. "There were thirty of us, when we started."

"How long ago?" asked Teo.

She looked at him as if the question were a joke in a language she'd forgotten.

"I was a child," she said.

They opened the dwarven ship and freed the chained ones. Most of them ran into the dark of the moon and were never seen again, and none of them thanked anyone. The woman stayed. She stood on the ramp and watched them go and didn't say goodbye to any of them either.

"What's your name?" Eli asked her.

She looked at him for a long, unfriendly time.

"When you've earned it," she said.

The dwarven ship was a beautiful thing, low and black, made by the Returned, the dwarves who had come back out of the maze-land with new tricks. It could squeeze through the world like an elf could, or almost. They kept it. Doc named it something unrepeatable. The dwarf in the good coat they left on the moon with a medkit and a water ration, which Doc said was an extraordinary kindness and frankly a little showy.

---

It took her four months to stop sleeping with a knife in her hand, and five to let Eli sit on the same side of the galley table.

He had been raised on the same stories as the archivist. Dark elves were monsters; it was one of the few things humans and elves agreed on. He found he had to unlearn it piece by piece, the way you unlearn a limp. She ate fast and silently, with her arm curled around her plate. She couldn't stand closed doors. She couldn't stand being thanked. She laughed at Doc's jokes, the filthy ones, with a rough bark that seemed to surprise her every time. She trained with the master at dawn and they barely spoke and seemed to like each other enormously.

Eli learned that she felt elves. Not at a distance, not across the void; out here among the stars she was alone in her own head, and she said it was like being deaf. But when they put down on a world where elves lived, she would go very still on the ramp, and he would watch her feel them, all of them, hundreds or thousands, pressing on her like weather, and feel them feel her. Then she'd pull her hood up and they'd leave quickly.

One night, very late, they sat in the cockpit with the stars squeezed thin outside, and she told him about Lirael.

"Even down there," she said. "In the dark. Everyone knows her. She was the hero of our people." She said *our* without seeming to notice. "They say she could take hold of a thing and make it fall into itself. The space around it. All of it, inward, at once." She curled the fingers of one hand slowly, like the statue. "Lirael's Collapse. I spent forty years learning it. Everyone down there wants to learn it. It's the one thing they gave us that we kept."

Jon was in the doorway. Eli didn't know how long he'd been there. He had a cup of something in his hand and he was smiling at the two of them the way you smile at children asleep.

"That's a hard one to learn," he said. "Good for you."

Then he went back down the corridor, and they heard him whistling.

She told Eli her name the next morning. He would never write it down anywhere, and nobody else on the ship ever heard her say it.

## Five: The Queen and the Giant

The queen came to them on a desert world at the far end of nowhere, where a human colony had been forgotten for so long it had forgotten everything else, and an elven ship had come down out of the sky into the middle of its oldest myth.

The colonists thought the elves were invaders. The elves thought the colonists were vermin. Both had started killing, slowly, the way frightened people do, and the dead were being stacked in the dunes on both sides of a dry riverbed. Eli's crew put down the stolen ship between the two camps because Jon had told them to, and because Eli could not think what else to do. Then Eli walked out into the riverbed alone, under both sets of guns, and talked.

It took nine days. He was good at it; it turned out he was very good at it. The palace had taught him that much. He found the colony's grandmothers and the elven captain's second-in-command and sat them down on either side of a folding table in the sun, and on the ninth day there was a line drawn in the riverbed that neither side would cross, and nobody had died for three days. It wasn't peace. It was the thing that sometimes comes before it.

On the tenth day the elven commander came down out of her ship to look at him.

She was taller than any elf he'd met, and armoured, and young-looking in the way that meant nothing with elves. Her sword hung at her hip in a sheath of something pale and soft and faintly ribbed, and when she moved, the sheath shifted against her leg like a sleeping animal. She looked at Eli's face, his clothes and his hands, and her mouth curled very slightly.

"You," she said, "are the human my king has instructed me to accompany."

Eli looked at Jon, who was sitting on the ramp of the ship eating something.

"I asked Mika for a favour," said Jon. "He was kind enough to agree. She's the best he has."

"I'm the best anyone has," said the queen. She didn't look at Jon. She was still looking at Eli, as if he were a problem she'd been set and didn't think much of. "I'm to see you safely to wherever you're going. I'm to help you, where helping you serves my king." Her eyes went flat. "No one said I had to like it."

"Do they call you Your Majesty?" asked Doc, delighted.

"They call me what my king calls me," said the queen, and walked up the ramp past all of them, and took the best bunk.

She was a better fighter than Eli in every way that could be measured, and she made sure he knew it. She sparred with him every day and put him on the deck every day, without malice, without effort, and walked away while he was still getting up. She never once called him by his name. She called him *the human*, or, when she was in a good mood, *the prince*. She treated the dark elf like a stain on the bulkhead that she was too well bred to mention, and the dark elf treated her like a door she was waiting to walk through.

"Why her?" Eli asked Jon, one night, when his ribs hurt too much to sleep.

"Because she hates you," said Jon, "and she's better than you. You'll get better faster." He patted Eli's arm. "And she's lovely, really. Underneath."

---

The giant lived on a forge world.

Jon wanted to see him, and so they went. It was a world of black rock and red sky and enormous shipyards, the greatest of the giants' forges, where the cathedral ships were grown. From orbit you could see them in their cradles, a dozen of them, each the length of a city, ribbed and pale and patient, half-made, like the skeletons of whales.

Everyone knew the giants were dying out. Everyone had always known it. They built ships for dragons and sold them and kept to themselves, and there were fewer of them every century, and one day there wouldn't be any. Eli had learned that from the same tutors who had taught him about dark elves.

The giant met them in a hall so large it had weather in it. He was sitting, and sitting he was the height of a four-storey house, and he looked at them with eyes the colour of old ice, and when he spoke Eli felt it in the floor.

"Jon," he said.

"Hello, old friend."

The giant considered him for a long time. "I am not your friend," he said, at last. "But I am friendly. I am, as far as I know, the last of us who is." He looked down at the rest of them. "Sit, little ones. You've come a long way to look at a relic. You may as well hear him talk."

He told them about the golden age. He told it plainly, without pride, the way you'd tell someone the history of a house you once owned. How the giants had written every law and kept every peace, and how the other races had lived under their protection whether they wanted it or not, and how the dragons, their seconds, their most trusted, had decided they would rule better. He told them about betrayal day: how the emperor's closest advisor, a dragon, had walked into the heart of the capital and destroyed it with a single breath, and died doing it, and how in the last moment of his life the emperor had taken his entire city, every body and every soul in it, and shaped it into a shield.

"And what was left," said the giant, "is the Tower. Your father's house." He looked at Eli. "Yes, little prince. I know whose son you are. Everyone at my age knows everything eventually. It's a curse."

Eli said nothing. Behind him he heard the queen go very still.

"It's not a building," the giant said. "People live in it as if it were a building. It's a city that converged, and everyone in it went into the walls, and they are still in the walls, all of them, all the souls of that day, run together. They are not themselves any more. There is no one left in there to be anyone. But it is the greatest store of power on this earth, and whoever sits at its heart has all of it at his elbow." He smiled, slow and enormous. "Which is why your father so rarely leaves home."

"I saw it come up," said Doc.

Everyone looked at him. He was sitting on the giant's footstool, which for him was a table, swinging his feet. For once he wasn't smiling.

"The morning the capital burned," he said. "A dragon told me it was a lovely day for a ride out of the city. So I rode. I looked back, at the top of the ridge." He was quiet for a moment. "The cloud first. Then, out of the cloud, the Tower. I remember thinking it looked like a pearl. Isn't that a stupid thing to think?"

"That," said the queen, "was before the dragons ruled. That was *ages* ago."

"Was it?" said Doc, brightening again. "Doesn't time fly."

The giant looked at him for a long, strange moment, and something passed over his ancient face that might have been recognition and might have been disgust. Then it was gone, and he laughed, and the hall shook.

"Go home, little ones," he said. "Whatever you are."

---

## Six: The Strike

The dragon knew his crew would die.

He did not tell them. There was no reason to. They were good crew, wingless kin, two-legged and scaled and quick, and they had served him for eighty years, and they called themselves dragons the way he did. If you had asked them, they would have said there had never been any other kind. They were strapped into their stations in the focusing chamber now, in the long curved bones of the ship's heart, and they were nervous, because they had never seen him come down here himself.

"Business as usual," he told them. "A demonstration. Aim for the forge-heart. On my mark."

The chamber was a giant's ribcage once, and still curved like one. At its centre sat the cradle where a dragon could pour every scrap of his strength into one place, and the ship would gather it and throw it across the dark in a single line. Crews took turns; a dragon could only manage a few shots before he was empty for a day. The ship's master did not take turns. The ship's master did not do this at all. That was what crews were for.

The dragon climbed into the cradle and began to strap himself in.

"Master," his first officer said. "The chamber is not rated—"

"Then it will learn," said the dragon. "Incompetents. Every last one of you. If you want a thing done—" He pulled the last strap tight with his teeth. "Aim."

Somewhere very far away, in a building no one he knew would ever enter, a contract had been signed with a company that had been owned for longer than he had been alive by someone he had never met. He didn't think about it. He thought about the giants, and about his grandfather's grandfather, who had died on betrayal day, and about all the things he was owed.

He opened his mouth, and everything in him went out of it.

---

From the planet's surface it looked like a line drawn with a ruler. It went in at the forge-heart and the forge-heart was gone. Then the shipyards. Then the cradles, and the cathedral ships in them, one after another, folding like paper boats. Then the crust around the forge-heart came up in a ring and kept coming, a wave of red rock taller than mountains rolling outward across the face of the world.

The dragon's ship drifted dark in orbit afterwards, and nothing moved on it. Its crew sat in their stations, every one of them, cooked inside their scales. Its master hung in the cradle with his eyes open and his jaw unhinged. He had known he wouldn't survive it. He had done it anyway.

Eli watched it from the bridge of the stolen ship, two hundred thousand miles out, where Jon had told them to wait.

Nobody spoke. The dark elf's hand was in his. The queen stood with her arms folded, absolutely still. Teo was crying and didn't seem to know it. Doc watched the wave of rock go round the world with his chin in his hands, the way a child watches a firework.

"The giant," said Teo. "He was down there."

"No," said Jon.

He was standing at the back of the bridge. He'd been there a while. He had a cup of tea and he hadn't drunk any of it.

"He left last night," he said. "He said he had some calls to make."

Eli turned to look at him. "You knew."

"I guessed." Jon set the tea down. "Somebody wanted that very much. Somebody asked very nicely, through a great many people, and it happened." He looked out at the burning world. "Well. Now everyone gets to stop pretending."

Within three days, the last friendly giant was broadcasting on every channel in the sector. He didn't look friendly any more. He didn't look anything. Behind him, out of shipyards on worlds nobody had known the giants still held, came fleets. Not a dozen cathedral ships: hundreds. Everyone had known the giants were dying out. Everyone had been wrong.

"The dragons made a mistake," the giant said, to the whole galaxy, in a voice like stone settling. "And so did those who stood behind them." He looked out of the screen, and it seemed to Eli that he was looking straight at him. "We have not forgotten what man did on betrayal day. We have only been patient."

Then the screen went dark, and the war began.

---

It took them seven months to cross it.

There were not words, afterwards, for most of what they saw. Cathedral ships the size of moons, grown from giants' dead, grinding into dragon fleets in the dark. Human Legion carriers whose hulls had been pressed from orcs, bought by the trillion and shipped in by the hold-load, so that you could not be sure, walking a Legion corridor, whether the faint warmth in the walls was the heating. Dragons in their focusing chambers, scorching lines across whole battle-groups. Dwarven merchant convoys running guns to everyone. Elven ships coming out of their squeezed nowhere to strike and vanish, sworn to kill the human Emperor, ignoring everyone else.

They lost the stolen ship over a gas giant whose name none of them ever learned. They crossed two systems in the hold of a dwarven arms freighter, sitting on crates of flesh rifles that twitched faintly when the drive spun up. They went through hell once, on a human transit hull, and the dark elf sat with her back against the bulkhead the whole way with her eyes shut, holding Eli's hand so hard she broke two of his fingers, and afterwards neither of them mentioned it. Doc set the fingers. He told jokes while he did it.

Somewhere in the second month, Jon stopped leaving.

He didn't say anything about it. He was simply there, every morning, making terrible breakfasts. He fought beside them, when they had to fight, and he was so much better than any of them that it was hard to watch. He never seemed to try. He killed with a hand on someone's shoulder and a kind word in their ear, or with a glance over his own shoulder at a thing behind him that then stopped existing. He sparred with the queen once, and she lasted a little longer than Eli did against her, and she walked away afterwards without a word and was very quiet for a day.

Once, in the hold of the arms freighter, a cargo of chipped orcs being shipped to the Legion yards died in their crates when the freighter took a hit and the cold got in. Teo found them. He came back white and couldn't speak. Jon went and looked and came back and sat down to his dinner.

"Shame," he said. "Pass the salt?"

The queen looked at him with something Eli had never seen on her face before. Eli couldn't name it. Later he thought it might have been recognition.

---

On the last night before Earth, they sat in the dark of a borrowed lander's cabin and Eli asked the question.

"When we get there," he said. "To him. What do we do?"

Jon looked up. He'd been mending his coat, badly, with an enormous needle.

"We tire him out," he said.

"We don't kill him?"

Jon smiled. "You can't kill your father, Eli. Not that one. People have tried." He bit off the thread. "No. We go in, and everyone does the very best thing they can do, the best thing they've ever done, and he has to deal with every one of them. And at the end of it he's tired. And then I sit him down and we have a talk." He held the coat up and looked at it critically. "That's all. That's the whole plan."

"And then what?" said the master, from the dark.

"Then," said Jon, "we'll see." He shrugged into the coat. "This is the last time I do this, you know. I'm getting too old for it."

He said it lightly. Doc laughed. Nobody else did.

---

## Seven: The Tower

Earth was one city, edge to edge, and it was on fire.

The Tower stood at the heart of it, pearl-white and impossible, so tall its top was lost, and the war broke around its base like surf around a rock. Giant ships hung in the upper air like weather. Dragons burned lines through the clouds. The Legion's guns fired from every rooftop in the city and the sky above the capital was a single churning ceiling of fire and smoke and falling things.

Jon flew them in himself. He didn't use the lander. He stood at its open door with the wind tearing at his coat and simply stepped out, and the rest of them came with him, as if falling had been told to make an exception for them. They fell through a mile of war. Eli saw a cathedral ship break in half directly overhead, its giant ribs coming apart like a cage, and saw Jon move one hand slightly, and the falling wreckage went past them on both sides, a foot away, close enough to touch. Jon was laughing. Eli would remember that. The whole way down, he was laughing like a man on a fairground ride.

They landed on a terrace two-thirds of the way up the Tower, where a door stood open as if they were expected.

They went in.

The silence came down on them like a lid.

One step inside the door, and the war was gone. Not muffled. Gone. No guns, no wind, no shaking of the floor. Eli turned and looked back through the open door and could see the sky burning outside, ships breaking, fire falling, all of it in perfect silence, like a painting of a war.

"Oh," said Doc softly. "Oh, that's clever."

They were in a corridor. It was a corridor of the Tower, pale and seamless, curving gently. And it was something else as well. The light was wrong. It came from nowhere. The air didn't move when you breathed it.

"We're not where we were," said the master.

The corridor went on. There were rooms along it, and each room was a puzzle, and none of them looked like a puzzle until you were in it. A room where the floor was a long way down and also right under your feet. A room with a door on every wall, where every door opened into the same room again. A room full of chairs, where you could not leave until everyone was sitting, and there was always one fewer chair than people, and when you took a chair away there were still one fewer.

The master solved them.

He did it quietly, the way he'd done everything in the temple at the rift. He sat with his eyes shut in each room and felt it out, the shape of whatever had made it, and then he'd stand up and say, "Here," or, "Like this," or, "Not that one, that one." He was enjoying himself. Eli could see it. It was the first time Eli had ever seen him look young.

Something was being done with Eli's own strength, in that place. He noticed it in the third room. When he reached, he reached further than he ever had: much further, with a hold so fine he could have counted the threads in Teo's sleeve from across the room. He looked at his hands. They were the same hands.

"It's the Tower," said Doc, watching him. "It's all around us. All those people in the walls." He patted the pale stone fondly, like a dog. "Doesn't it feel lovely? Like standing on the shoulders of a whole city." He grinned. "Everyone's going to be at their very best in here. I can't wait to see."

At the end of the corridor there was a last room, empty and round and white, and there was a door on the far side of it that would not open. And in the middle of the room, something sat that hadn't been there a moment ago.

It was hard to look at. It was a little like a man and a little like a spider and a little like a hole. It had too many joints in the wrong order. It was sitting in the middle of the floor as if it had been sitting there forever, which, Eli would realise afterwards, it more or less had.

"Visitors," it said, in a voice like a door creaking open in an empty house. "Oh, *visitors*. It's been so long." It unfolded, slightly. "He put me here, you know. The man upstairs. I came for him once. I was young. I built him a room just like this one and I put him in it, and he walked in and smiled at me and told me he'd had this conversation thousands of times already, and that he'd got very good at it." It made a sound that might have been laughter. "He had, too. So now I'm the one in the room. Until a new foe comes in and loses." It turned its heads toward them. "Which of you is it to be?"

"What are the rules?" said the master.

"One goes in," said the beast. "The rest wait. The one who goes in plays my game, and loses, and then I am free, and the door upstairs opens, and you can all go and see him. If you like."

"And if no one goes in?"

"Then you stay here," said the beast pleasantly, "forever. Like me."

The master nodded slowly. He didn't look at any of them.

"I'll go," he said.

"Master—" said Eli.

"Be quiet." The old man was looking at the beast. "Tell me something first. Your room. Nothing leaves it?"

"Nothing."

"No light. No heat. Nothing at all."

"Nothing," said the beast, with enormous contempt. "It isn't even attached to your universe any more. It's mine."

"Swear it," said the master. "Do you swear this room will not allow any energy to leave?"

The beast laughed at him. "I swear it."

"Again."

"I *swear* it, old man. Nothing gets out. Not a spark. That's the point."

"Once more," said the master.

The beast stopped laughing. It looked at him for a long moment with whatever it had for eyes.

"I swear it," it said. "Nothing leaves this room."

"Good," said the master. He let out a long breath, and the lines in his face seemed to ease. Then he turned around, finally, and looked at Eli.

"You'll never be fine," he said. "But you're better than you were."

He walked into the middle of the round white room, and the beast unfolded around him, and the wall of the room closed between them and the rest.

It was not a wall exactly. It was more like a pane of something milky, and they could see through it, and they could hear nothing at all. They saw the beast set its game. They saw the master sit down on the floor, cross-legged, the way he'd sat in the temple every morning. They saw him close his eyes.

Eli understood what he was doing a moment before it happened, and could not stop it, and would not have been allowed to.

The master took hold of himself.

Not of the room, not of the beast: of his own body, every part of it at once, and he began to pull it in. Eli saw the strain go through him. His shoulders came in. His hands curled. His face changed, the skin drawing toward a point somewhere just in front of his breastbone, slowly, and then not slowly. He was conscious. Eli saw his eyes open once, and they were still the master's eyes, terribly clear. His mouth moved. Eli would never know what he said. He liked to think it was something rude.

Then the pane began to darken from the middle outward.

It took Eli a moment to understand why. The light in that room was falling. Whatever the master had made of himself, at the centre of it, was pulling the light in before it could reach them, so that the first thing to go was the sight of him. They saw the edges last: the floor peeling up in long pale curls and sliding inward; the beast, at the rim of the dark, stretching, its too-many limbs reaching back for the walls of its own room, which it had sworn three times would let nothing out. Its mouths were open. Then the dark closed over that too, and nothing left that room, not a sound, not a spark, not the smallest glimpse of what it was like to be inside.

The milky pane went black.

When it cleared, the round white room was empty, and the door on the far side stood open. The floor where the master had sat was perfectly smooth. There was nothing on it. Not a mark.

The queen was the first to move. She went to the open door and stood beside it, and when Eli finally came past her, she said, very quietly, without looking at him: "That was well done. Your master." It was the first thing she had ever said to him that wasn't an insult. He didn't answer. He couldn't.

---

The stair beyond the door wound up through the heart of the Tower, and there were things along it. Rooms, half seen through arches. A room full of ancient weapons made of what looked like flesh. A room full of glass jars. A hall hung with banners from wars that had never been written down.

In one alcove, on a plinth of pale stone, there was a cage.

It was a small cage, beautifully made, gold wire as fine as hair. Inside it, in a crystal vase, there was a single white rose. Its stem was green. Its leaves were fresh. It was in full bloom. It was missing one petal.

Jon stopped.

He looked at the rose for a moment, with his head on one side, as if it were an old painting he was fond of.

"Hm," he said. "This time it seems more full."

Teo was beside him. Teo, who always asked. "This time?" he said. "You've been here before?"

Jon smiled at him and walked on up the stair.

"Not for a long time," he said.

---

The throne room was enormous and almost empty.

It was the top of the Tower, or near enough. The ceiling was lost in shadow. The floor was pale and seamless and went on for a long way. Along one side ran a great curved window, the whole height of the room, and through it you could see the war: silent now, like the war outside the door below, the sky over Earth a slow wheel of fire. At the far end of the room, on a dais, there was a chair.

A man was sitting in it.

He was older than Eli had expected, though he had never let himself expect anything. Lean, grey at the temples, dressed in plain dark clothes, with a face that was intelligent and tired and unremarkable. He was resting his chin on one hand. He looked like a man waiting for a late train.

Eli had never seen his father's face. No one had. He knew it anyway, with a certainty that went through him like cold water.

Beside the throne stood a younger man in black armour, with a pale ribbed sword at his hip, watching them come with an expression of pure, frightened hate.

"Jon," said the man in the chair. "You're late."

"Derrek," said Jon warmly. "You're old."

Teo had gone ahead of them all. He'd always been the first through any door; it was his best and worst habit, and it was what had got the two of them out of the palace in the first place. He was halfway across the floor, staring up at the dais, and he had his mouth open to say something. Something brave, probably. Something he'd been rehearsing for nine months.

"You're the Emp—" Teo began.

"—the Emperor, yes, and you're the boy who forged the access code," said Derrek, at exactly the same moment, in exactly Teo's rhythm, word for word, sounding bored. "It was a very good forgery. I had it framed."

Then he reached out across the length of the room and took Teo by the face.

He didn't get up. He didn't change expression. He simply closed his hand, a little, in the air, and across the room Teo's head jerked up as if a great invisible palm had cupped it, fingers over his eyes and mouth, and lifted him off the floor. Teo kicked. His hands came up and clawed at nothing. There was a sound, short and wet and final, like a man stepping on something in the dark.

Derrek opened his hand. Teo fell. He lay on the pale floor with his face turned away from them, and he didn't move, and he would never move again.

"There," said Derrek. "Now we've all been introduced."

He'd done it the way a mean man puts down a dog that won't stop barking. He had already stopped looking at the body. He was looking at Jon.

Eli heard someone screaming, and it took him a long time to understand it was himself.

---

## Eight: Everyone

Afterwards, Eli would never be able to put it in order.

He remembered the dark elf's hand on his arm, holding him back, so hard her nails broke his skin. He remembered Jon, beside him, saying something kind and quiet in his ear, and he'd never remember what. He remembered Doc whistling, low and impressed.

He remembered the queen drawing her sword.

The sheath slid off it like skin. The blade underneath was pale and alive, a long ribbed thing that flexed in her hand as she brought it up, the way a muscle flexes. She didn't look at Jon for permission. She looked at the man in black armour beside the throne.

"Yours," said Derrek to his apprentice, without interest. "Go on."

The apprentice came down off the dais.

He was human. Eli could see that now. Young, perhaps younger than Eli, broad and hard and scarred, built like something bred on purpose, and his eyes were red-rimmed. He was crying. He didn't seem to know it. He drew his own pale sword and came across the floor at the queen like a falling wall.

She met him and it was beautiful.

Eli had sparred with her every day for half a year. He had thought he knew how good she was. He hadn't known anything. In the Tower, with all that dead city around them at their elbows, she moved like nothing he'd ever seen: every block exact, every step measured to the hair, her living blade flowing into the gaps in his guard and out again, and the sound of it all was strange and soft, like meat on meat. She was smiling. It was the first time Eli had ever seen her smile.

She cut the apprentice four times in the first ten seconds. He bled and kept coming.

Then he looked back at the dais.

Derrek was yawning. He had his head resting on his hand, and he was looking out the great window at the burning sky, and he covered his mouth politely, and he wasn't watching at all.

Something happened to the apprentice's face.

He reached. Not outward, the way you reach for a thing. Down, into the floor, into the walls, into the Tower itself. The lights along the walls dimmed all at once, and the floor shivered under Eli's knees, as though the whole building had drawn a breath. The apprentice began to shine. Light came up through his skin as if he were a lantern someone had lit from inside. His blade blazed white. He moved, and he was faster than she was, suddenly, faster than anything, and he struck, and she caught it.

She caught every blow. That was what Eli would remember. She didn't miss a single one. He hit her with the whole city behind him, again and again, and every time her blade was there, perfect, exact, and every time Eli heard something in her give. A wrist. A forearm. The long bones of her arms, one after another, cracking under the weight of blocking him, and she kept blocking, and she kept smiling, and her arms bent in places where arms didn't bend.

Then the light around the apprentice turned to fire, and the last blow came through.

She fell in two pieces. She was still smiling.

The apprentice stood over her, burning, and turned to the dais with his arms spread, and Eli would never forget his face: a child's face, wet and hopeful.

"Master," he said. "Are you proud?"

Derrek looked at him for the first time.

"Proud?" he said. "Proud of what, exactly? You only killed one, and now you're burning alive." He sighed. "You've always been a disappointment, and I can't believe you've embarrassed me again today."

The apprentice didn't hear it. He was already screaming. The fire wasn't around him any more; it was in him, coming out through his skin, through his mouth, his eyes, the joints of his armour. He'd pulled too much of the Tower through too little of himself. He burned out the way the boy at the rift had, but faster and worse: from the inside, all at once, his body coming apart like paper in a stove. The scream went on for a long time. Then there was a shape on the floor, black and smoking and very small.

Nobody had heard a word of it. Derrek sighed. Another of his amazing one-liners, ruined by idiots.

---

The dark elf let go of Eli's arm.

He didn't see her go. Nobody saw her go. That was the point of her. One moment she was beside him and the next the shadows behind the throne moved, just slightly, the way a curtain moves in a closed room, and she was there, at Derrek's back, with her hand already rising.

Eli saw her fingers curl.

Lirael's Collapse. Forty years in the dark learning it. The one thing they'd given her that she'd kept. She closed her hand, and the space around Derrek folded inward toward him, all of it at once, the air and the light and the throne and the dais. Eli saw the stone of the floor run toward him like water down a drain. It was enormous. In the Tower, with that whole dead city at her back, it was the greatest thing she had ever done. For one moment Eli believed it.

Then Derrek turned around in his chair and looked at her, mildly, as you'd look at someone who had tapped you on the shoulder in a queue.

"Oh, *please*," he said. "From behind? Who taught you that?"

He did something with two fingers, almost lazily, like turning a key. The thing she had made went inside out.

Everything that had been falling toward him fell toward her instead.

She had time to look at Eli. He would hold on to that: she had time. Her silver eyes found his across the room. Then the space around her folded in, and she went into it, all of her at once, her body drawn in toward a point just behind her heart, her arms coming in, her face. It made almost no sound. And then the air closed with a small flat noise, like a book being shut, and where she'd been there was nothing.

Eli was on his knees. He didn't remember falling.

Beside him, Jon was standing very still, watching the empty place in the air behind the throne.

His face had changed. Eli would try for the rest of his life to describe how. It wasn't grief. It wasn't anything. The warmth had gone out of it, all at once, like a lamp turned down, and what was underneath was smooth and cold and patient and very, very old. Jon was looking at the place where she had died the way a man looks at a page he's read before.

Derrek was looking back at him, over her empty place. He seemed, for just a moment, sorry. Then that was gone too.

"Did you like it?" Derrek said softly. "I know how you love that one."

Jon said nothing at all.

---

Doc was laughing.

He walked out into the middle of the floor, past the queen's body and the black thing that had been the apprentice, and he was laughing, a big happy laugh, as if this were the best party he had ever been to.

"Oh, you're *good*," he said to Derrek. "Oh, you're very, very good. I've been waiting so long to meet somebody like you." He cracked his knuckles. They were small and pudgy and they cracked like gunshots. "I saw the Tower come up, you know. I was there. I've wanted to see what was inside it since before your empire had a name." He grinned with every one of his teeth. "Let's find out what you're made of."

He opened his arms.

Things came out of him.

They poured out of the little round body like smoke out of a chimney, a great twisting torrent of shapes, faint and grey and screaming. Faces. Hands. Giants and dragons, elves and humans. Dwarves leading orcs, and gnomes, so many gnomes, little round faces with their mouths open. They came out of him by the hundred, by the thousand, more than the room could hold, more than any one body could ever have held, and they went at Derrek in a howling wave, and Eli understood, finally, kneeling on the floor, what Doc was, and what had happened behind the curtain in the clinic, and every time before that.

*I wasn't able to save your loved one.*

The wave broke over the throne.

Derrek sat in it as if it were weather. He didn't even raise a hand. The shapes went into him and off him and around him, tearing, clawing, and it made no difference. He was still exactly himself. After a while he sighed.

"What a waste," he said. "All those people." He looked past Doc at Jon, and there was something in his face that looked like contempt and something that looked like sorrow. "Is this really the best you could find?"

Doc kept pouring. He was smaller now. His face was sinking. His skin was going grey and loose, like a fruit left too long in the sun, and still the shapes came, and still they broke on Derrek and fell away. Ages of them. Whole ages.

The last one came out slowly.

It was a gnome. A young one, thin and sullen, with a face very like Doc's had once been, perhaps. It didn't scream. It didn't fly at Derrek with the others. It hung in the air in front of Doc for a long moment, and turned, and looked at him, with eyes so full of hate it was hard to watch. Then the magic took it, and flung it, and Derrek brushed it away with the back of his hand, and it was gone.

Doc sat down.

He was very old now. He was older than anything Eli had ever seen, his face folded and folded again, his body shrinking into his clothes. He looked around the throne room with his milky eyes as if he were seeing it for the first time.

"Oh," he said, in a small, surprised voice. "Oh, it's quiet."

For the first time in as long as he could remember, it was quiet. He found it peaceful.

Then he was dust, and the dust lay on the pale floor in the shape of a small round man sitting down, and then even that slid apart.

Jon was smiling.

Eli saw it. He'd never be sure, afterwards, that he had. Jon was watching the dust where Doc had been, and his cold, still face had cracked open into something that was almost a smile, almost longing, as if he were watching someone step off a cliff and envying them the fall.

"Jon," said Derrek.

The smile was gone. Jon looked up.

"It's just us now," said Derrek. "Shall we?"

---

Eli went first.

He didn't decide to. He was up off the floor and running across that long pale room before he knew he was going to do it, and he reached with everything he had, everything the master had taught him and everything the Tower would give him, all that dead city at his elbow, more than he had ever held in his life. He went for the man on the throne. His father. He went for him with his whole heart.

Derrek didn't look at him. He moved one hand, slightly, the way you'd wave off a fly, and Eli was on the floor fifty feet away with his ribs broken and his reach gone out of him like breath. He lay there. He couldn't get up. He watched.

Jon walked past him without looking down.

What happened next, Eli would never be able to tell anyone, and in the end he would stop trying.

They fought. That was all he could say. Two men, in a room at the top of the world, and the room couldn't hold them. The great window starred and held. The floor came up in waves. The light went strange, and stranger, and then he couldn't look at it. There were moments when they seemed to be in several places at once and moments when they didn't seem to be anywhere, and once he saw Jon standing perfectly still in the centre of the floor with his eyes shut while the whole far wall of the throne room came apart around Derrek and put itself back together, and once he saw Derrek, for the first time, get up out of his chair.

It went on for a long time. Or it took no time at all.

At the end of it Derrek was on his knees.

He was bloodied. His plain dark clothes were torn. He was breathing hard, like a man at the end of a long climb, and for the first time since they'd walked into that room he looked old. Really old. Older than the giant. Older than Doc.

Jon stood over him. He was breathing hard too. He looked down at Derrek with that cold, smooth, patient face, and then he did something with both hands, carefully, precisely, like a man tying a difficult knot, and Derrek's arms went behind his back and stayed there, and his head went back, and he couldn't move.

Not dead. Eli had thought, the whole time, that Jon was trying to kill him. He saw now he wasn't. He'd done exactly what he said he'd do. He had tired him out.

Jon let out a long breath. Then he reached down and hauled Derrek up by the collar, as easily as lifting a coat, and walked him over to the great window, and sat him down on the floor beneath it with his back to the glass.

Then he sat down beside him.

---

## Nine: The Window

Eli dragged himself across the floor to them. It took a long time. Nobody helped him.

When he got there, the two men were sitting side by side under the great window, close as old friends on a bench. Outside, the sky over Earth was full of fire.

And the ground was opening.

Eli saw it from up here before he understood it. Down there, far below the Tower, in the burning city, there was a place where the ground had always been wrong: an old wound, the oldest rift of all, black and glassy, at the city's heart. It was spreading. The black was creeping outward through the streets like ink through paper. And there were other places, further off, toward the horizon and beyond it. Other rifts. All of them spreading. Something was coming up out of them. He couldn't see what. He could only see that it was wrong, the way a word you have said too many times becomes wrong, and that the ships fighting over the city had started, one by one, to stop fighting and turn toward it.

"Jon," said Derrek.

He hadn't looked at Eli. He hadn't looked at him once since they'd come into the room. Eli lay on the floor three feet from his father's knee, bleeding, and his father looked straight past him at Jon, as if Eli were a crack in the wall.

"You remember who our master is," said Derrek.

Jon didn't answer. He was looking out of the window.

"You know what you have to do," said Derrek. "You know it. You've always known it."

Jon sighed.

It was a long sigh, a tired one, the sigh of a man asked for the thousandth time to take out the rubbish. He leaned his head back against the glass. He looked, for a moment, very young.

"Just enjoy the show, old man," he said.

"What's happening?" said Eli.

Nobody answered him.

"What is that? What's coming out of the ground?" His voice cracked. "What does he have to do? *Jon.* What does he mean, your master? What master? Who *are* you?" He looked at his father. "Why did you kill Teo? You didn't even *look* at him. You're my father, why did you— why did any of this— *answer me!*"

Derrek didn't turn his head. Jon didn't turn his head. They sat side by side under the window with the fire on their faces and watched the black spread through the city, street by street, toward the Tower.

After a while, without looking, Derrek moved his bound shoulders a little closer to Jon's, the way you'd lean on someone on a long train journey. Jon let him.

Outside, the world went on ending. Inside, the two men sat at the window together, quiet, at peace, and waited for it to arrive.

Eli lay on the floor beside them and asked his questions until he ran out of them, and nobody answered any of them at all.

---

*End of sample.*
