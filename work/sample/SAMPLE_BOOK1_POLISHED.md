## One: Property

The guard didn't get tired. That was the whole problem.

Eli did. It started in his thighs and climbed into his chest, until every breath came in hot and short and not enough. Behind him the guard's boots hit the slag in a rhythm that never broke, and the rig on the man's back, a ribbed hump of grown flesh in a steel cage, gave a wet click every few strides.

"Stop! By imperial warrant!" The voice came out of a speaker at his collar, flat and much too loud. "Return what you took!"

Eli could have stopped him. One reach and the guard would be a stain on the wall. But a reach that size, in the street, would be seen, and somebody would know whose it was. So he ran.

The city ended here. Behind him the towers rose white out of the bedrock without a seam in them. Ahead was a fence of plain wire, and past it the yards: slag flats, cranes, open sheds, the pens. The air tasted of hot fat and ozone.

He went over the fence. It bit him on the way, and he came down hard on one hip, and got up, and kept going.

The pens were full. Orcs stood in rows under the shed roofs, hundreds deep, men and women both, heavy in the shoulder and bare to the waist, waiting their turn on the ramps that ran up into the hull-presses. Each of them had a neat pale scar at the base of the skull. At the rail a young one had wrapped a rag round his hand where the wire had cut it. He looked up at Eli going past, and then down at his feet.

The guard didn't bother with the fence. He stopped at the wire, took a slate off his belt and spoke into it.

Every orc in the yard turned their head at once.

They came out of the pens in a slow wave, over the rails, hands open and reaching. The young one with the rag was crying. An old man near the front with a split lip was saying something over and over while his legs walked him forward, and when he got close enough Eli heard that it was a name.

Eli reached.

The air in front of him went hard and he swung it, and the front rank went over into the rank behind. He felt bones go, and heard them. More came on over the ones he'd dropped. The ones on the ground were trying to get up. Some of them couldn't.

He could have cleared the yard. He didn't want to touch them again, even from where he stood.

A man was sitting on the counterweight of the nearest crane, twenty feet up, eating a peach. He wore a long coat too warm for the weather, and he had the kind of face that looks pleased to see you before it knows who you are.

"You look," he said, "like you could use an exit."

He dropped off the crane, landed hard, and came across the slag still chewing. A woman with burn scars up both arms got to Eli first. The man stepped in and laid two fingers on the back of her neck, on the scar, the way you'd test a child's forehead for fever. Her legs went. He let her down.

He took the next one by the wrist, turned him, touched, and let him down too. Then two at once, a hand for each, and he grunted at that and shook his fingers out afterwards. He kept himself between them and Eli and took them as they came. By the fifth or sixth he was breathing through his mouth. The old man with the split lip reached them still saying the name, and the man put a hand to the back of his head, and the name stopped halfway.

Then nobody else came. Over by the fence the guard had lowered the slate and was backing off, talking fast into his collar. In the pens and on the ramps the rest stood where they'd been stopped, hands half up. The young one with the rag was still crying.

The man wiped his fingers on his coat and put a warm hand on Eli's shoulder.

"Breathe," he said. "You're doing beautifully." He nodded at the fence. "He's seen your face. He'll write it up, and it goes up, and up again, till it lands on the desk of whoever you're running from." The hand stayed where it was. "Your call. I won't make it for you."

Eli had ordered men flogged. At sixteen he had watched a steward's hands broken for theft, because his tutors said a prince should see what his word cost. He had never done it himself.

He reached across forty yards of slag, closed his grip on the guard's throat, and held it. It took longer than he'd thought it would. The guard's heels drummed on the slag. When it was over the body sat down against the fence, and the rig on its back kept clicking.

Eli's hand was shaking. He looked at it.

The man took the hand in both of his and held it until it was still. "That's how you know you're not him," he said. "Whoever he is." He let go. "What did you steal, by the way?"

Eli wiped his mouth.

"Me," he said.

The man laughed so hard he had to bend over with his hands on his knees. Then he straightened up, finished the peach, and dropped the stone on the old orc with the split lip. It landed on his cheek and stayed there.

Teo† was meant to be at the fence with the flyer. He wasn't. Eli stood among the bodies and watched the sky over the sheds and counted to six hundred, and started again. Somewhere in the city a siren went up and down and quit. The man in the coat sat on an upturned bucket, cleaning his nails with a sliver of fence wire, and didn't offer to help.

"He's not coming," the man said pleasantly, somewhere in the second six hundred. "We could walk."

Eli didn't move.

The flyer came in low over the sheds forty minutes late, an old plain-engine thing with a cracked canopy and a fresh dent down one side, and hit the slag hard. Teo leaned out, white in the face, talking before the engine had stopped.

"They put a checkpoint on the ring road, I had to go round by the drainage cuts, the whole way round, I thought you were dead, I thought they'd—" He saw the bodies. He looked at the guard against the fence, and then at the stranger.

Nine days ago Teo had forged the code that got them both out of the palace. His parents were still inside.

"Did you do all that?" he said to Eli.

"No," said Eli.

"Jon," said the stranger, and climbed into the back of the flyer as though he'd been invited. "Friend of the family."

"Which family?" said Eli.

"Long story. Bad ending."

Teo lifted off. Below them the orcs were still standing in the pens.

"Where to?" said Teo.

## Two: The Master

Jon rode with them for six days, and said he knew which back roads the drones didn't watch. On the fourth day a drone came down one of them and hung in the air ahead of the flyer, looking, and Teo had to drop them into a river gorge, kill the engine and sit under the overhang until dark with the water loud underneath. Jon slept through most of it. When he woke he said they must have changed the routes, and that was all he said about it.

On the second night Teo had sat alone on the flyer's wing for an hour. When he came back in he said to Eli, quietly, "He just touched them and they died. He was eating a peach." In the morning Jon made Teo breakfast and asked about his parents and listened to the whole answer, and said they must be very proud of him. By noon Teo was laughing at his stories again.

On the seventh morning Jon climbed out at a fuel stop in the middle of nothing and stretched until his back cracked.

"I have to go and see some elves about a thing," he said.

"What do you want from me?" said Eli.

Jon looked at him pleasantly.

"Company," he said, and walked off down the road.

---

Over the next year he came and went.

He turned up for a week on a salvage hull where Eli and Teo were cutting cable out of dead ships for cash, then stayed away all winter. He turned up again on a transit platform with a bag of peaches, stayed a month, and was gone. When Eli asked where he went, he said, "Elves."

Eli learned what food cost. He went hungry three days on a station where no one would hire a man with soft hands, and on the fourth he broke a dock boss's knee for a job, and took the job. Teo didn't speak to him for a week. Half the shopfronts on that station kept a little shrine to the Emperor's god, lit with a cheap bulb, and not one of them had the Emperor's face on it, because nobody knew it. A dockhand kicked a cargo loader that had stopped to think too long. "Battery," he said, and spat.

In the fourth month Eli found his own face on a post outside a customs hall.

RECOVER IMPERIAL PROPERTY, it said under the seal. It had him down to the scar on his chin. By the date, it had gone up seven days after he ran. Two days before the yard.

He stood in front of it while the shift changed in the hall behind him. The guard had been chasing a face that was already on every post in human space, and his report would have gone up to people who already had it. The man against the fence had died for nothing. Jon had known that, and had held Eli's hand until it stopped shaking.

Teo came up beside him, read it, and said nothing.

"Property," said Eli.

His father had never once asked to see him. Tutors, reports, orders, never a face. Eli had told himself that running was enough. They would post him on every world until somebody dragged him home.

"I'm going to the Tower," he said. "I'm going to take the chair away from him."

Teo laughed, then saw his face, and stopped.

When Jon next turned up Eli told him, and told him the rest before Jon could: he would need people, and he would need to be better than he was, and he meant to go and find both. Jon heard him out without a word. Then he put his hand on Eli's shoulder, where it had been in the yard.

"A great deal better," he said.

Eli didn't ask him about the guard. Jon was already asking Teo if there was anything to eat.

---

He had been taught to push, hard and clean. He could break a wall from across a room. He could not tell, with his eyes shut, where the wall was. So he went looking for a master, and found one at a rift.

The rift was on a grey, cold world too far from anything to matter, apart from the wound in the ground: three miles across, the rock gone to glass, the air tasting like a struck bell. Remnant welled up out of it like oil from a bad well, so there was a city on the rim, and the city had been at war for longer than anyone in it had been alive. Five companies held wells on the rim. Somebody had built a temple on the near edge, and somebody else had shelled it, and somebody had built it again.

Soldiers came off the transports in amplifier rigs that let an ordinary man reach three times as far as he should. On his second day Eli watched one burn out. He was perhaps nineteen, holding a line on the north wells, and he pushed through his rig twenty seconds longer than his body could carry. His face went grey, then slack. He sat down in the mud with blood coming out of his ears and eyes. The rig was off him before he was cold and buckled onto the next boy in line.

"Burnout," said the woman who sold Eli noodles that night. "Rig's fine. It's the meat that goes."

The master lived in the shelled temple. He was old and spare, and at one time or another he had trained rig soldiers for every company on the rim. He watched Eli come up the broken steps and then looked at his hands.

"Who taught you?"

The three best tutors in the Empire, Eli told him, and a duelling master from the dragon schools.

"They taught you to shout," said the master. "Sit."

He kept Eli five months and never once let him push. He sat him blindfolded in the temple and told him to find the one moth in the room, and when Eli couldn't, he left him sitting until he could. He had him lift a cup of water without spilling it, then lift the water without the cup.

"You've the strength of an army," he said, "and the hands of a man in mittens. One day it'll kill you."

In the fifth month Eli threaded a needle across the room without touching it.

"You'll never be fine," said the master. "You started too late. But you'll be less likely to kill your friends by accident."

Before he left, Eli told him who he was and where he was going, and asked him to come.

The master went to the temple door and stood looking out over the glass and the far fires of the company lines.

"My master went up to that Tower," he said. "Fifty years ago. Best I ever saw. He was sick of watching boys burn out over a hole in the ground for men who never came near it. Said he'd go and see who sat in the chair." He didn't turn round. "He didn't come back. Nobody ever told me how far he got."

He was quiet a while.

"No. I won't walk into that place behind a boy with a grudge. Go and die somewhere better than this."

Eli didn't argue.

On the ship Teo said, "We'll find someone else. There's other masters. There's got to be."

Eli didn't answer. He sat with his eyes shut and tried to find the walls of the cabin, and couldn't, and kept trying until Teo fell asleep.

## Three: The Healer and the Statue

They needed a healer, and Eli picked the gnome.

Teo didn't like it. They were in a dwarven mining district on a world known only by a number. The district had a gnome quarter, and the quarter had a clinic with a line out the door. In the dwarven districts gnomes worked on contracts that ran for life, and they got very good at not being noticed.

The healer was small even for a gnome, round in the face, bald on top, with enormous ears, and he talked the whole time he worked. Eli stood at the back and watched him set a miner's shattered forearm while the man screamed, and chat all the way through it about a goat.

"Ate my whole herb garden, that goat. The winter half the village came to me with fever, and there's me with not a leaf. I could have strangled him. Didn't, mind. I was very fond of him. Hold still."

Then he took a feverish girl from her mother and sat with her behind a curtain for an hour, and brought her out again cool and asleep.

The mother tried to thank him. He held up a small hand.

"Thank me when she's grown," he said. "Next."

When he saw Eli and Teo he looked them over, quick and professional. "Not dying?"

"No," said Eli.

"Pity. I could use one I can fix." He dried his hands. "So what is it you want?"

A long journey, Eli told him. A small crew. Someone who could close a wound and set a bone and not ask where either came from.

"Then you'll have to buy me," said the gnome. "The council holds my paper." He named a price. It was most of what they had.

Outside, Teo said, "There's something wrong with him."

"He's good."

"He's too good. Did you see that line? Nobody that good stays somewhere like this. Nobody. Why would you?"

"Then he'll be glad to leave," said Eli, and went back in and paid.

The gnome signed his own transfer with a flourish. Eli read it over and put it in the clinic stove.

The gnome watched it burn. Then he smiled up at Eli, very warmly.

"Well," he said. "Where would I go?"

"What do we call you?" Teo asked.

"Doc†. The children call me that. Saves everyone the bother of a name." He picked up his bag.

On the river bridge out of the district a troll sat in a toll box, a squat grumbling fellow a head taller than Doc and still a head short of Eli's shoulder. Doc paid him in coin and a joke about his mother. The troll didn't laugh. A mile on, Teo noticed the troll's stick was gone from the box. It was in Doc's bag, with a nail through the end.

"Always wanted one," said Doc.

---

Eli wanted to see the elves first. They were the Empire's oldest enemy, and if he took the chair he would inherit their war. Jon said they were the only people left who could build a city that made you want to cry, and came along to get them in.

It stood on a world of high cold rivers, grown whole like the old giant cities, but finer. Its towers leaned in toward each other at the top.

Getting in took three hours. Papers. A search. An inquisitor in grey asked Eli the same eleven questions three times in the same order and watched his eyes. At the end Jon spoke to him in elvish and showed him a token on a chain. The inquisitor turned the token over, then held out his hand and kept it out until Jon took the chain off his neck and gave it to him. He put it in his coat. Then he stamped the papers, three days and not one more, with a red bar across the corner, and wrote something in a ledger beside each of their names.

"I helped the king once, when he was a boy," Jon said, inside. He touched his collar where the chain had been. "That was my only one. Try not to steal anything."

A grey coat was behind them in the market that afternoon, and another at the end of their street that night, and it was never the same coat twice.

On the second day they crossed a square where an elf knelt on the stones with his hands bound behind him and his mouth sewn shut with silver wire. People walked past without looking.

"What did he do?" Teo whispered.

"Disagreed," said Jon. "Out loud."

Nobody jostled in the streets. Nobody said excuse me in a doorway. Crowds parted and closed round each other without a word or a glance, and Eli, who had grown up in a palace full of whispering, couldn't get used to it. The king's name was on the arches and on the coins and said quietly in the markets: Mika. Every order came from him.

A young archivist, under orders to be helpful, told them about the dark elves in two sentences. They were born in the prison realm with the soul thieves and the traitors, and they came out of it full of hate. They were not really elves any more.

On the last afternoon they crossed a long square with a statue in the middle of it.

An elven woman, cut in white stone at twice life size. She stood with one hand raised and the fingers a little curled, about to close them on something only she could see. Fresh flowers were heaped at her feet, and elves stood round her in silence.

"Lirael," said Jon. "Hero of her people. The king's sister. A long time ago."

At the foot of the statue an old elven woman was crying without a sound. Teo crouched beside her and asked in his bad elvish if she'd lost someone.

"Yes," she said.

"Who?"

She looked at him.

"I don't know," she said. "I never know. None of us do." She turned back to the statue. "We come here because it seems the place to bring it."

The others walked on. Eli turned to say something to Jon, and Jon wasn't beside him.

He was standing in front of the statue with his hands in his coat pockets, looking up into the stone face. He stood there long enough that the old woman looked up at him, and Eli started to say his name.

Then Jon turned round.

"Dinner," he said. It came out hoarse. He cleared his throat and said it again. "Come on."

## Four: The Door

Two months later, over a bad meal in a worse port, Jon mentioned the crater.

He mentioned it between two other stories. A dead moon at the edge of elven space, with a black floor where the world had worn thin. The Inquisition kept a station on one side of it. On the ninth of every month a dwarven ship set down on the other.

"What for?" said Eli.

"Buying," said Jon, and went on eating.

Eli asked what they were buying. Jon told him without looking up.

Eli lay awake with it. In the morning he said, "We're going to the crater."

"Why?" said Teo. "They're elves. It's an elf thing. Isn't it? It's their thing."

"The buyers are ours," said Eli. "Rich men, Jon said. Human. That's Empire money on that ship." He was already checking the flyer's charge. "If I'm taking that chair, I'd like to know what comes with it."

Jon didn't come. He had elves to see.

---

They watched from the rim for a day. On the evening of the ninth, inquisitors in grey walked a line of chained figures across the black floor to the dwarven ship. All of them were starved thin and shackled wrist to wrist, and their eyes caught the station lights and gave them back silver. They walked with their heads down and didn't look at the ship. At the ramp a dwarf in a good coat counted them, and an inquisitor counted the money.

Overhead, a human freighter blinked out of the sky above the crater, and the air over the black floor shuddered after it like water after a stone.

"Where'd it go?" said Teo.

"Through," said Doc. He was watching the floor. "Their prison's just the other side of that, you see. Humans drop through it to cross quicker, and the elves hate that, they hate it terribly. They go their own way, squeezed down a dimension, and while they're in there they can't feel each other at all. Not a whisper."

"How do you know all that?"

"I listen," said Doc.

The trade at the ramp was nearly done. Eli went down the crater wall.

He didn't sweep. He found the guns on the dwarven ship as he'd found the moth in the temple, and closed his grip on each barrel and bent it. He was still feeling along the hull for more when one he'd missed opened up from underneath, and it walked across the floor and through the end of the prisoners' line before he found it and crushed it. After that everything went at once. He tore some of the inquisitors' weapons out of their hands and lost track of the rest. Something cracked off the rim behind him, and Teo shouted, and then Teo's rifle went twice. Doc came down the wall after Eli, slow and puffing and talking the whole way under his breath, the way he'd talked through the miner's arm. A dwarven crewman came at him with a cutting bar. Doc stepped aside, small and clumsy, and the bar went into the floor, and Doc hit the man across the knees with the troll's stick until he stopped trying to get up.

It was over in a few minutes. The inquisitors were dead or running, and the dwarf in the good coat was face down on his own ramp with Eli's grip on the back of his neck. Up on the rim Teo sat in the black grit with both hands clamped on his thigh and blood coming through his fingers, swearing at the man he'd shot.

They broke the chains. The freed prisoners didn't thank anyone. Most of them ran into the dark. The two at the end of the line lay where the gun had left them, still chained to each other.

One, an old man with no teeth, stayed. He stood at the edge of the place where the floor went strange and stared at the air above it. The air there trembled faintly, like a pane of glass in wind.

"Someone's there," he said. "I know that shake. I made it once, from the other side. Someone's at the door."

Eli couldn't feel souls; nobody had ever said he could. But he could feel the world a little now, and the air over the black floor was thin, and it was bulging, slightly, toward him.

"They've been at it a while," Doc said quietly. "Look how the floor's worn. Poor thing."

"Can it be opened?"

The old man laughed with no teeth. "From this side? By who? It doesn't open. It takes."

Eli put out his hands.

He had the strength. What he didn't have was practice. He had to find the edge of the thin place with a sense he'd been using for five months, get his grip into a seam narrower than a needle's eye, and pull in a direction that wasn't there. His nose began to bleed. His sight went white at the edges. Somewhere Teo was saying his name.

The seam gave. A woman fell out of the air onto her hands and knees, and it closed behind her with a sound like a cough.

She was starved thin, in rags tied over rags, with a short curved knife in each hand, and she came up off the ground already moving. She went past Eli without looking at him. The last two inquisitors were running for their station. She caught them at the door and went in close and touched them, a palm on a chest, fingertips at a throat, and they folded.

She walked back and looked at the blood on his lip, and at the place in the air behind him.

"You opened it," she said. Her voice was rough from not being used. "From out here."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Someone was pushing."

She looked at him with plain contempt.

"Thirty of us, when we started," she said. "No one escapes alone. That's what they say down there." She looked at the closed air. "I was a child."

Her eyes were silver, like the eyes in every story the palace nurses had ever told him.

*Oh*, thought Eli.

"What's your name?"

"When you've earned it."

They took the dwarven ship. It was low and black, built by the Returned†, the dwarves who had come back out of the maze-land with new tricks, and it could squeeze through the world the way dwarves did. Teo flew it out with his leg tied off. They left the dwarf in the good coat on the moon. Doc wanted to leave him a water ration. Eli said no, and the dark elf looked at Eli properly for the first time.

Two days out, Eli counted the stores. They were a water ration short. Doc was in the galley dressing Teo's leg and talking about goats. Eli counted again and got the same number.

---

It took her four months to stop sleeping with a knife in her hand.

Eli had been raised on the same stories as the archivist, and he unlearned them slowly. She ate fast and without a sound, with her arm curled round her plate. She couldn't stand closed doors, or being thanked. She'd trained on the road, she said, fifty years of it, with whoever would teach her, and paid for every lesson in ways she didn't describe.

And she felt the elves, always. Even out in the dark between the stars there was a light at the edge of her: the homeworld, trillions of them, shining from across the galaxy.

"It never goes out," she said once in the galley, not looking at him. "Worst thing they did. They let us keep that."

Another time Teo called her a dark elf without thinking. She didn't look up from her plate. "There's a sun down there," she said. "It's a world. Like these. They named it hell. Then they named us after it." Teo didn't use the word again. Eli had stopped using it some time before and couldn't have said when.

Late one night Eli came down the corridor and found her alone in the hold, practising. She stood in the middle of the empty deck with one hand raised, the fingers a little curled, and closed them slowly. The air in front of her folded in on itself with a small flat sound, like a book being shut. A cargo strap a yard away went into it and was gone.

She saw him and dropped her hand.

"Lirael's Collapse," she said. "Down there you learn her name before your own. Everyone wants that one." She looked at where the strap had been. "Forty years. It's the one thing of theirs we took."

Jon was in the doorway behind Eli. He had come aboard that evening after five weeks away, grey in the face, and gone straight to his bunk without telling anyone where he'd been.

"That's a hard one to learn," he said. "Good for you."

He went back down the corridor whistling. Halfway along he lost the tune, stopped, and started it again from the top.

## Five: The Queen and the Giant

The colony was on a desert world at the far end of nowhere, cut off so long it had forgotten anyone else was out there. An elven seedship had come down in the middle of its oldest myth and broken its back in the dunes. The colonists took the elves for invaders, the elves took the colonists for vermin, and both had started killing. The dead were stacked in the dunes on either side of a dry riverbed.

Jon had brought them there for water, he said. Eli put the ship down in the riverbed between the two camps and walked out alone under both sets of guns.

This, at least, the palace had taught him. He found the colony's grandmothers and the elven captain's second, sat them across a folding table in the sun, and let each side describe the other until they ran out of words. On the second day the elves walked out. On the fourth a grandmother spat on the table and the talks stopped for two days, and Eli sat at the table alone in the heat through both of them, so that both camps could watch him sitting there. On the sixth day a colonist boy shot an elf at the water line. Eli walked the boy to the elven camp himself and handed him over, and stood there while they did it, so the grandmothers would believe the line meant something. One of the grandmothers was the boy's. She came back to the table the next morning and never spoke to Eli again. By the ninth day there was a line in the riverbed that neither side crossed, and nobody had died for three days.

On the twelfth day an elven ship came down out of the sky, and a woman walked down its ramp.

She was taller than any elf he'd met, armoured, young-looking in the way that meant nothing with elves. Her sword hung at her hip in a pale, faintly ribbed sheath that shifted against her leg when she walked. The seedship elves straightened as she passed and stopped talking.

"They call her the Queen," Jon said quietly beside him. "Like on the board, the strongest piece in the box. Nothing to do with who her mother was."

She stopped in front of Eli and took in his face, his clothes and his hands, and her mouth curled a little.

"You saved four hundred of my king's people," she said, "with a folding table. My king has heard of you. He hears you are going to the Tower."

Eli looked at Jon. Jon was looking at the seedship.

"My king has sworn to kill the man in that Tower," said the Queen. "So has every elf alive. In all the ages he has sat in it, not one of us has passed its door. Now a human walks toward it, a human who can talk his way across a riverbed, and my king would like me to walk behind him." Her eyes were flat. "No one said I must like it."

She went up the ramp past all of them and took the best bunk.

The dark elf didn't come out of her cabin for two days. When she did, her hands weren't quite steady. On a ship that small the two elves felt each other all the time. In the corridors neither looked at the other, and they never spoke at all, and everyone else learned to wait until one of them had gone by.

The Queen was a better fighter than Eli in every way that could be measured, and she made sure he knew it. She sparred with him every day and put him on the deck every day, without malice and without effort, and walked off while he was still getting up. She called him *the human*.

"Why her?" Eli asked Jon one night, when his ribs hurt too much to sleep.

"Mika sent her," said Jon. "Mika's very much in charge." He patted Eli's arm. "I'm glad he did, though. She's better than you, and she hates you. You'll come on twice as fast."

---

The giant lived on a forge world, and going to see him was Eli's idea.

The war Eli meant to finish would need ships, and giants built ships. They built the cathedral ships for the dragons, and sold them, and kept to themselves, and there were fewer of them every century. Everyone knew the giants were dying out. But Jon knew one who would talk to a human.

From orbit the forge world was black rock under a red sky, and its shipyards held a dozen cathedral ships in their cradles, each the length of a city, ribbed and pale and half-made.

The giant met them in a hall so big it had its own weather. Sitting, he was the height of a four-storey house. When he spoke, Eli felt it in the soles of his feet.

"Jon," he said.

"Hello, old friend."

"I am not your friend," said the giant. "But I am friendly." He looked down at the rest of them. "Sit, little ones."

Eli stayed on his feet. He asked for passage, and a ship, and the giants' old quarrel with the dragons turned on the one man who sat above them all. The giant heard him out in silence.

"And why would I lend a ship to a human?"

Eli had been raised to know what he had and when to spend it.

"Because I'm the Emperor's son," he said.

Behind him the Queen went very still.

The giant didn't move. Then he told them about the golden age: the giants' laws and the giants' peace, and the dragons who had been their seconds and decided they would rule better. On betrayal day the emperor's closest advisor, a dragon, walked into the heart of the capital and breathed it to ash. In the last moment of his life the emperor took his whole city, every body and every soul in it, and shaped it into a shield.

"What was left is the Tower," said the giant. "Your father's house. Do not call it a building. The souls of that day are still in its walls, all of them, run together." He leaned down until his face filled the air in front of Eli. "And you would like me to send giants to knock on its door. No."

A servant showed them out by a side door. It was the one they used for deliveries.

---

The Queen came for him that night in the dark corridor outside the giant's hall.

He didn't see the first cut. He felt it, a line of cold across his ribs, and the master's five months saved him from the second: he felt the air move before the blade did and put a wall of hard air in its way. The living blade flexed against the wall and slid round it. He got his grip on her wrist. She broke it with a twist he didn't follow, her knee came up into his chest, and he was on the floor with her foot on his throat and the pale edge an inch from his eye.

"His son," she said. Her voice was level. "My king sent me to guard the son of the man who—" She stopped. The blade trembled. He had never seen it do that. "He knew. My king knew, and sent me anyway, and did not tell me."

Eli looked down the corridor for Jon. It was empty. Jon had stayed in the hall to talk with the giant, and the doors were shut.

"Then ask him," Eli said. It came out a croak round her foot. "The relay's on the ship. If he says kill me, you've lost a day."

She looked down at him. Then the blade moved, very fast, and opened his cheek from ear to jaw, and she stepped back and sheathed it, and the sheath closed over it like a mouth.

"So you remember who you are," she said.

Doc stitched the cheek on the ship without a joke and told him it would scar. Jon came back near dawn, saw the stitches and asked what had happened, and when Eli told him he said, "Ah." He lifted a hand toward Eli's face, then put it in his coat pocket. That was all.

She sent the message. The reply came in eleven hours. Eli never saw it. He saw her face when she read it. Then she went down to the training deck and put her fist through the bulkhead twice, and came back with her knuckles split to the bone and her face exactly as it had been.

"Continue," she said to no one, and went to her bunk.

After that she stopped calling him *the human*, or anything at all. She sparred with him every day and put him on the deck as before. But she no longer walked off while he was getting up. She stood and watched until he was on his feet, and then she put him down again.

They left the forge world the next morning.

---

## Six: The Strike

The dragon knew his crew would die.

He didn't tell them. They were good crew, wingless kin, two-legged and scaled and quick. They had served him eighty years and called themselves dragons as he did, and if you had asked, they would have told you there had never been any other kind. They were strapped into their stations in the focusing chamber now, along the long curved bones of the ship's heart, and they were nervous, because they had never seen him come down here himself.

"A demonstration," he told them. "Aim for the forge-heart. On my mark."

At the centre of the chamber sat the cradle, where a body could pour its whole strength into one place and the ship would gather it up and throw it across the dark in a single line. Crews took turns in it, a few shots each before their bodies needed a day. A ship's master let the crew do it. That was what crews were for.

Tonight the master climbed into the cradle himself and began to strap in.

"Master," said his first officer. "The chamber is not rated for—"

"Then it will learn." He pulled the last strap tight with his teeth. "Aim."

He thought about the giants. He thought about his grandfather's grandfather, who had died on betrayal day, and about everything he was owed.

He opened his mouth and drove the chamber far past anything it had been built to take. His heart laboured and tore. The long bones of his neck cracked one after another under the strain of holding the line, and he held it anyway.

---

From the surface it looked like a line drawn with a ruler. It went in at the forge-heart and the forge-heart was gone, and then the shipyards, and the cradles, the cathedral ships buckling in them one after another like ribs under a boot. Then the crust came up in a ring and kept coming, a wave of red rock taller than mountains rolling out across the face of the world.

Afterwards the dragon's ship drifted dark in orbit. Its crew sat in their stations, cooked inside their scales. Its master hung in the cradle with his neck broken in three places and his eyes open.

Eli watched it on the long-range display, a day out and still accelerating.

Nobody spoke. The dark elf stood at his shoulder, close enough that he could hear her breathing. Teo was crying and didn't seem to know it.

"The giant," said Teo. "Was he— did he get—"

Nobody answered. Jon stood at the back of the bridge looking at the screen, holding a cup of tea he hadn't touched.

Within three days fleets came out of shipyards on worlds nobody had known the giants still held. There were hundreds of cathedral ships.

---

Eli went back for the master.

It took them a month to reach the rift world through a sector coming apart at the seams. The city on the rim was emptier. Three of the five companies had pulled their rigs and their boys and gone to the war. The master was in his temple. He watched Eli come up the broken steps and didn't get up.

"You're better," he said.

"You said nobody told you how far he got," said Eli. "Your master." He sat down across from him. "I'm going up those stairs. Come and find out."

The master looked at him a long while.

"That's a cheap trick," he said at last. "Get out of my temple."

They waited on the rim four days, because the ship needed a part and the part had to come from two systems away. Eli didn't go back up the steps. On the fourth night the part came, and Teo had the drive warm by morning, and they had the ramp half up when the master came across the landing field in the rain with his bag on his shoulder.

Eli opened his mouth. The old man went past him up the ramp.

"Your drive's whining," he said from inside. "Get somebody to look at it."

---

It took them most of a year to cross the war.

Cathedral ships the size of moons ground into dragon fleets in the dark. Legion carriers went by with hulls pressed from orcs bought by the trillion, and if you walked a Legion corridor you couldn't be sure the faint warmth in the walls was only the heating. Dwarven convoys ran guns to everyone. Elven ships came out of nowhere, hit the Emperor's, and were gone.

An Inquisition cutter had been behind them since the crater. They lost it twice and it found them twice, and the third time it caught them over a gas giant whose name none of them ever learned. They got off the Returned ship in a lifeboat with what they could carry, and the cutter took the ship in tow and left the lifeboat to drift in a sector with no shipping lanes at all. A salvage tug picked them up on the nineteenth day, on the last of the water. Eli's ribs took four months to knit. They crossed two systems in the hold of a dwarven arms freighter, sleeping on crates of flesh rifles that twitched whenever the drive spun up.

Doc fought when they had to, and fought badly. He was slow and short of wind, and his reach was a market conjurer's, enough to knock a rifle aside or trip a man on a stair. Then he'd finish the man with the troll's stick and complain about his knees. Once, on the freighter, after a boarding party, the master stood watching Doc bandage a dwarf's leg and chat to him about goats, and the master's face went still and strange, as if he were listening for something in another room. Then Doc looked up and asked whether he'd hurt himself. The master said no, and walked off rubbing his eyes.

They went through hell once, on a human transit hull, because there was no other way across.

The moment the hull dropped through, the dark elf went rigid. The homeworld's light was gone from the edge of her. In its place, from the world outside the hull, came hundreds of millions of her own kind, everyone she had ever escaped, pressing in from every side.

She sat with her back to the bulkhead and her eyes shut and held his hand. Over the eleven hours of the crossing she broke two of his fingers, one at a time. He didn't make a sound, and he didn't take the hand away.

When they came out the other side she let go and looked at his hand, and then at him.

Doc set the fingers. The next morning in the galley Eli flexed the splinted hand at her across the table.

She told him her name. Nobody else on the ship ever heard her say it.

---

The dragon came for them on an airless moon where the freighter had put down for water.

It was young for a great dragon, which was the only reason any of them lived. It came down out of the black with a quarter-mile of wing spread over them, hunting freighters, and it had found one. Its first breath took the freighter's drive section off at the root. Its second took the dwarven crew, who had run for the hills.

"Make it breathe," said the master, very calm. "It can't do that forever. Make it waste them."

So they did. The Queen went out first, across the open dust in plain sight, so fast she seemed to be in two places at once. The dragon swung its head after her and breathed, and missed, and the line cut a canyon across the moon. Teo ran the other way, firing a flesh rifle into its flank. It breathed at him and missed by less than it should have, because the dark elf came up out of the dust under its jaw a step late and laid her palm flat on the scales of its throat, and the whole head jerked aside. The edge of the line went past Teo close enough to take the skin off his left hand and forearm through the suit. Doc stood in the freighter's shadow throwing rocks and shouting, his voice gone high and cracked.

It breathed again, and again, slower each time. The fifth breath came out ragged and short. The dragon's sides heaved, and it stood on the dust with its head down, swaying.

"Now," said the master.

Eli had waited at the edge of all of it and done nothing while Teo's arm burned. He reached.

He went slowly, blind, as he had for the moth. Through the scales, which was like feeling through a mountain. Through muscle and bone and the lungs, huge and hot. He found the heart, the size of the flyer and labouring after five breaths, and in it the narrow place where everything went through.

He closed his hand.

The dragon lifted its head and looked straight at him across the dust, as if it had only now noticed he was there. Then it fell, and the moon shook, and the dust went up over it in the airless dark and stayed up.

Eli was on his knees with blood running from his nose again. The master stood over him.

"That," said the master, "was fine."

Teo was sitting against the freighter's landing strut with his burned arm held away from his body, the suit seal hissing where the skin had come off with the cloth. Doc was already cutting it open. The freighter wouldn't fly again. They lived in its hold for three weeks on dwarven rations, the six of them, waiting for anyone at all to answer the beacon, and Teo's hand healed stiff and never closed right after that.

Jon was away that month. When he came back, on the ship that finally answered, he walked out to the carcass and looked at it for a while. Then he came back and put his hand on Eli's shoulder.

"I knew you would," he said.

"How?"

Jon opened his mouth and shut it. He looked out at the carcass again.

"I don't know," he said, and took his hand away.

---

A month later, on another freighter, the hull took a hit and the cold got into the hold where forty chipped orcs were being shipped to the Legion yards. Teo found them. They had pressed together against the warmest bulkhead, and some of them had their arms round each other. He came back shaking and couldn't speak.

Jon went and looked. Then he came and sat with Teo for an hour with an arm round his shoulders, and talked to him softly about the first dead he had ever seen, and how it never stopped mattering, and how that was how you knew you were still a person. Teo cried into his coat. When the purser came by, Jon, without taking his arm off Teo, booked their passage to the next system in that same hold.

"At a discount," he said. "They won't be needing it."

The purser wrote it down.

---

On the last night before Earth they sat in the dark of a borrowed lander's cabin, and Eli asked.

"When we get there. What do we do?"

Jon looked up. "We tire him out."

"I didn't come all this way to tire him out. I came to take the chair."

"I know," said Jon. "That's why we tire him out." He held up a hand and counted on his fingers as he went. "Your father has sat in that chair longer than there are records of anyone sitting anywhere. People have tried to kill him. Every kind of people, with every kind of thing." He put the hand down. "So we don't. We go in, and every one of you does the best thing you've ever done, and he has to answer every single one. At the end of it he's tired. Tired men can be held. Tired men can be talked to. Tired men give up chairs."

"In his own house," said the master, from the dark. "In the strongest place on earth."

"It's strong for everyone," said Jon. "It makes everyone bigger. It doesn't make anyone less tired."

"And when he's tired," said Eli, "I talk to him. That part's mine."

Jon looked at him. "Then we'll see," he said.

The master stood up, slowly. He stood in the dark of the cabin with his bag at his feet, and Eli saw that he was going to pick it up and walk off the lander, here, the night before, and that nobody was going to stop him.

"Your master," Jon began, gently.

"Don't," said the master. "You didn't know him."

Jon shut his mouth. After a while he turned his left hand over in his lap and looked at the palm, and went on looking at it.

The master picked up the bag and went to the hatch and stood in it with the cold coming in round him. Nobody spoke. Eli thought of three things to say and said none of them. In the end the master came back and sat down and shoved the bag under his seat, and didn't speak for the rest of the night, and never said why.

Somewhere in the dark Doc laughed softly.

"Sorry," he said. "I was thinking about a goat."

---

## Seven: The Tower

Earth was one city, edge to edge, and it was on fire.

The Tower stood at its heart, pearl-white, so tall its top was lost, and the war broke round its base like surf. Giant ships hung in the upper air. Dragons burned lines through the clouds. The Legion's guns fired from every rooftop, and the sky over the capital was a ceiling of smoke and falling things.

Jon took them in. He stood at the lander's open door with the wind tearing at his coat and stepped out, and the rest of them came with him and fell no faster than he did. They fell through a mile of war. A cathedral ship broke in half directly overhead, its ribs coming apart, and Jon moved one hand and the wreckage went past them on both sides close enough to touch. One spar he didn't move. It came edge-on out of the smoke and opened the Queen's shoulder to the bone and was gone. She didn't make a sound. Jon didn't look at her, and didn't speak the whole way down.

They landed on a terrace two-thirds of the way up the Tower, where a door stood open. Doc bound the Queen's shoulder on the terrace with her standing, and she let him. Then they went in.

One step inside and the war was gone. Eli looked back through the door and could see the sky burning and ships breaking, all of it without a sound.

They were in a pale, seamless corridor that curved gently away. The light came from nowhere. The air didn't stir when you breathed it.

"We're not where we were," said the master.

Eli reached without meaning to, the way you put a hand out in the dark. He reached further than he ever had, with a hold so fine he could feel the threads in Teo's sleeve from across the corridor. He looked at his hands. They were the same hands.

Doc laid his palm flat on the wall, held it there, and took it away.

Jon walked on ahead without waiting for them.

---

The corridor had rooms along it, and each room was a puzzle that didn't look like one until you were in it. A room with a door in every wall, where every door opened back into the same room. A room full of chairs that you couldn't leave until everyone was sitting, and there was always one chair fewer than there were people.

The master solved them. In each room he sat with his eyes shut and felt out the shape of whatever had made it, then stood and said "Here," or "Like this." Eli had never seen him so near to happy.

The third room he couldn't solve by feel.

The ceiling was coming down, an inch a breath, a whole floor's weight of stone, and the way out was a slot at the far end that opened only while something held the ceiling up. The master felt for a trick and found none. There was only weight.

"Well," said the master. "That one's yours."

Eli held it.

He held the ceiling while the others went through the slot one at a time: Teo, the Queen, Doc puffing, the dark elf looking back at him. With the Tower behind him it was possible, just, and he felt it in his teeth and his spine and the backs of his eyes. Jon went through without looking back. Then the master, who stopped in the slot.

"Let it down slow," he said. "Don't drop it. Walk out from under."

Eli did. The ceiling came down behind him and met the floor with a sound like a door shutting in another house.

At the end of the corridor was a last room, round and white and empty, with a door on the far side that wouldn't open. Then it wasn't empty. Something sat in the middle of the floor.

It was hard to look at: a little like a man and a little like a spider and a little like a hole, with too many joints in the wrong order. It sat there as though it always had.

"Visitors," it said, in a dry, creaking voice. "It's been so long." It unfolded a little. "The man upstairs put me here. I came for him once. I built him a room just like this one and put him in it, and he walked in and smiled, and told me he'd had this conversation thousands of times and had got very good at it." It made a sound that might have been laughter. "So now I'm the one in the room. Until a new foe comes in, and loses."

"And if one does?" said the master.

"Then I'm free." Its heads turned toward the rest of them. "Free, in here, with all of you."

"And the door?"

"Opens when the room is done with." It settled. "One goes in. The rest wait. If no one goes in, you stay here forever. Like me."

The master looked at Jon.

Everyone did. Jon was standing a little apart with his hands in his coat pockets, looking at the beast.

"Can you do it?" said the master.

"Yes," said Jon. "And then I'd have nothing left for upstairs."

He kept his eyes on the beast as he said it. The master nodded.

"I'll go," said the master.

"No," said Eli.

"Be quiet." The old man was watching the beast. "Your room. Nothing leaves it?"

"Nothing."

"No light. No heat. Nothing."

"Nothing," said the beast, with enormous contempt. "It isn't even attached to your universe any more. It's mine. And I'm bound to it until someone loses."

"Swear it," said the master. "Do you swear this room will not allow any energy to leave?"

The beast laughed. "I swear it."

"Again."

"I swear it, old man. Nothing gets out. Not a spark. That's the point."

"Once more."

The beast stopped laughing and looked at him.

"I swear it," it said. "Nothing leaves this room."

"Good," said the master, and the lines in his face eased. "Then nothing reaches them. And nothing gets out. Including you."

He turned to Eli.

"You'll never be fine," he said. "But you're better than you were."

He walked into the middle of the round white room, and the beast unfolded around him, and a wall closed between them and the rest.

It was a pane, milky and smooth. Nothing could leave that room, not even light, so what showed on it was a picture the beast painted for them to watch. It showed the beast setting out its game. It showed the master sitting down on the floor cross-legged, as he had sat in the temple every morning, and closing his eyes.

Then the picture began to fail.

It failed from the middle outward, where the master was, and Eli never saw what the master did to himself. He saw the picture fray and pull, as if the hand painting it had started to shake. At the rim the beast was stretching, its limbs reaching back for the walls of its own room. The edges of the picture curled in toward the centre like paper in a flame. The beast's mouths were open.

The pane went black.

No sound came through, and no tremor. They stood in front of it for what might have been a minute.

When it cleared, the round white room was empty and the door on the far side stood open. The floor where the master had sat was smooth and bare.

Somewhere far above them the whole Tower shuddered once, and the light dimmed and came back.

Eli stood in the doorway. He had gone back for the master with a cheap trick, and the master had come.

The Queen stopped beside him on her way to the door.

"That was well done," she said, without looking at him. "Your master."

---

The stair beyond the door wound up through the heart of the Tower. Arches along it opened on half-seen rooms: a hall of ancient grown weapons, pale and ribbed; a hall hung with banners from wars nobody had written down. They didn't stop to look. By the second turn Doc needed the rail.

---

The throne room was enormous and almost empty.

The ceiling was lost in shadow. A great curved window ran the whole height of one wall, and through it the war turned in silence over Earth. At the far end, on a dais, there was a chair, and a man sitting in it.

He was older than Eli had let himself expect: lean, grey at the temples, plainly dressed, with an intelligent, tired, unremarkable face. Eli had never seen his father's face. Nobody had. He knew it anyway.

Beside the chair stood a younger man in black armour with a ribbed sword at his hip, watching them come. He looked scared, and angry.

"Jon," said the man in the chair.

Jon said nothing.

Teo had gone ahead of them all. He always went first through doors; it was how he'd got them both out of the palace. He was halfway across the floor, staring up at the dais, his mouth already open on something he had carried across a whole war.

"You're the Emperor—" Teo began.

"—the Emperor," said Derrek at the same moment, in the same rhythm, word for word, flat, still looking at Jon.

He lifted a hand a little, without looking, and closed it.

Across the room Teo's head snapped back as if a great palm had cupped his face, fingers over his eyes and mouth, and lifted him off the floor. There was one short wet sound. Derrek opened his hand. Teo dropped and didn't move. Like a mean man would put down an unruly dog.

Eli reached for him.

It went out of him like a shout, everything he had and everything the Tower would give. The dark elf's arms locked round him from behind and dragged him back, and Jon's hand came down on his shoulder, lightly, and the reach spilled out of him across the floor between them and the dais. It scored a long gouge in the stone that stopped six feet short of the chair.

Derrek didn't look at the gouge. He looked at Jon.

"You brought children," he said.

---

## Eight: Everyone

The Queen drew her sword.

The sheath slid off it like skin. The blade underneath was pale and alive, a long ribbed thing that flexed in her hand. She looked past the apprentice at the man in the chair, and then, because the apprentice stood between them, at the apprentice.

"Yours," Derrek said to him, without interest. "Go on."

The apprentice came down off the dais.

He was human, young, broad and scarred, built as if he'd been bred for it, and his eyes were red-rimmed. He drew his sword and came across the floor at her like a falling wall.

She met him.

Eli had sparred with her every day for more than a year and thought he knew how good she was. In the Tower, with the dead city at her back, she was better than he had imagined: every block exact, every step measured, her blade going into the gaps in the apprentice's guard and out again. The blades made a soft, wet sound when they met. She cut him four times in the first ten seconds, and Doc's binding on her shoulder went dark. He bled and kept coming. She breathed through her nose, short and even, and gave ground only when she meant to.

The apprentice looked back at the dais.

Derrek was yawning and resting his head on his right hand, looking out of the great window at the burning sky.

The apprentice's face changed.

He reached down. Into the floor, into the walls, into the Tower itself, deeper than anyone had ever told him was safe. The lights along the walls dimmed all at once. The floor shivered. On the dais Derrek's head came up off his hand: whatever the apprentice was pulling out of the walls, he was pulling it out from under his master.

The apprentice began to shine. Light came up through his skin and his blade blazed white. He moved faster than she did, faster than anything Eli had ever seen, and he struck, and she caught it.

She caught every blow. He hit her with the whole dead city behind him, again and again, and every time her blade was there, and every time Eli heard her bones breaking under the stress of blocking: a wrist, a forearm, the long bones of her arms one after another. She didn't cry out. Her arms bent where arms don't bend and she kept blocking with them, giving ground a step at a time, straight back, until her heels touched the foot of the dais.

Then the light around the apprentice turned to fire, and the blows came through.

She took each one standing, and on the last she went down on her knees, and Eli saw that she had let him drive her there. Her ruined arms came up one last time, and she threw.

The living blade left her hands and went up the steps at the man in the chair.

Derrek caught it by the blade. It bit him, twisting in his grip, and opened his palm to the bone before he crushed it. He let the pieces fall. He was breathing through his mouth now.

She was dead before the pieces stopped moving.

The apprentice stood over her, burning, and turned to the dais with his arms spread. His face was wet and hopeful.

"Master," he said. "Are you proud?"

Derrek looked at him.

"Proud?" he said. "Proud of what, exactly? You only killed one, and now you're burning alive." He sighed. "You've always been a disappointment, and I can't believe you've embarrassed me again today."

The apprentice didn't hear it. The fire was inside him now, coming out through his skin and his mouth and the joints of his armour.

Eli wasn't watching him. He was watching the Queen. She lay on her side at the foot of the dais with her arms folded under her where arms don't fold, and her eyes open on the steps. Every day for a year she had put him on the deck and stood over him until he got up. He waited for her to get up.

Behind her the apprentice was screaming. It went on a long time, and changed, and stopped. When Eli looked up there was a shape on the floor beside her, black and smoking and very small.

Derrek looked round the room at each of them in turn, as if waiting for someone to laugh. No one had really heard him. He sighed through his nose, sat back and yawned again, and covered his mouth politely with his bleeding hand. The hand was shaking.

---

Her arms were still round him from behind. He could feel her breathing against his back, slow and even, as she breathed in the hold when she practised.

"Don't," he said.

She found his hand, and the two fingers she had broken on the crossing, and pressed them once. Then she let go of him.

Nobody saw her go; that was the point of her. One moment her arms were round him, and the next the shadows behind the chair moved and she was there, at Derrek's back, her hand already rising, the fingers curling like the statue's.

She closed her hand.

The space around Derrek folded inward, all of it at once: the air, the light, the chair, the dais. The stone of the floor ran toward him like water toward a drain. In the Tower, it had him. His sleeve went first, then the skin of his forearm, drawn toward a point in front of his chest.

Derrek stood up.

He came up out of the chair as it folded behind him, turned, and took hold of what she had made with both hands. It took both. His teeth showed. His bleeding palm left a smear on the air. Then he turned the thing slowly, like a man forcing a rusted wheel, inside out.

Everything that had been falling toward him fell toward her instead.

Her silver eyes found Eli across the room. Then the space around her folded in and took her whole, toward a point just behind her heart. It made a small flat noise, like a book being shut, and where she had been there was nothing.

Nobody moved. A little grit slid across the stone toward the empty place and stopped. Eli could hear Derrek breathing, and his own breath, and nothing else in the whole Tower. He was on his knees, holding the two fingers in his other hand, and didn't remember taking hold of them.

Jon had taken one step forward as the air closed. He was still standing with one foot in front of the other.

Derrek stood in the wreckage of his chair with his right arm hanging. The skin of the forearm was gone, wrung like a cloth.

---

Doc was laughing.

He walked out into the middle of the floor, past the Queen and the black thing that had been the apprentice, laughing a big happy laugh.

"Oh, you're good," he said. "Oh, you're very, very good." He cracked his small pudgy knuckles. "I saw this place come up, you know. The morning the capital burned. A dragon told me it was nice warm sunny weather for a quick ride out of the city, so off I rode, and I looked back from the top of the ridge, and there was the cloud, as far as I could see, and then up out of the cloud, this." He tapped the floor with his foot. "Like a pearl. I've wanted a look inside ever since."

He opened his arms.

Eli saw nothing come out of him. There was no light and no shape. There was weight: a pressure that came off the little man and leaned on Eli's chest from across the room, more and more of it, a crowd shoving out through a door too small for them. The air round Doc wavered like air over a summer road. The floor under him scored in long lines toward the dais, and on the far side of the room Derrek took a step back.

Derrek put up his good hand. Then the ruined one as well.

"Giants and dragons," said Doc, conversationally. "Elves and humans. A lot of humans; you get everywhere." Something struck Derrek and he rocked on his feet. "Dwarves leading orcs." The air round Derrek boiled. His hair blew back off his face though nothing else in the room moved. "And fellow gnomes. Oh, a great many gnomes. Everyone brought me their sick, you see. Every family in the village. And I told every one of them the same thing."

He smiled up at Derrek, very kindly.

"I wasn't able to save your loved one."

Derrek was being driven back across the dais, step by step. Blood came from his nose and then from his ears. His plain dark clothes tore along the seams as if hands were pulling at them. His face ran with sweat, and he held, and the weight kept breaking over him.

Doc was getting smaller. His face was sinking and his skin had gone loose, like fruit left too long in the sun, and still the air round him bent and still Derrek gave ground.

"And last," said Doc, in a voice gone thin and old. "My brother."

The weight went out of the room. Nothing pressed at all, as if something had stopped on its way out and turned back to Doc. Doc looked at the empty air. Then the pressure came once more, all of it, flung, and Derrek, staggering, brushed it aside with the back of his ruined hand.

Doc sat down.

He was very old now, his face folded and folded again, his body shrinking inside his clothes. He looked round the throne room with milky eyes as if he had never seen it before.

"Oh," he said, small and surprised. "It's quiet."

He seemed to listen to it.

"First time in as long as I can remember," he said. "It's peaceful."

His face went slack and easy, like a man falling asleep.

Then he was dust, and the dust lay on the floor in the shape of a small round man sitting down, and then even that slid apart.

Jon was watching the dust. A single tear ran down beside his nose, and he didn't wipe it.

"Jon," said Derrek.

Jon looked up. The tear was still there.

Derrek stood swaying at the edge of the dais, slack-mouthed, bleeding from his nose and ears and his opened palm, one arm hanging at his side, breathing like a man at the top of a long climb.

"It's just us," said Derrek.

---

Eli went first.

He was up off the floor and running the length of the room before he knew he would, and he reached with everything the master had taught him and everything the Tower would give, more than the ceiling, more than the dragon's heart, at the man on the dais. His father.

It hit Derrek in the chest and took him off his feet and back three steps, and the dais cracked end to end under his heels. Derrek's bloody face showed what might have been surprise.

Then he flicked his good hand, and Eli was on the floor fifty feet away with his ribs broken and the reach knocked out of him. He couldn't get up.

Derrek still hadn't looked at him. He was looking at Jon, and Jon was looking back, and neither of them moved.

Eli got an elbow under him.

"You put me on a post," he said. It came out wet. "Recover imperial property. They had my face down to the scar on my chin. You never once asked to see it."

Derrek's eyes stayed on Jon.

"A man died at a fence for your warrant, and it was already on every wall. There was a boy in your yard with a rag round his hand, crying while your guard walked him at me." He spat blood on the floor. "Teo carried something across your whole war to say to you. His mother and father are still in your house. You said it for him."

Nothing.

"I asked them to come. All of them. I sat at their tables and asked." His voice went, and he let it. "Here's your property. It came all this way. Look at it."

Derrek didn't turn his head. His good hand had been coming up, open, toward Jon. It stopped halfway and stayed there.

Jon was already walking past Eli.

They fought. Eli saw three things and nothing in between. He saw the great window star from top to bottom and hold. He saw Jon standing perfectly still in the middle of the floor with his eyes shut while the far wall of the throne room came apart around Derrek and put itself back together. He saw Derrek fall to one knee, get up, and fall again.

At the end of it Derrek was on his knees, and stayed there.

Jon stood over him, breathing hard. Then he did something careful with both hands, and Derrek's arms went behind his back and stayed there, held by nothing Eli could see. His head went back, and he stopped fighting it.

He was alive. Tired men can be held, Jon had said.

Jon hauled Derrek up by the collar as easily as lifting a coat off a hook, walked him to the great window, and sat him on the floor beneath it with his back to the glass.

Then he sat down beside him.

---

## Nine: The Window

Eli dragged himself across the floor to them. Nobody helped him.

The two men sat side by side under the great window. Outside, the sky over Earth was full of fire.

And the ground was opening. Down in the burning city, at the oldest wounds, the places where it had always been wrong, the ground was coming apart, and the split was running out through the streets. Further off there were more. Something was coming up out of them. He couldn't see what. Over the city the ships stopped firing at each other, one by one, and turned toward it.

"Jon," said Derrek.

He hadn't looked at Eli once since they came into the room. Eli lay three feet from his father's knee, and his father looked past him at Jon.

"You remember who our master is," said Derrek.

Jon was looking out of the window.

"You know what you have to do," said Derrek.

Jon sighed and leaned his head back against the glass.

"Just enjoy the show, old man," he said.

"What is that?" said Eli. "Coming out of the ground."

Nobody answered.

"Who is your master?"

Nobody answered.

"What does he have to do?"

Neither of them turned his head.

Eli looked at his father.

"Why did you kill Teo?"

The two men sat with the fire on their faces and watched the opening spread through the city, street by street. After a while, without looking, Derrek moved his bound shoulder a little closer to Jon's. Jon let him.

Below, the ground went on opening, toward the foot of the Tower.

"Why did you kill Teo?" said Eli again.

Neither of them turned.
